    A Building Is Only Finished Once

    The day a building opens is the start of its longest and hardest test: decades of Minnesota weather, wear, and changing owners. This last chapter shows you what makes a building last, how to read the warning signs when it starts to fail, and how to give old buildings a second life. Let's build it right!

    Every earlier chapter described how to make a building. This chapter asks how long it will stay that way. A building is exposed to weather every hour of its life, and unlike a car or a phone, it is rarely replaced when it wears out. It is repaired, renovated, and eventually either reused or taken apart. The skills in this chapter, which are keeping a building performing, diagnosing it when it does not, and deciding what to do with it at the end, connect everything you learned about loads, moisture, materials, codes, energy, and sustainability.

    We return a last time to the Riverbend Youth Center from Chapter 2, the invented one-story, wood-framed Minneapolis building of about 9,000 ft². All dollar figures and conditions for Riverbend in this chapter are illustrative. The failures we discuss are common ones, chosen because they follow predictable patterns that you can learn to recognize and prevent.

    Durability¶

    Durability is the ability of a building, assembly, or material to keep performing its required function in its service environment, over its intended life, with only the maintenance planned for it. The definition has three parts that matter. A material is not durable in general. It is durable for a particular environment, so brick that lasts centuries in a dry climate may crumble within decades in a place with many freeze-thaw cycles. Durability is also not strength: a strong material can still decay if it stays wet. And durability has a cost dimension, since a material that needs frequent expensive repair is not durable in any useful sense.

    Buildings are attacked by a limited set of agents of degradation, which are forces and conditions that wear materials down. Water is the most important, as it dissolves, swells, freezes, corrodes, and feeds decay. The others are temperature swings, which cause expansion and contraction, sunlight, which breaks down plastics and finishes, chemicals such as deicing salts, living organisms such as fungi and insects, mechanical wear, and fire. Minnesota concentrates several of these at once: temperatures can range from below −20 °F to above 90 °F in a year, snow and ice load roofs and form ice dams, and salt reaches walks and slabs.

    Worked example: protecting the Riverbend wall base. The base of an exterior wall faces splashing rain, snowmelt, ground moisture, and winter salt. The design team lists the exposures and a defense for each.

    Exposure at the wall base Defense Remaining risk to monitor
    Rain splashing off the ground Wall base raised well above grade, with a gravel splash zone Mulch or snow piled against the wall
    Water flowing down the wall Drainage layer and flashing that sheds water outward Flashing laps and corners
    Moisture rising from soil or concrete Capillary break between the foundation and wood A sill plate placed on bare concrete
    Snowmelt and deicing salt Grading and drains that carry water away Salt spray on the lowest masonry
    Wood near wet conditions Preservative-treated sill plate Cut ends left untreated

    The pattern is general: identify each agent that reaches the assembly, give each a defense, and note the point where the defense is most likely to fail. This is durability design, and it uses the control layers of Chapter 11 and the properties of Chapter 5.

    Water Is the Master Variable

    Think of water as the one variable that turns on almost every kind of decay, from rot to rust to frost damage. If you can keep an assembly dry, or let it dry quickly after it gets wet, you have solved most of its durability problem.

    Service Life¶

    Service life is the period during which a building or component performs at or above its required level, under the conditions in which it is actually used. It is different from design life, which is the period the designer intended, and from physical life, which is how long the object exists. A building can end its service life long before it falls apart, because of obsolescence: a functional one when the building no longer suits its use, an economic one when it costs more to keep than to replace, or a technical one when systems become outdated.

    Different parts of a building have very different service lives, which is why building designers think in layers. The following ranges are rough, commonly cited orders of magnitude, and they vary widely with product, climate, and care.

    Building layer Examples Rough service life
    Structure and foundation Footings, frame Fifty to over a hundred years
    Enclosure Walls, windows, roof membrane Twenty to fifty years
    Mechanical and electrical HVAC equipment, panels Fifteen to thirty years
    Interior finishes Paint, flooring, ceilings Five to twenty years

    Engineers estimate the service life of a component with the factor method from the international standard ISO 15686. It begins with a reference service life under standard conditions and multiplies it by factors for the quality of the components, the design level, the quality of the work, the indoor environment, the outdoor environment, the in-use conditions, and the level of maintenance. A factor of 1.0 means standard conditions, a value below 1.0 shortens the life, and a value above 1.0 lengthens it.

    Worked example: a Riverbend roof membrane. The reference service life of the membrane is 25 years. The team assigns illustrative factors: quality of components 1.0, design level 0.9 because of a detail with limited drainage, quality of work 0.9 because the roof is installed in cool weather, indoor environment 1.0, outdoor environment 0.8 for severe freeze-thaw, snow, and sunlight, in-use conditions 1.0, and maintenance 1.1 because the owner will inspect twice a year. The estimated service life is

    \[ 25 \times 1.0 \times 0.9 \times 0.9 \times 1.0 \times 0.8 \times 1.0 \times 1.1 \approx 17.8 \text{ years} \]

    The method does not predict a date. It shows which decisions move the number: improving the drainage detail and the installation quality would raise the estimate, and skipping maintenance would lower it. The result also lets the owner schedule a replacement fund.

    Building Maintenance¶

    Building maintenance is the work of keeping a building and its systems in a condition that allows them to perform their required functions through their service life. It includes routine tasks such as cleaning, repairs after something breaks, scheduled servicing, and planned replacement of worn components. Maintenance is the main way to reach, or exceed, the service life a designer assumed, and neglect is a leading reason that buildings fail early.

    Maintenance falls into three styles, and the cost and risk of each differ.

    • Reactive maintenance: fixing something after it fails. It costs nothing until the failure, but failures occur at the worst times and can damage other parts.
    • Preventive maintenance: servicing on a schedule to avoid failure, discussed next.
    • Predictive maintenance: servicing when measurements show that a failure is approaching, such as vibration monitoring of a motor.

    A well-run building uses all three, and the owner budgets for them at the outset. The owner's manuals and warranties delivered at closeout in Chapter 2 are the starting point for the maintenance plan.

    Preventive Maintenance¶

    Preventive maintenance is scheduled servicing and inspection performed at set intervals to keep components working and to find small problems before they become large ones. It rests on a simple economic observation: a small, planned task, such as cleaning a gutter, costs far less than the water damage that results if it is neglected. The schedule typically follows the manufacturer's recommendations, the building's exposure, and the history of the building.

    For a building like Riverbend, a short preventive-maintenance schedule might include the tasks in the table.

    Task Typical frequency Failure it prevents
    Inspect roof, flashings, and drains Twice a year and after severe storms Leaks, ponding, ice dams
    Clean gutters and clear drains Spring and fall Overflow at the wall base
    Check sealants and caulk joints Yearly Water entry at windows and joints
    Replace HVAC filters Per manufacturer, often quarterly Poor airflow, equipment wear
    Test emergency lights and the fire alarm Per code and manufacturer Failure during an emergency

    Life-Cycle Cost¶

    Life-cycle cost (LCC) is the total cost of owning a building or component over a stated study period, including the initial cost, energy, maintenance, repair, replacement, and end-of-life costs. It differs from the first cost, the price paid at construction, which is only a part of the total. Because money spent in the future is worth less than money spent today, LCC converts future costs to their present value using a discount rate \( r \):

    \[ \text{PV} = \frac{\text{cost}}{(1 + r)^n} \]

    where \( n \) is the number of years until the cost occurs.

    Worked example: two roofs over 60 years. Option 1 costs $8 per ft² and lasts 20 years, so it is installed in years 0, 20, and 40. Option 2 costs $10 per ft² and lasts 30 years, so it is installed in years 0 and 30. The costs are illustrative. Undiscounted, option 1 totals \( 3 \times 8 = \$24 \) per ft² and option 2 totals \( 2 \times 10 = \$20 \). At a 3 percent discount rate, the present values are

    \[ 8 + \frac{8}{1.03^{20}} + \frac{8}{1.03^{40}} \approx \$14.88 \qquad 10 + \frac{10}{1.03^{30}} \approx \$14.12 \]

    per ft², so option 2 is cheaper over the period by about $0.76 per ft², or roughly $6,900 over the 9,000 ft² roof. The cheaper roof to buy is not the cheaper roof to own, although the margin shrinks as the discount rate rises.

    Building Failure¶

    Building failure is the condition in which a building or one of its parts no longer meets its required performance. The word covers a wide range. At one end is collapse, which is rare and catastrophic. At the other are far more common serviceability failures, in which the building stands but does not work: a roof leaks, a floor cracks, a window will not close, or a room molds. A failure can be structural, enclosure-related, mechanical, or aesthetic, and the cost and danger vary accordingly.

    Failures are better understood by their causes than by their symptoms. A failure rarely has a single cause. It usually results from a chain of conditions, each of which the project might have broken. The common categories of cause are shown below.

    Cause category Description Riverbend example
    Design Error or omission in drawings, details, or calculations Window head with no flashing detail
    Materials Defective or unsuitable product Wood framing delivered wet
    Construction Work not built as designed Flashing installed backward
    Maintenance Required upkeep skipped Clogged roof drains
    Environment Loads beyond the design assumptions Record snow load
    Use Changes that the building was not designed for Heavy storage added to a classroom floor

    Worked example: how a missing flashing becomes a rotted wall. Consider a window in the Riverbend classroom wing. The design details omit the flashing above the window head, and the installers, who work from the drawings, install none. In the first winter, wind-driven rain and meltwater reach the top of the window and run into the wall cavity behind the siding. The wet sheathing and studs stay above about 20 percent moisture content for weeks at a time, because the wall cannot dry toward either side. Within a few years, decay fungi take hold, the framing softens, and paint peels at the corner. An interior stain appears on the ceiling about five years in. The visible symptom is a stain, but the failure began on a drawing. Breaking the chain at any link, such as detailing the flashing, checking it at inspection, or catching the first stain, would have prevented the loss.

    Most of the chains in building failure involve water, and most could have been broken early. That is why the following sections treat the common mechanisms one at a time.

    Water Intrusion¶

    Water intrusion is the unwanted entry of liquid water into a building or its enclosure, and it is the most common cause of enclosure failures. Water arrives from several sources: rain driven through gaps and poorly flashed openings, roof leaks, meltwater behind ice dams (Chapter 13), groundwater pressing on foundations (Chapter 9), plumbing leaks, and moisture drawn upward through concrete and masonry by capillary action. It then moves by gravity, wind pressure, capillary suction, and air movement.

    Designers control it with four related strategies, sometimes called the four Ds:

    • Deflect water with overhangs, flashings, and slopes.
    • Drain what gets behind the cladding through a drainage plane.
    • Dry the assembly by allowing vapor to escape.
    • Use durable materials where wetting is unavoidable.

    These strategies extend the enclosure principles of Chapter 11 and the air sealing of Chapter 12. Finding the point of entry in an existing building can be difficult, since water travels along framing and appears far from where it entered.

    Freeze-Thaw Damage¶

    Freeze-thaw damage is the cracking, flaking, and crumbling of porous materials such as concrete, brick, and stone caused by water freezing inside them. Water expands by about 9 percent when it freezes, and if a pore is nearly full of water, the pressure can exceed the material's tensile strength. Each freeze weakens the material a little, and Minnesota's many freeze-thaw cycles repeat the damage year after year. It appears as spalling, in which chips or layers break off a surface, and as scaling of concrete surfaces, which deicing salts make worse.

    The defenses reduce the water that can enter and give it room to expand. Air-entrained concrete, which contains microscopic bubbles that act as relief space for freezing water, is the standard defense for exterior concrete in cold climates (see Chapter 8), and a low water-to-cement ratio makes concrete less permeable. Brick in northern climates is selected for its freeze-thaw resistance, and designers keep water out of masonry with flashings, copings, and drainage. Avoid using deicing salts on young concrete.

    Rot and Decay¶

    Rot and decay are the biological breakdown of organic materials, most commonly wood, by fungi. Decay fungi need four things at once:

    • A food source, which is the wood itself.
    • Moisture, meaning wood that stays above about 20 percent moisture content for long periods.
    • Oxygen, which is present nearly everywhere except under water.
    • A moderate temperature, roughly between cool and warm indoor conditions.

    Remove any one and the fungus cannot grow, which is why dry wood lasts for centuries. Fungi turn the wood into a crumbly, weak mass, and wood-boring insects such as carpenter ants and termites also damage wet wood.

    The remedies follow from the four needs. Keep wood dry through the detailing described above, separate it from soil and concrete, use preservative-treated or naturally durable wood where wetting is expected, and provide the ventilation or drying path that lets an occasional wetting dry out. The steel counterpart is corrosion, the electrochemical reaction that turns steel to rust when it is exposed to water and oxygen, and it is controlled by coatings, drainage, and concrete cover over reinforcing bars.

    Mold¶

    Mold is a type of fungus that grows on damp organic surfaces and produces visible fuzzy or stained patches, odors, and airborne spores. It needs moisture, a food source such as the paper facing on gypsum board, dust, or wood, and a moderate temperature, and it can start growing within a couple of days of a material becoming wet. Condensation on a cold surface in winter, a leak, or a flood can all supply the moisture. Mold can cause allergy symptoms, irritation, and respiratory problems in some people, so occupants' health is a concern.

    The remedy is always to correct the moisture source first, then dry the area and remove the affected materials, or clean those that can be cleaned. Wiping a stain without fixing the leak only hides the problem.

    Watch Out: Treating the Stain, Not the Source

    A common trap is painting over a ceiling stain or bleaching mold and calling the job done. The moisture is still arriving, so the damage keeps growing out of sight. Find and stop the water first, confirm the area is dry with a moisture meter, and only then repair the finish.

    Efflorescence¶

    Efflorescence is a white, powdery deposit of salts that appears on the surface of masonry or concrete. It forms when water moves through the material, dissolves soluble salts, and carries them to the surface, where the water evaporates and leaves the salts behind. It is not mold, although the two are sometimes confused, and it does not damage the material by itself. It is a signal that water is passing through the wall, so the cause deserves investigation.

    Brushing or washing removes the deposit, but it returns if the water source remains. A persistent pattern, such as a band along the base of a brick wall, usually points to a leak, a missing flashing, a drainage problem, or moisture rising from the soil.

    Cracking¶

    Cracking is the formation of separations in a material or assembly, and its width, direction, and pattern point to its cause. Shrinkage cracks appear in concrete as it dries and are typically fine and random. Thermal cracks result from expansion and contraction with temperature. Structural cracks result from loads exceeding the capacity of a member, and settlement cracks result from foundation movement. Cracks are not all dangerous, since many are cosmetic, but a wide crack, a growing crack, or a crack with offset between its sides deserves expert review.

    Thermal movement can be calculated from the length change \( \Delta L = \alpha L \Delta T \), where \( \alpha \) is the coefficient of thermal expansion, \( L \) is the length, and \( \Delta T \) is the temperature change. For a 100 ft (1,200 in) concrete wall with \( \alpha = 5.5 \times 10^{-6} \) per °F and a seasonal swing of 100 °F:

    \[ \Delta L = 5.5 \times 10^{-6} \times 1{,}200 \times 100 = 0.66 \text{ in} \]

    That is a large movement, and it explains why designers place control joints and expansion joints, which give a material a planned place to move, as Chapter 8 described for concrete and masonry. A wall without joints will find its own place to crack.

    Settlement¶

    Settlement is the downward movement of a building's foundation as the soil beneath it compresses. Uniform settlement, in which the whole building sinks evenly, is usually harmless. Differential settlement, in which one part sinks more than another, is the dangerous kind, because it distorts the frame and causes cracks, sticking doors, and sloping floors. Causes include soft clay that slowly consolidates, poorly compacted fill, erosion or leaking pipes that wash out soil, and groundwater changes, topics of Chapter 9.

    A common rule of thumb limits angular distortion, which is the differential settlement divided by the distance between the two points, to about 1/500 to avoid cracking in walls. If two Riverbend columns 40 ft (480 in) apart settle differently by 1 in, the angular distortion is \( 1/480 \approx 0.0021 \), which is just beyond 1/500 (0.0020) and near the point where cracking begins. This is a rough guide and not a code limit, and the foundation engineer designs to the actual criteria.

    Fire Damage¶

    Fire damage is the harm done to a building by heat, flame, smoke, and the water used to extinguish a fire. Heat weakens structural materials in different ways. Steel loses roughly half its strength at temperatures near 1,100 °F, concrete loses strength and may spall at high temperatures, and wood chars on the surface while the core remains sound, a behavior that Chapter 18 used to calculate fire resistance. Smoke and soot spread far beyond the burned area, and firefighting water often causes more widespread damage than the flames.

    After a fire, a structural engineer assesses every damaged member before the building is reoccupied, since a member can look sound while having lost strength. The repair plan addresses structure, smoke and odor removal, and drying of water-soaked materials, and it triggers permits and inspections under the codes of Chapter 17.

    The table below summarizes the mechanisms from this part of the chapter as a diagnostic aid, pairing each visible symptom with its likely mechanism and the first thing to check.

    Symptom Likely mechanism First check
    Brown stain on ceiling or wall Water intrusion Window heads, roof drains, flashing
    Soft, crumbly wood Rot and decay Moisture content and source of wetting
    Fuzzy or discolored patches on a surface Mold Leaks and winter condensation
    White powder on brick or concrete Efflorescence Water path through the wall
    Flaking or chipped masonry or concrete surface Freeze-thaw damage Drainage and exposure to salt
    Hairline random cracks in a slab Shrinkage cracking Joint spacing, curing
    Diagonal cracks from corners of openings, sticking doors Differential settlement Soil, drainage, foundation level survey
    Discolored, blistered, charred or sooty surfaces after a fire Fire damage Structural engineer assessment

    Building Assessment¶

    Building assessment is the systematic evaluation of a building's present condition to determine how it is performing, what is deficient, how much life remains, and what actions are needed. Owners commission assessments before buying a property, before a renovation, for maintenance budgeting, and when something seems to be going wrong. Types include condition assessments, structural and envelope assessments, energy audits, and accessibility reviews.

    A thorough assessment follows a consistent sequence. It begins with a review of drawings, past repairs, and maintenance records, followed by interviews with occupants and maintenance staff. A visual walk-through documents conditions, which is often supported by non-destructive tools, such as moisture meters, infrared cameras that show temperature differences in a wall, and ground-penetrating radar that locates reinforcement in concrete. Where needed, destructive investigation, such as cutting a small opening or taking a concrete core, confirms hidden conditions. The assessor then analyzes the findings and prioritizes the actions by safety, cost, and urgency.

    Assessors often summarize the condition with the facility condition index (FCI), the ratio of the cost of deferred maintenance and needed repairs to the current replacement value of the building:

    \[ \text{FCI} = \frac{\text{cost of needed repairs}}{\text{current replacement value}} \]

    Worked example: Riverbend at 25 years. Suppose a condition assessment finds $310,000 of repairs and replacements needed, including roof, windows, and mechanical equipment, and a replacement value of $6,200,000 (both illustrative). The index is \( 310{,}000/6{,}200{,}000 = 0.05 \), or 5 percent. Owners commonly describe an FCI of about 5 percent or less as good, 5 to 10 percent as fair, and above that as poor, although the bands differ among organizations. At the boundary of good and fair, Riverbend is healthy, but the owner should plan to fund the roof and mechanical replacements before they fail.

    Beau's Tip: Start With a Walk-Through and a Camera

    Before any instrument comes out, walk the whole building with a camera, a notebook, and a moisture meter, and photograph every stain and crack with a tape measure or coin for scale. The log you keep becomes the baseline that shows whether a problem is growing.

    Building Forensics¶

    Building forensics is the investigation of a failure to determine what happened, why it happened, and who or what was responsible. It differs from a building assessment, which describes the condition of a building. Forensics asks about cause, and its findings may be used to repair the building correctly, to prevent the same failure elsewhere, or to resolve a legal dispute. Forensic engineers and architects use the same scientific method as researchers.

    The method proceeds in steps. The investigator first defines the problem and protects the evidence, so that no one repairs or discards the failed part before it is documented. Next comes collection of facts: drawings, specifications, submittals, inspection records, weather data, interviews, and photographs. Then the investigator proposes several competing hypotheses for the cause, and tests each with observation, sampling, laboratory work, analysis, or a water test. A hypothesis that survives testing, and explains all of the evidence, becomes the conclusion, which is written in a report.

    Worked example: tracing the classroom stain. Return to the Riverbend stain at the classroom ceiling. The investigator reads the drawings and finds no flashing detail at the window head. A moisture meter scan shows a wet band that runs upward from the stain to the window. A water test, in which a hose is applied to the wall in stages from the bottom up, produces a leak only when the water hits the head. A small opening in the siding reveals darkened, soft sheathing and no flashing. Each step eliminates a competing hypothesis, such as a roof leak or condensation, and supports the conclusion that the missing flashing was the cause.

    Forensic history teaches design lessons as well as repair. In the Hyatt Regency walkway collapse in Kansas City in 1981, a change in how hanger rods were attached to the walkway beams, made during construction and not checked in review, roughly doubled the load on one connection. The case remains a standard example of why changes need engineering review, which connects to the submittals and change-order process of Chapter 2.

    Diagnosing Causes Is Hard

    Going from a symptom back to a cause is one of the hardest skills in the field, and experienced investigators are often wrong on their first guess. You already know how water, heat, loads, and materials behave, so write down every plausible cause, list what evidence would separate them, and test them one by one.

    Renovation and Retrofit¶

    Renovation is the work of altering, repairing, or upgrading an existing building to extend its life or improve its performance, and a retrofit is the addition of new components or systems to an existing building to improve a specific performance, such as energy use, safety, or accessibility. A renovation might replace a worn roof and refinish interiors, while an energy retrofit adds attic insulation, air sealing, new windows, and a heat pump. Chapter 19's strategies are often applied as retrofits, since a large share of the buildings that will be in use for decades already stand.

    Existing buildings bring constraints that new ones do not. The condition of hidden structure and materials is uncertain, which makes the assessment step above essential. Older buildings may contain hazardous materials such as asbestos and lead paint, which require licensed handling. The building code treats existing buildings through provisions for alterations, with requirements that scale with the extent of the work, so the team confirms with the building official, as Chapter 17 taught, which provisions apply.

    Adaptive Reuse¶

    Adaptive reuse is the conversion of an existing building to a use other than the one for which it was built, such as turning a warehouse into apartments or a factory into offices. Minneapolis has many examples, including former flour mills and warehouses that now serve as housing, offices, and museums. Reuse saves the embodied carbon of the existing structure (Chapter 20), preserves a community's character, and may qualify for historic preservation incentives.

    The challenge is that a new use means new code requirements. A change from warehouse to residential changes the occupancy classification and so triggers the requirements of Chapter 18: fire separations, egress, and sprinklers. The floors may not carry the new loads, the windows and walls may not meet the energy code, and the building may need accessibility upgrades. A feasibility study tests the structure, the code path, and the cost before the owner commits.

    Deconstruction¶

    Deconstruction is the careful, selective dismantling of a building, in roughly the reverse order of its construction, to recover materials for reuse and recycling instead of demolishing it and sending the debris to a landfill. It puts the salvage strategies of Chapter 20 into practice at the end of a building's life. Crews remove fixtures, doors, windows, flooring, and structural timber, and sort the materials for resale, donation, or recycling.

    Deconstruction takes longer and more labor than demolition, so it competes on avoided disposal costs, the resale value of the materials, and sometimes tax benefits for donations. It also needs a survey for hazardous materials before work begins. The approach works best when a building was designed for it, with mechanical fasteners and accessible connections, so designers who think about reuse at the start make deconstruction easier at the end.

    Building Resilience¶

    Building resilience is the ability of a building to withstand, adapt to, and recover quickly from disruptive events, such as severe storms, flooding, extreme heat or cold, and loss of power or water. It treats rare but severe events as design inputs, in the same way the structural chapters treated snow and wind loads. A related idea is passive survivability: how long a building stays livable without power or fuel. In a Minnesota January, a well-insulated and airtight building loses heat slowly and gives occupants more time before pipes freeze.

    Resilient design adds margin and recovery paths. Examples include placing equipment above flood levels, specifying durable enclosures, providing emergency power for critical functions, as Chapter 18 described, using shading and insulation to limit temperature swings, and arranging systems so that the failure of one does not disable the others. Resilience connects durability, energy efficiency, and life safety, because the buildings that cope best with disruption are those that were built well in all three.

    You Finished the Whole Journey

    You can now diagnose a leak from stain to flashing, estimate a component's service life and life-cycle cost, assess a building with a condition index, and plan its reuse. Across this book, you also learned to trace a load path, read a wall section, apply the code, estimate heat loss, and weigh materials with life-cycle data. That makes you a builder who can justify a decision with evidence, so go build it right!

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Durability is performance over time in a specific environment, and it is designed by identifying each agent of degradation, giving each a defense, and noting where the defense is most likely to fail.
    • Service life depends on the component, the environment, the quality of the work, and the maintenance, and the factor method shows which of these decisions most affects the estimate.
    • Preventive maintenance costs far less than the damage it prevents, and life-cycle cost, discounted to present value, shows that the cheapest first cost is often not the cheapest to own.
    • Failures rarely have a single cause. They are chains of design, material, construction, maintenance, environment, and use conditions, and water is involved in most enclosure failures.
    • Water intrusion, freeze-thaw damage, rot, mold, and efflorescence all follow from moisture, while cracking, settlement, and fire damage come from movement, loads, and heat.
    • Assessment describes the condition of a building, and forensics finds the cause of a failure by testing competing hypotheses against evidence.
    • Renovation, retrofit, adaptive reuse, and deconstruction extend a building's value and keep embodied carbon and materials in use.
    • Resilient buildings combine durability, efficiency, and life-safety systems so that they can withstand and recover from disruption, which is the sum of what this book has taught: loads, heat, moisture, materials, systems, codes, and sustainability working together in one well-built building.
