    The Rulebook Every Building Plays By

    Every wall, stair, outlet, and exit sign you will ever specify has to pass through a set of rules written by people who studied what went wrong in past buildings. Learn how those rules are organized and who enforces them, and you will stop fearing the permit counter and start planning for it. Let's build it right!

    The earlier chapters explained how buildings carry loads, manage heat and moisture, and distribute power. This chapter explains the legal framework that decides whether a particular building may be built and occupied. A building can be well designed and still be refused a permit, or can be built carefully and still be refused permission to open. Codes, permits, and inspections are how a community checks that its buildings meet a shared minimum before people use them.

    We return to the Riverbend Youth Center from Chapter 2, the invented one-story, wood-framed community building in Minneapolis with a 40 ft multipurpose room, three classrooms, a kitchen, and offices. Every number attached to Riverbend here is illustrative. Code values change with each edition and each jurisdiction, so where this chapter mentions a specific value it is hedged, and your job in practice is always to read the edition in force for your project.

    Building Codes¶

    Building codes are legally enforceable sets of minimum requirements for the design, construction, alteration, and use of buildings, adopted by a government to protect public health, safety, and general welfare. The authority comes from the police power, the long-standing ability of a state to regulate private activity when public safety is at stake. A code is therefore not advice or a best-practice guide. It is law, and a building that violates it can be denied a permit, stopped during construction, or refused an occupancy approval.

    Codes grew out of disasters. Many provisions that now look routine, such as limits on how close buildings may stand, requirements for more than one exit, and sprinklers in certain buildings, were added after fires, collapses, and building-related illnesses showed what unregulated construction costs. Reading a code with this history in mind makes it easier to remember, because each requirement answers a specific failure. The code is also a minimum. It sets the least performance the community will accept, and it says nothing about whether a building is comfortable, efficient, or beautiful.

    How a Code Is Organized¶

    Building codes are organized so that a designer can start from the building's use and follow a chain of questions to the requirements that apply. Most codes begin with administrative provisions that say who enforces the code, when a permit is needed, and how appeals work. A definitions section follows. The technical provisions then address use and occupancy, fire protection, means of egress, accessibility, the building envelope, structural design, and the materials chapters for wood, steel, concrete, and masonry. The last part lists the referenced standards, covered later in this chapter.

    Requirements come in two styles. A prescriptive requirement states exactly what to do, such as a minimum thickness or a required component, and it is easy to check. A performance requirement states the result that must be achieved, such as resisting fire for a stated time, and leaves the means to the designer, who must then show by test or analysis that the result is met. Most codes mix both. Prescriptive paths are faster for ordinary buildings, and performance paths give room for unusual designs.

    Worked Example: Reading the Code as a Procedure¶

    Code use is a procedure: classify the building, find the controlling provision, calculate, and then check. Consider how the design team applies it to the Riverbend multipurpose room, which measures 2,400 ft² of usable floor area. The team first decides what the room is used for, which determines its occupancy classification, a concept Chapter 18 develops. A room with tables and chairs for meals and gatherings is an assembly use. The code then assigns a floor area per occupant, a number of square feet allowed for each person, and the occupant load (the number of people the space must be designed to hold) follows by division:

    \[ \text{occupant load} = \frac{\text{floor area}}{\text{floor area per occupant}} \]

    For assembly space with tables and chairs, the code commonly uses about 15 net ft² per person, which gives \( 2{,}400 / 15 = 160 \) people. If the room will often be set up with chairs in tight rows, the code treats it as concentrated use, commonly about 7 net ft² per person, and the load becomes \( 2{,}400 / 7 \approx 343 \), or roughly 340 people. The designer then looks up how many exits a space of that load needs. Codes commonly require at least two exits once the load passes a small threshold, and more exits as the load rises. In practice the team verifies every factor against the adopted edition, since the values here are illustrative.

    The point is not the answer but the chain: use, factor, load, exits. Each later step uses the result of the one before, which is why classifying the occupancy correctly at the start matters so much.

    The Code Is a Floor, Not a Ceiling

    Think of the code as the floor of a room: you may not go below it, but nothing stops you from building higher. A design that meets the code only just barely may still leave the owner with a building that is drafty, noisy, or expensive to run.

    Three terms need a short definition before we look at the diagram below. A model code is a code written by a private standards organization instead of a legislature, so it has no legal force until a government adopts it. To adopt a code means that a legislature or agency enacts it by law, usually with changes called amendments that suit local conditions. The authority having jurisdiction is the government office that enforces the code for a particular project. Later sections treat each in turn.

    Beau's Tip: Find the Edition Before You Calculate

    Before you pull a single number from any code, write down the edition and the Minnesota amendments in force on your permit date at the top of your calculation sheet. A number from the wrong edition is the quietest way to fail a plan review.

    Model Codes¶

    Model codes are comprehensive codes developed by private organizations through consensus processes so that governments do not each have to write their own. Committees of building officials, engineers, architects, manufacturers, and fire service members propose and debate changes, and the organization publishes a new edition on a regular cycle, typically every three years. Because the document is written for national use, it is general and does not reflect any one state's climate or law.

    The advantage is consistency. A contractor who knows the model code in one state can use it in the next, manufacturers can test and label products to one set of requirements, and a state can adopt a document that has already been through years of review. The cost is a lag. A state takes time to review each new edition, hold hearings, add amendments, and write the rules, so the edition in force for a project is often one or two cycles behind the newest published edition.

    Worked example: which edition governs Riverbend? Suppose, illustratively, that a model code organization publishes a new edition in 2024, while Minnesota's adopted rules still reference the edition published in 2021. The Riverbend team applies for its permit in 2025. The permit is reviewed against the edition in force in Minnesota on the application date, so the 2021 edition, plus Minnesota's amendments, governs. A designer who used the new 2024 edition for the stair dimensions would find that the reviewer measures the design against different numbers. Amendments illustrate why a model code is never used alone. Minnesota, for example, commonly amends model provisions to reflect its cold climate, such as the depth of footings needed to get below frost, a topic covered in Chapter 10. The only safe practice is to confirm the adopted edition and the amendments with the building official at the start of design.

    International Building Code¶

    The International Building Code (IBC) is the model code for most buildings other than one- and two-family houses and townhouses. The International Code Council (ICC) publishes it and it is the basis for the commercial building code in Minnesota and in most other states. It covers structure, fire protection, egress, accessibility, the envelope, and the materials used to build, so for a building like Riverbend it is the central document, with several others working alongside it.

    The IBC follows the organization described earlier, and the sequence of its technical chapters matches the order in which a designer works. Chapter numbers shift slightly between editions, so this list names groups of provisions rather than numbers.

    1. Scope, administration, and definitions.
    2. Use and occupancy classification, which sorts buildings by what people do in them.
    3. Height, area, and construction types, which limit how big a building may be and what it is made of.
    4. Fire and smoke protection, including fire-resistance ratings and fire protection systems.
    5. Means of egress, the path people follow to leave a building, and accessibility.
    6. Interior environment and energy provisions, including light, ventilation, and references to the energy code.
    7. Exterior walls and roofs.
    8. Structural design, soils, foundations, and the material chapters for concrete, masonry, steel, and wood.
    9. Special inspections and tests, and a closing list of referenced standards.

    Worked example: routing five Riverbend questions. The first column below lists questions the team asks at different points in design. The second names the group of IBC provisions where the answer starts.

    Riverbend question Where the answer starts
    Is the multipurpose room an assembly use? Use and occupancy classification
    How large may the wood-framed building be without sprinklers? Height, area, and construction types
    How long must the glued-laminated beams resist fire, if at all? Fire-resistance provisions, tied to construction type
    How many exits does the classroom wing need? Means of egress
    What snow load must the roof carry? Structural design, which refers to a loads standard

    The Code Book Looks Bigger Than It Is

    A model code can run to hundreds of pages, and the many exceptions make everyone feel lost at first. You already know how to follow a chain of dependent steps from Chapter 2, so start at occupancy classification and follow the cross-references one at a time, writing each answer down as you go.

    Minnesota State Building Code¶

    The Minnesota State Building Code is the set of building regulations that Minnesota adopts into state rules and applies to construction across the state. The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry administers the adoption process, and it generally adopts a model code such as the IBC with Minnesota amendments. The state publishes the result as a set of codes by subject, for example the commercial building code, the residential code, the energy codes, and the accessibility rules.

    The code applies statewide, and most cities and many counties administer it through their own building officials, who issue permits and perform inspections. Local government may add some rules of its own, such as zoning, but generally cannot weaken the state code. Because of this layered structure, the first practical question on a Minneapolis project is who the authority having jurisdiction is and which edition they enforce. The answer is on the city's permitting website or from a call to the building department.

    The Family of Technical Codes¶

    A building is regulated by several subject-specific codes in addition to the building code, because each building system has its own specialists and hazards. The sections below define each, and the table that follows summarizes who publishes and who typically enforces each one.

    National Electrical Code¶

    The National Electrical Code (NEC) is the standard for the safe installation of electrical wiring and equipment, published as NFPA 70 by the National Fire Protection Association and revised on a three-year cycle. It governs conductors, overcurrent protection, grounding, and the equipment introduced in Chapters 15 and 16. It is an installation code and not a design manual, so it sets safety limits and leaves the choice of design to the engineer. States adopt it into law. In Minnesota, the state's electrical program, rather than the city building inspector, typically handles electrical inspection, which is one reason an electrical designer pulls a separate electrical permit.

    Residential Code¶

    The residential code, in model form the International Residential Code (IRC), covers one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses up to a stated height. It is a single prescriptive document that includes structural, mechanical, plumbing, and energy provisions, so a builder can find nearly everything in one place. Buildings outside its scope, such as apartment buildings and commercial buildings like Riverbend, fall under the IBC.

    Fire Code¶

    The fire code governs the use and maintenance of buildings after they are built, whereas the building code governs how they are built. It covers matters such as keeping exits unobstructed, storing hazardous materials, testing sprinklers and alarms, and limiting crowds in assembly spaces. Fire marshals and fire departments typically enforce it, and Chapter 18 treats fire protection and life-safety requirements in detail.

    Plumbing Code and Mechanical Code¶

    The plumbing code regulates water supply, drainage, venting, fixtures, and backflow prevention in the systems described in Chapter 14. The mechanical code regulates heating, ventilation, air conditioning, exhaust, fuel-burning equipment, and ductwork. Each sets installation requirements that protect health, such as safe drinking water and safe combustion venting, and each is typically enforced by its own inspectors. States vary in which model plumbing code they build on, so confirm the Minnesota version for the project.

    Accessibility Standards¶

    Accessibility standards require that buildings be usable by people with disabilities, including people who use wheelchairs, have limited vision, or have limited hearing. They cover accessible routes, ramps, door clearances, restrooms, parking, and signage. Two sources apply. The building code references a technical standard on accessible design, and a federal civil rights law, the Americans with Disabilities Act, separately requires accessibility for public accommodations and public buildings. A design may pass the building code and still need a check against the federal requirements, so the designer reviews both.

    Referenced Standards¶

    Referenced standards are documents written by standards organizations that a code names and thereby makes enforceable. A building code cannot repeat every detail of every test method and design procedure, so it cites the document instead. The cited edition becomes part of the code to the extent the code references it, which is why the code carries a list of referenced standards.

    Worked example: tracing the Riverbend beams. The IBC requires the glued-laminated beams to carry the design loads. The IBC points to a loads standard, ASCE/SEI 7, for snow, wind, and live loads, and to the wood design specification from the American Wood Council for the strength of the wood members. The beams must also be made by a producer who meets an industry manufacturing standard. A single sentence in the building code therefore opens a trail through three or more documents. A designer who reads only the building code and never opens the referenced standards has read part of the requirement.

    The table below summarizes the family of codes that this section defined.

    Code Typical publisher Covers Riverbend example
    International Building Code ICC Structure, fire, egress, envelope Beam size, exits, fire ratings
    National Electrical Code NFPA Electrical installation Switchboard, grounding
    Plumbing code ICC or IAPMO (varies) Water, drainage, venting Kitchen waste, restroom fixtures
    Mechanical code ICC HVAC, exhaust, ducts Rooftop unit, kitchen hood
    Fire code ICC Use and maintenance for fire safety Exit signs, extinguishers
    Accessibility standards ICC and federal Accessible routes and fixtures Ramp, restrooms

    Zoning¶

    Zoning is a local land-use law that controls what may be built on a parcel and how the building sits on it. A city divides its land into districts, and each district limits the permitted uses, the building height, the lot coverage, the setbacks (the required distances between building and property lines), the amount of parking, and sometimes the appearance. Zoning answers the question "may we build this here?", while the building code answers "is this built safely?"

    Both reviews are needed, and zoning usually comes first, because a building that is not permitted on the site never reaches building-code review. A zoning rule can also shape the structure: a setback that pushes a building toward a property line can trigger fire-resistance requirements for the wall closest to it. For Riverbend, the team confirms before schematic design that a community center is a permitted use in the site's district and that the footprint respects the setbacks.

    Building Official¶

    The building official is the person designated by a jurisdiction to administer and enforce the building code. The title may be building official, code official, or building inspector, and in larger cities the office employs plan reviewers and inspectors under that person's direction. The official's authority comes from the code and local law. It includes the power to issue permits, to review plans, to inspect work, to accept alternative materials and methods that meet the code's intent, to order unsafe work stopped, and to deny the occupancy certificate.

    The role has a limit that students sometimes miss. The building official checks that the work complies with the code and does not design the building, manage the project, or guarantee quality. The official also cannot waive the code on request. A request to depart from it must go through a formal process, discussed under variances and appeals. Treating the official as a partner in problem-solving, not an adversary, usually produces the best outcomes.

    Worked example: a pre-application meeting. Before finishing design development, the Riverbend architect meets the building official to confirm three items. The first is the edition and amendments in force, and the second is whether the kitchen's cooking equipment will be treated as an assembly accessory use. The third is the preferred way to submit drawings. The official answers the first and third directly and asks for a written narrative on the second. The 45-minute meeting costs almost nothing and removes three sources of later conflict. Without it, any of those items could have surfaced during plan review, after the construction documents were complete, when a change would be expensive.

    Building Permits¶

    A building permit is the official written authorization from the jurisdiction to begin specified construction work. It is issued after the jurisdiction reviews the plans, and it records that the work will be inspected. Typically a permit is required for new buildings, additions, alterations that change structure or systems, and the installation of major mechanical, plumbing, and electrical equipment. Cosmetic work such as painting generally does not require one. Permits also protect the owner, since later buyers, lenders, and insurers often ask for the permit and inspection records.

    Applying means submitting an application form, construction documents, and the fee, and in many cities the fee is calculated from the estimated construction value. The permit card or posted placard stays on site, and the permit expires if work does not begin or stops for a long period. Working without a permit is a violation that can bring stop-work orders, fines, and the cost of opening finished walls so that inspectors can see the work.

    Worked example: permit timeline for Riverbend. The team uses an illustrative review schedule to check whether the permit fits the construction start date. The jurisdiction takes 15 business days for the first plan review. The design team needs 5 business days to answer comments and resubmit, and the second review takes 10 business days.

    \[ 15 + 5 + 10 = 30 \text{ business days} \]

    Thirty business days is six working weeks. If the owner wants to break ground on a Monday in early April, the application must be submitted at least six weeks earlier, and more if the review comes back with additional comments. A contractor may also hold a separate, earlier permit for site grading, so that excavation can begin while review of the building proper continues. The key lesson is that the permit is a schedule activity, which belongs on the critical path of Chapter 2.

    Watch Out: Work Without a Permit

    A common trap is starting a small piece of work because it seems too minor to need a permit. The danger is that unpermitted work can force you to open finished walls later, so call the building department first and ask whether the work needs one.

    Plan Review¶

    Plan review is the process by which the building official examines the construction documents for compliance with the code before issuing a permit. Reviewers check the drawings against occupancy classification, construction type, area limits, fire-resistance ratings, egress, accessibility, structural design, energy, and the mechanical, plumbing, and electrical systems. For some buildings, other agencies, such as the fire marshal or a state office, also review plans. The reviewer returns a list of comments or corrections, and the design team revises the documents and responds in writing.

    Reviewers can only judge what the documents show. Clear code summaries on the drawings, with occupancy, construction type, areas, occupant loads, and egress widths all stated, shorten review because the reviewer can see how the designer applied the code. Plans approved by the jurisdiction are stamped and kept on site, and the inspectors use them as the standard for the work.

    Code Inspections¶

    Code inspections are visits by the building official's inspectors to the site, during construction, to verify that the work matches the approved plans and the code. They happen at defined points, typically before the work is covered and cannot be seen. The contractor requests each inspection, the inspector examines the work, and the result is recorded as approved or as a list of corrections. A failed item is fixed and reinspected before work proceeds.

    A typical sequence for a wood-framed building includes the following inspections.

    • Footing and foundation, before concrete is placed.
    • Underground plumbing and electrical, before backfill or slab.
    • Framing, once the structure and rough-in are visible.
    • Insulation and air sealing, before drywall hides them.
    • Final, when everything is complete.

    The inspector sees only a sample of the work on a given day, and inspection does not replace the contractor's own quality control.

    Inspection and Testing¶

    Inspection and testing is the broader category of checks that includes the official's inspections plus tests that show whether materials and systems perform. Some tests are required on materials, such as strength tests on concrete cylinders, compaction tests on soil under footings, and welding inspection on structural steel. Others are required on systems, such as pressure tests of piping, acceptance tests of fire alarms, and airtightness tests of the enclosure, the subject of Chapters 11 and 12.

    For structural and other critical work, the code often requires special inspections: inspections performed by an independent qualified agency hired by the owner, not by the building department. The building official reviews their reports. Testing agencies provide the data the inspector cannot gather by looking, and the reports become part of the project record that supports the certificate of occupancy.

    Watch Out: Covering Work Before It Is Inspected

    It is tempting to pour or close up a wall when the inspector is running late. The risk is that the inspector may order the work uncovered, which costs far more than waiting, so confirm every required inspection is signed off before concealing the work.

    Code Enforcement¶

    Code enforcement is the set of actions a jurisdiction uses to compel compliance with the code. During construction, enforcement means refusing to issue a permit until the plans comply, issuing a correction notice for defects found at inspection, and issuing a stop-work order when work proceeds unsafely or without a permit. For existing buildings, enforcement continues through complaints and periodic inspections, with notices of violation, fines, and in the most serious cases orders to vacate or demolish an unsafe building.

    Enforcement works by consequence. A building that has not passed final inspection cannot legally be occupied, a contractor who repeatedly fails inspections may face penalties, and an owner who ignores a notice may be fined or taken to court. Most enforcement is routine and cooperative: the inspector identifies a problem, the contractor corrects it, and the process continues. The legal tools exist for the cases where cooperation fails.

    Certificate of Occupancy¶

    A certificate of occupancy is the document issued by the building official that states a building has been inspected and complies with the code, and that it may be occupied for its stated use. It lists the occupancy classification and may state the approved occupant load, which is the figure for which the building was designed. Chapter 2 introduced it as the last step of closeout. It requires final approval from the building official, and often from the fire marshal and other reviewers, and it usually depends on the test reports and corrections described above.

    Some jurisdictions issue a temporary certificate of occupancy, which permits use of part of the building, or the whole of it, while minor items remain unfinished. If an owner changes the use of a building later, for example from offices to a restaurant, a new certificate is commonly required, since the new use brings new code requirements.

    Variances and Appeals¶

    A variance is a formal permission to depart from a requirement when strict compliance would cause unusual hardship, and an appeal is a request that a higher authority review a decision by the building official. The two are related but distinct. The usual path starts with a conversation, then a written request to the building official to accept an alternative that meets the intent of the code, and finally a hearing before a board of appeals if the designer disagrees with the official's ruling. In Minnesota, the appeal route can continue to the state level.

    Zoning variances follow a different path, since they go to a local board that decides on departures from setbacks, height limits, or use restrictions. A variance is a poor way to rescue a design that ignored the code. It works best when the designer shows that the alternative achieves the same level of safety as the prescribed rule, with evidence such as an engineering analysis or a test.

    You Can Navigate the Rulebook

    You can now explain how a model code like the IBC becomes the Minnesota State Building Code, trace a requirement through occupancy, occupant load, and exits, and follow a project from zoning check and permit through plan review, inspection, and certificate of occupancy. That is the whole path from a drawing to a legally occupied building.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Building codes are enforceable minimums adopted under the police power to protect public health, safety, and welfare. They set a floor, not a target for good design.
    • Codes use prescriptive and performance requirements, and designers apply them as a procedure: classify the occupancy, find the controlling provision, calculate, and check.
    • Model codes such as the International Building Code are written by private organizations and have no legal force until a state or local government adopts them, usually with amendments.
    • The Minnesota State Building Code applies statewide and is typically administered locally, so confirm the edition in force and the authority having jurisdiction at the start of design.
    • Subject codes, including the National Electrical Code, residential, fire, plumbing, and mechanical codes, and accessibility standards, work alongside the building code, and referenced standards extend each one.
    • Zoning decides whether a use is allowed on a site, while the building code decides whether the building is safe.
    • The building official administers the code but does not design the building or waive requirements; permits, plan review, inspections, and testing are the checks, and the certificate of occupancy is the final approval.
    • Enforcement tools include correction notices and stop-work orders, and variances and appeals provide a formal way to propose alternatives that meet the code's intent.
