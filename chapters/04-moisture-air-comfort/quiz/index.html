    3. 4. Moisture, Air Movement, and Thermal Comfort

    Quiz: Moisture, Air Movement, and Thermal Comfort¶

    Test your understanding of the concepts in this chapter with these review questions, one for each of the 12 concepts covered.

    1. Why do designers separate the four ways that moisture reaches a building instead of treating moisture as a single problem?¶

    1. Each mechanism affects a different building material, so a single control can be chosen for each material in turn
    2. Each mechanism occurs in a different season, so controls can be scheduled for the time of year
    3. Each mechanism has a different driver and needs a different control, so controlling only one leaves the others
    4. Each mechanism produces the same damage, so any single control protects the whole assembly
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Bulk water is stopped by flashing and drainage, capillary action by breaks in the material, air-carried vapor by an air barrier, and diffusion is slowed by vapor-resistant layers. The mechanisms are separated because their drivers differ, not because of materials or seasons, and a designer who controls only one has left three pathways open.

    See: Moisture

    2. A kitchen exhaust hood removes 1,000 CFM from a building that has no makeup air and a gas water heater. Why is this a safety problem as well as a comfort problem?¶

    1. The building is pressurized, so warm humid indoor air is pushed into the walls and condenses on the sheathing
    2. The hood raises the temperature of the kitchen, which lowers the stack pressure that keeps flue gases rising
    3. The hood reduces wind pressure on the windward wall, which stops the flue from drawing properly
    4. The building is depressurized, so air is pulled in through every gap, including the flue, carrying gases inside
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Air moves from higher pressure to lower pressure. An exhaust fan that removes more air than enters lowers the indoor pressure, and replacement air comes through every available path, including a water heater flue. Pressurizing describes supply fans, not exhaust fans, and the hood does not change wind pressure on walls.

    Concept Tested: Air Pressure Differences

    See: Air Pressure Differences

    3. A room leaks 40 CFM of outdoor air on a design day when the indoor-outdoor temperature difference is 70°F. Using Q = 1.08 × CFM × ΔT, how much heat must the system supply to warm that air?¶

    1. 2,800 BTU/h
    2. 3,024 BTU/h
    3. 30 BTU/h
    4. 4,536 BTU/h
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Substituting into the sensible-heat form for air gives 1.08 × 40 × 70 = 3,024 BTU/h. The result 2,800 BTU/h forgets the 1.08 factor, which combines the density and specific heat of air. The value 30 BTU/h divides instead of multiplies, and 4,536 BTU/h uses a temperature difference of about 105°F. Leakage is a large heat loss that insulation alone does not address.

    Concept Tested: Air Leakage

    See: Air Leakage

    4. Why can warm air hold far more water vapor than cold air?¶

    1. The saturation limit rises quickly with temperature, so cold air saturates with much less vapor
    2. The humidity ratio of air is fixed, so warm air only appears to hold more vapor than cold air does
    3. Cold air condenses its vapor into the walls before it can be measured
    4. Warm air is denser than cold air, so it displaces more vapor in each cubic foot of space
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Air holds a maximum amount of vapor at a given temperature, and that saturation limit rises quickly with warming. At 70°F saturated air holds about 27 times as much vapor as saturated air at −10°F. The humidity ratio is not fixed, and warm air is lighter than cold air, not denser, so density does not explain the difference.

    Concept Tested: Humidity

    See: Humidity

    5. A sealed parcel of air at 40 percent RH is warmed by 20°F with no water added. Using the rule of twenty, what is the RH afterward?¶

    1. About 40 percent
    2. About 20 percent
    3. About 80 percent
    4. About 100 percent
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The saturation limit roughly doubles for every 20°F of warming, so the same amount of vapor represents about half the capacity and the RH falls to about 20 percent. RH stays at 40 only if temperature is unchanged. The values 80 and 100 reverse the rule, which applies to cooling, where RH roughly doubles until the air reaches saturation.

    Concept Tested: Relative Humidity

    See: Relative Humidity

    6. How does the dew point differ from relative humidity?¶

    1. The dew point does not change when air is warmed or cooled, only when moisture is added or removed
    2. The dew point rises when air is warmed, while relative humidity stays constant as the air heats
    3. The dew point is the temperature at which relative humidity becomes zero percent
    4. The dew point changes with the temperature of the surrounding surfaces but not with vapor content
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The dew point is the temperature to which air must be cooled, with no change in vapor content, for it to become saturated. Because it measures the vapor itself, it is stable under heating or cooling, whereas RH changes with temperature. A higher dew point means more moisture in the air, and surface temperatures do not alter the air's dew point.

    See: Dew Point

    7. Why can vapor diffusion occur through gypsum board even when the air is perfectly still?¶

    1. Vapor is carried through the board by pressure differences that push moving air across the whole wall
    2. Vapor condenses on the warm face of the board and is drawn through by capillary action
    3. Vapor passes only through Class I vapor retarders, which are driven by temperature differences between faces
    4. Vapor molecules migrate through tiny pores from the side of higher vapor pressure to the side of lower pressure
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Diffusion is driven by a difference in vapor pressure, not by moving air. In winter the heated interior holds more vapor than the cold outdoors, so vapor pushes outward through porous materials. It usually moves far less water than air leakage does, and retarders slow it rather than being the only path.

    Concept Tested: Vapor Diffusion

    See: Vapor Diffusion

    8. Which building would experience the strongest stack-driven pressure difference?¶

    1. A short building on a mild day, because the neutral pressure plane then sits near the roof
    2. A tall building on a mild day, because warm air rises faster in taller shafts
    3. A tall building on a very cold day, because the effect grows with height and temperature difference
    4. A short building on a very cold day, because cold air is denser near the ground floor
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The stack effect arises from differences in air density between warm interior and cold exterior air. Stack pressure is about proportional to height times the difference of the reciprocals of absolute temperatures, so it increases with both height and temperature difference. That is why stairwells and elevator shafts in tall cold-climate buildings produce noticeable drafts.

    See: Stack Effect

    9. Groundwater is wicking from a concrete footing into a wood sill plate above it. Which detail best stops this?¶

    1. A thicker coat of paint on the exposed face of the sill plate and the adjacent wall
    2. A membrane between the footing and the sill that breaks the continuous pore path
    3. A tighter air barrier installed on the interior side of the exterior wall
    4. A vapor retarder installed below the finished floor of the room above
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Capillary action draws liquid water through narrow pores by surface tension, and concrete, masonry, and brick are full of such pores. A capillary break such as a membrane, coarse gravel, or damp-proofing interrupts the continuous path, because water cannot climb across a large gap. Paint, air barriers, and vapor retarders do not address liquid water wicking.

    See: Capillary Action

    10. Why can a well-insulated building with warm interior surfaces feel comfortable at a lower air temperature?¶

    1. Air speed is the only factor of comfort, so warm surfaces reduce the speed of drafts near the windows
    2. Comfort depends only on humidity, so insulation raises the humidity near the surfaces of the room
    3. Warm surfaces raise the thermostat reading, so occupants perceive the room as warmer
    4. Mean radiant temperature is a factor of comfort, so warm surfaces reduce radiant heat loss from occupants
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Comfort depends on air temperature, mean radiant temperature, air speed, humidity, metabolic rate, and clothing insulation. A person near a cold window loses heat by radiation and feels chilled at 70°F, while warm surfaces reduce that loss. The thermostat measures air temperature alone and is not changed by surface warmth.

    11. On a psychrometric chart, how do you find the dew point of air at a given state point?¶

    1. Move horizontally to the left at constant humidity ratio until you reach the saturation curve
    2. Move vertically upward at constant temperature until you reach the saturation curve
    3. Move horizontally to the right at constant humidity ratio until you reach the saturation curve
    4. Move along the constant relative humidity line until it meets the dry-bulb axis
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The horizontal axis is dry-bulb temperature and the vertical axis is humidity ratio. Sliding left keeps the vapor content unchanged while the air cools, and the temperature where the path meets the 100 percent saturation curve is the dew point. Moving right is heating, and moving vertically changes the vapor content, which defeats the definition.

    See: Psychrometric Chart

    12. Why is concealed condensation more dangerous than visible condensation on a window?¶

    1. It occurs only in winter, when freezing prevents materials from drying afterward in the spring
    2. It releases latent heat as the vapor condenses, which damages the surrounding materials directly
    3. It occurs inside walls, attics, and roofs, where it can wet insulation and framing for months unnoticed
    4. It forms only on the warm side of the wall, where the structural members are located inside
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Condensation happens when humid air touches a surface at or below its dew point. Visible condensation on glass or pipes is noticed and wiped away, while concealed condensation can wet insulation, framing, and sheathing for months. It occurs on the cold side of the wall in winter, and in summer cold ducts and pipes can sweat, so it is not limited to one season.
