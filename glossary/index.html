    Glossary of Terms¶

    Welcome to the Glossary

    Hi, I'm Beau! Every trade has its own language, and this glossary is your translator for the built environment. Look up any term here when a chapter uses a word you want to pin down. Let's build it right!

    The terms below come from the book's concept list. Each definition is followed, where helpful, by an example and links to related terms.

    Accessibility Standards¶

    The rules, such as ADA standards and the accessibility chapter of the building code, that set dimensions and features so people with disabilities can use buildings. They cover ramps, doors, restrooms, and routes.

    Example: A ramp may not be steeper than 1:12.

    Reference: 2010 ADA Standards, Section 405 Ramps (U.S. Access Board)

    See also: Elevators, Means of Egress

    Adaptive Reuse¶

    The conversion of an existing building to a new use different from its original purpose. It saves embodied energy and preserves character.

    Example: An old flour mill in Minneapolis becomes apartments.

    See also: Renovation and Retrofit, Embodied Energy

    Aggregates¶

    Granular materials, such as sand, gravel, and crushed stone, that make up most of the volume of concrete and mortar. Their size, shape, and cleanliness affect strength and workability.

    Example: Concrete for a driveway may use crushed limestone up to 3/4 in. in size.

    See also: Concrete Mix Design

    Air Barrier¶

    A material or assembly that blocks the movement of air through the enclosure and withstands wind pressure. Its performance depends on sealed joints and penetrations.

    Example: A self-adhered membrane over sheathing forms the barrier at a commercial wall.

    See also: Air Control Layer, Air Sealing

    Air Conditioning¶

    The process of cooling and dehumidifying indoor air using a refrigeration cycle, along with filtering and circulating it. It protects comfort in warm weather.

    Example: A split system has an outdoor compressor and an indoor coil.

    See also: Cooling Systems

    Air Control Layer¶

    The continuous plane in an assembly that stops air from moving through the enclosure. It can be membrane, sheathing, or drywall with sealed joints.

    Example: Taped OSB sheathing can serve as the air control layer in a wall.

    See also: Air Barrier, Air Leakage

    Air Distribution¶

    The delivery of conditioned air to spaces through ducts, registers, diffusers, and returns, so temperatures are even. Design balances airflow in each room.

    Example: A supply register under a window blows warm air against cold glass.

    See also: Ductwork

    Air Leakage¶

    The uncontrolled flow of air through gaps, cracks, and joints in the enclosure. It wastes energy and carries moisture into assemblies.

    Example: A gap around a plumbing pipe through the top plate lets warm moist air reach the attic.

    See also: Air Sealing, Blower Door Test

    Air Pressure Differences¶

    The gaps in air pressure between two locations, such as inside and outside a building. They drive air through cracks and openings.

    Example: Wind pushing on one wall creates higher pressure on that side and lower pressure on the opposite side.

    See also: Stack Effect, Air Leakage

    Air Sealing¶

    The work of closing gaps, cracks, and penetrations in the enclosure using caulk, tape, gaskets, and foam. It cuts energy loss and moisture problems.

    Example: A crew foams the gaps around wiring penetrations in the attic floor.

    See also: Air Leakage, Blower Door Test

    Allowable Building Area¶

    The maximum floor area per story permitted by code for a given occupancy and construction type, with increases for sprinklers and open frontage. It limits fire exposure.

    Example: A one-story Type VB (wood-frame) store may be limited to 9,000 sq ft per floor unless it is sprinklered.

    Reference: IBC 2018 Table 506.2, Minnesota edition (UpCodes)

    See also: Construction Types, Occupancy Classification

    Alternating Current¶

    Electric current that reverses direction periodically, at 60 cycles per second in North America. It travels efficiently over long distances and is the form delivered to buildings.

    Example: Wall outlets supply alternating current at 60 hertz.

    Contrast with: Direct Current

    Architect¶

    A licensed design professional who plans buildings, coordinates the design team, and prepares the drawings and specifications for permit and construction. The role carries responsibility for overall layout, appearance, and code compliance.

    See also: Structural Engineer, Mechanical Engineer

    ASTM Standards¶

    The published test methods and product specifications developed by ASTM International through consensus of industry experts. Codes and project specifications cite them to define quality.

    Example: ASTM C150 sets requirements for portland cement.

    See also: Material Testing, Referenced Standards

    Attic Ventilation¶

    The intentional flow of outdoor air through a roof space, via soffit and ridge vents, to carry off moisture and cold-weather warmth. It helps keep the roof deck cold and dry.

    Example: Continuous soffit and ridge vents draw air along the underside of the roof deck.

    See also: Ice Dams

    Basements¶

    Building levels located partly or fully below ground, enclosed by foundation walls. They provide living space, storage, or mechanical rooms and require moisture control.

    Example: A walkout basement is open to grade on one side.

    See also: Foundation Walls, Foundation Waterproofing

    Beams¶

    Horizontal structural members that carry loads across an opening to supports at their ends. They resist bending and shear, and their depth largely governs how far they can span.

    Example: A steel beam spans a basement to carry the floor above.

    See also: Girders, Joists

    Bearing Walls¶

    Walls that carry loads from floors or roofs above down to the foundation, in addition to their own weight. Removing one without replacement can cause collapse.

    Example: An interior wall running perpendicular to the ceiling joists often carries the joist ends.

    Contrast with: Shear Walls

    Bidding and Procurement¶

    The phase in which contractors price the work and the owner selects who will build it, along with the purchasing of materials and equipment. Competitive prices and clear scopes shape the contract.

    Example: Three general contractors submit sealed prices for a library addition, and the owner awards the contract.

    See also: Design-Bid-Build

    Blower Door Test¶

    A field test in which a calibrated fan depressurizes a building to measure its air leakage, often reported as air changes per hour at 50 Pascals. It also helps find leaks.

    Example: A new house testing at 3 ACH50 meets the Minnesota residential target.

    Reference: Minnesota Energy Code, Chapter 4 Residential Energy Efficiency (UpCodes)

    See also: Air Leakage, Air Sealing

    Boiler¶

    An appliance that heats water or makes steam for radiators, in-floor tubing, or other hydronic emitters. It can burn gas, oil, or use electricity.

    Example: A boiler sends hot water through baseboard heaters in a school.

    See also: Radiant Heating, Heating Systems

    Bolted Connections¶

    Joints that join steel members by high-strength bolts passing through aligned holes. They resist load by clamping friction or by bearing and shear on the bolts.

    Example: Bolts through an angle clip join a beam web to a column flange.

    See also: Welded Connections, Shear Strength

    Bracing¶

    Diagonal or cross members that stabilize a frame against sideways movement and buckling. They may be made of wood, steel, or tension rods.

    Example: X-shaped steel rods in a warehouse wall stiffen the frame.

    See also: Lateral Load System

    Branch Circuits¶

    The wiring between the final overcurrent device and the outlets, lights, or equipment it serves. Each has a rated amperage, such as 15 or 20 amps.

    Example: One 20 amp circuit powers the kitchen counter receptacles.

    See also: Circuit Breakers, Receptacles

    Brick¶

    A rectangular building unit made of fired clay or concrete, typically about 8 in. long, used in walls and paving. Fired clay units are valued for durability and appearance.

    Example: A modular brick measures about 2-1/4 in. by 3-5/8 in. by 7-5/8 in.

    Reference: Brick Industry Association Technical Note 10

    See also: Masonry, Brick Veneer

    Brick Veneer¶

    A single layer of brick attached to a wall frame by metal ties, providing appearance and weather protection but no structural support. An air space behind it allows drainage.

    Example: A house with a 4 in. brick face tied to wood studs uses this system.

    See also: Brick, Rainscreen

    Brittleness¶

    The tendency of a material to fracture with little or no visible deformation. Failures of this kind can occur suddenly and without warning to occupants.

    Example: Glass and unreinforced concrete crack abruptly when overloaded.

    Contrast with: Ductility

    Building¶

    An enclosed, roofed structure erected on a site to shelter people, activities, equipment, or goods. It combines structure, enclosure, and mechanical and electrical systems into one working assembly.

    Example: A two-story wood-framed house, a steel-framed warehouse, and a brick school are all buildings.

    See also: Building Types, Building Systems

    Building Assessment¶

    The systematic evaluation of an existing building's condition, performance, and needs through inspection, measurement, and records. It guides maintenance and renovation decisions.

    Example: A survey rates a school's roof, windows, and boiler for repair.

    See also: Renovation and Retrofit, Building Forensics

    Building Automation¶

    The networked controls and sensors that monitor and operate building systems such as HVAC, lighting, and security. They adjust equipment to schedules and conditions.

    Example: A control system lowers heating setpoints in a school overnight.

    See also: Lighting Controls, Commissioning

    Building Codes¶

    The legal rules adopted by governments that set minimum standards for the design and construction of buildings to protect health, safety, and welfare. They are enforced through permits and inspection.

    See also: Model Codes, Code Enforcement

    Building Enclosure¶

    The physical separator between the conditioned interior and the exterior environment, including walls, roof, foundation, windows, and doors. It controls water, air, vapor, and heat flow.

    The enclosure is the center of cold-climate building science and is a major focus of this book.

    See also: Control Layers, Wall Assemblies

    Building Failure¶

    An event or condition in which a building element or system no longer performs as intended, ranging from leaks to structural collapse. Its causes include design errors, defects, and neglect.

    Example: A roof collapse under heavy snow is a structural failure.

    See also: Building Forensics, Water Intrusion

    Building Forensics¶

    The investigation of failures and defects through observation, testing, and analysis to determine their causes. Findings may support repair, learning, or legal action.

    Example: An engineer examines a leaking wall by cutting an opening and testing moisture.

    See also: Building Failure, Building Assessment

    Building Information Modeling¶

    The creation and use of a digital, data-rich three-dimensional model of a building shared by the project team. Elements in the model carry properties such as size, material, and cost.

    Example: A model reveals a duct that collides with a steel beam before any material is purchased.

    See also: Interdisciplinary Coordination

    Building Maintenance¶

    The routine upkeep and repair of building elements to keep them safe and working, such as cleaning gutters or recaulking joints. It extends service life.

    Example: Resealing a deck every few years protects the wood.

    See also: Preventive Maintenance

    Building Materials¶

    The raw, manufactured, or processed substances used to form the structure, enclosure, and finishes of a building. Common groups include wood, concrete, masonry, steel, glass, plastics, and insulation.

    Example: Dimensional lumber, concrete block, and gypsum board are building materials found in a typical Minnesota home.

    See also: Material Properties, Material Selection

    Building Official¶

    The local government employee who administers and enforces the building code, reviews plans, issues permits, and conducts inspections. This person has legal authority to approve or reject work.

    See also: Building Permits, Code Inspections

    Building Permits¶

    The official approvals issued by a local authority that allow construction to begin after plans are reviewed. They permit inspections during the work.

    Example: A homeowner obtains a permit before building a deck.

    See also: Plan Review, Building Official

    Building Resilience¶

    The capacity of a building and community to resist, absorb, and recover from hazards such as storms, floods, power loss, and extreme temperatures. Design planning supports quick recovery.

    Example: A shelter with backup power remains usable after a blackout.

    See also: Durability, Emergency Power

    Building Science¶

    The applied study of how heat, air, moisture, light, and sound move through buildings and affect durability, comfort, and energy use. It uses physics to explain why building assemblies succeed or fail.

    Building science ties together most chapters of this book, especially enclosure and durability.

    See also: Heat Transfer, Moisture

    Building Systems¶

    The organized groups of components that perform a building function, such as structure, enclosure, heating and cooling, plumbing, fire protection, and electrical service. Each group works with the others to keep a building safe and usable.

    See also: HVAC Systems, Plumbing Systems, Electrical Systems

    Building Types¶

    The categories used to sort buildings by their purpose, such as houses, offices, schools, factories, and hospitals. Each category carries typical sizes, layouts, and code requirements.

    Example: A fire station and a restaurant are different types, even if both use steel framing.

    See also: Occupancy Classification

    Built Environment¶

    The human-made surroundings in which people live, work, and gather, including structures, roads, utilities, and the spaces between them. It contrasts with natural landscapes untouched by construction.

    Example: A Minneapolis neighborhood with its houses, sidewalks, skyway links, and storm sewers is part of the human-made setting students study in this book.

    See also: Building, Construction Industry

    Capillary Action¶

    The movement of liquid water through tiny pores or narrow gaps in a material, even against gravity, because of surface tension and adhesion. It can pull ground moisture into concrete and masonry.

    Example: Water wicks upward from damp soil into an unprotected brick wall.

    See also: Porosity

    Carbon Sequestration¶

    The long-term capture and storage of atmospheric carbon dioxide in materials such as wood, soil, or plants. Wood products hold the carbon absorbed during growth.

    Example: A timber frame stores carbon taken in by the trees.

    See also: Mass Timber, Renewable Materials

    Cast-in-Place Concrete¶

    Concrete poured into forms at its final location on the construction site and cured in place. It allows continuous, custom shapes.

    Example: A basement wall and footing are poured on site in forms.

    Contrast with: Precast Concrete

    Cellulose Insulation¶

    Loose-fill or dense-pack insulation made mostly from recycled paper treated with fire retardant. It provides about R-3.5 per inch and fills irregular spaces well.

    Example: Blown cellulose fills an attic to a depth of about 14 in. for R-50.

    Reference: U.S. DOE Energy Renovations: Insulation guide

    See also: Insulation, Recycled Content

    Cement¶

    A fine powder, usually portland type, that reacts chemically with water to bind sand and gravel into a solid mass. It is the active ingredient in concrete, mortar, and grout, and it is a major source of building carbon emissions.

    Example: A 94 lb bag of portland cement is a standard unit for small mixes.

    See also: Concrete, Cement Substitutes (SCMs)

    Cement Substitutes (SCMs)¶

    Supplementary cementitious materials, such as fly ash, slag, and silica fume, that replace part of the portland cement in concrete. They lower carbon emissions and can improve durability.

    Example: A mix replaces 30% of cement with slag.

    See also: Cement, Embodied Carbon

    Certificate of Occupancy¶

    The official document issued by the building department stating that a completed building meets code and may be used. It lists the approved use and occupancy group.

    Example: A restaurant opens only after it receives this certificate.

    See also: Occupancy and Closeout

    Change Orders¶

    Written agreements that modify the contract's cost, schedule, or scope after the work has begun. Each records what changed and how the price or time is adjusted.

    Example: An owner adds six electrical outlets to a classroom, and a change order adds $1,800 to the contract.

    Circuit Breakers¶

    Resettable switches that automatically open a circuit when current exceeds a safe level. They protect wires from overheating and reduce the chance of electrical fires.

    Example: A 20 amp breaker trips when a kitchen circuit is overloaded.

    See also: Panelboards, Branch Circuits

    Cladding¶

    The exterior surface layer of a wall that protects it from weather and gives its appearance. It sheds most rain and is separated from the structure by a drainage plane.

    Example: Fiber-cement lap boards, brick, and metal panels are all claddings.

    See also: Siding, Rainscreen

    Code Enforcement¶

    The set of actions taken by local officials to bring buildings into agreement with adopted codes, including plan review, inspection, written notices, and penalties.

    Example: A building official orders correction of unpermitted electrical work.

    See also: Building Official, Code Inspections

    Code Inspections¶

    The site visits by the building official to verify work matches approved plans and codes at stages like footing, framing, and final. Approval is needed to proceed.

    Example: An inspector checks the framing and fireblocking before insulation is installed.

    See also: Building Official, Inspection and Testing

    Columns¶

    Vertical structural members that carry loads mainly in compression from beams or floors to the foundation. Their strength depends on cross-section and height.

    Example: A steel pipe column supports a beam in the middle of a basement.

    See also: Beams

    Combustibility¶

    The tendency of a material to ignite and sustain burning under fire conditions. Codes classify materials as combustible or noncombustible by standardized test.

    Example: Wood is combustible, while concrete and steel are classified as noncombustible.

    See also: Fire Resistance

    Commercial Construction¶

    The building of structures used for business activity, including offices, retail stores, restaurants, hotels, and warehouses. Projects are generally larger and more complex than houses and follow the commercial building code.

    Example: A four-story office building with a steel frame and curtain wall is commercial work.

    Contrast with: Residential Construction

    Commissioning¶

    The quality-assurance process of testing building systems to verify they operate as the owner intended. It catches installation and control errors before the building is turned over.

    Example: A commissioning agent confirms that a rooftop unit heats, cools, and ventilates at the design airflows.

    See also: HVAC Systems, Occupancy and Closeout

    Compressive Strength¶

    The maximum squeezing stress a material can bear before crushing or failing. It is the main measure of quality for concrete and masonry.

    Example: A concrete cylinder crushed at 4,500 psi has a compressive strength of 4,500 psi.

    Contrast with: Tensile Strength

    Concrete¶

    A hardened construction material made by mixing cement, water, sand, and gravel, which cures into a stone-like mass. It is strong in compression and weak in tension.

    Example: Sidewalks, foundation walls, and parking ramps are commonly made of concrete.

    See also: Cement, Aggregates, Reinforced Concrete

    Concrete Masonry Units¶

    Hollow or solid precast blocks of concrete, commonly 8 by 8 by 16 in., stacked and mortared to form walls. The hollow cores can be filled with grout and reinforcement.

    Example: An 8 in. block foundation wall is built from these units.

    See also: Masonry, Grout

    Concrete Mix Design¶

    The selection and proportioning of cement, water, aggregates, and additives to achieve a target strength, workability, and durability. Mix recipes are tested before large pours.

    Example: A mix for a Minnesota exterior slab includes air-entraining admixture to resist freezing.

    See also: Water-Cement Ratio, Freeze-Thaw Damage

    Concrete Placement¶

    The process of delivering, depositing, spreading, and consolidating fresh concrete into forms. Proper handling avoids voids and separation of materials.

    Example: Crews pump concrete into wall forms and vibrate it to remove trapped air.

    See also: Formwork, Curing

    Concrete Slabs¶

    Flat horizontal concrete elements used as floors, pavements, or roof decks. They range from thin residential floors to thick structural slabs.

    Example: A 4 in. basement slab with a vapor retarder beneath forms the floor.

    See also: Slab-on-Grade, Control Joints

    Condensation¶

    The change of water vapor into liquid when air meets a surface at or below its dew point. In buildings it forms on cold windows or inside wall cavities.

    Example: Droplets on a single-pane window on a cold morning show that warm moist air touched the glass.

    See also: Dew Point, Vapor Diffusion

    Conduction¶

    The flow of thermal energy through a solid or between materials in direct contact, passing from molecule to molecule. Metals conduct readily, while foam and air pockets resist it.

    Example: A steel nail through a wall carries cold to the inside face faster than the surrounding wood.

    See also: Thermal Bridging

    Conductors¶

    Wires or bars of copper or aluminum that carry electric current. Their size is chosen to carry the load without overheating.

    Example: 12-gauge copper wire is common for 20 amp circuits.

    See also: Conduit, Current

    Conduit¶

    Metal or plastic tubing that holds and protects electrical wires. It gives mechanical protection and a path for adding wires later.

    Example: Steel conduit shields wiring in an industrial shop.

    See also: Conductors

    Construction Administration¶

    The design team's service during construction, including reviewing submittals, answering questions, observing progress, and approving payment requests. It helps the finished work match the documents.

    See also: Submittals, Requests for Information

    Construction Documents¶

    The final set of drawings and specifications used to obtain permits, price the work, and build the project. They contain enough detail for a contractor to construct the building without further design decisions.

    See also: Specifications, Bidding and Procurement

    Construction Drawings¶

    The set of drawn documents that show the size, location, and arrangement of a building and its parts. They communicate design intent to builders, inspectors, and permit reviewers.

    See also: Plans, Elevations, Sections, Details

    Construction Industry¶

    The economic sector of firms, trades, suppliers, and professionals that design, erect, alter, and repair buildings and infrastructure. It covers both the planning side and the physical work on site.

    Understanding how the sector is organized helps students see where their future careers fit.

    See also: General Contractor, Subcontractor

    Construction Manager at Risk¶

    A delivery method in which a construction firm advises the owner during design and then guarantees a maximum price for the work. The firm assumes financial responsibility for cost overruns beyond that price.

    See also: General Contractor

    Construction Process¶

    The sequence of activities that turns completed designs into a finished building, from site work through final cleanup. It involves scheduling, material delivery, trade coordination, and inspection.

    See also: Construction Sequencing

    Construction Sequencing¶

    The planned order in which building tasks are carried out so that each trade can work without waiting on or damaging another. Foundations precede framing, and enclosure precedes interior finishes.

    See also: Critical Path

    Construction Terminology¶

    The specialized vocabulary used by designers, builders, and inspectors to describe materials, assemblies, processes, and documents precisely. A shared vocabulary reduces costly misunderstandings on a project.

    Example: Calling a horizontal framing member at a window opening a "header" tells the crew exactly what is meant.

    Construction Types¶

    The code categories, Type I through Type V, that classify a building by the combustibility and fire resistance of its structural elements. They limit height and area.

    Example: A noncombustible, rated steel frame is Type I or II, while a wood-frame house is Type V.

    See also: Allowable Building Area, Fire-Resistance Ratings

    Construction Waste¶

    The materials discarded during building, such as cut-offs, packaging, and damaged goods. It makes up a major share of landfill volume.

    Example: A crew sorts wood, drywall, and metal into separate dumpsters.

    See also: Reuse and Salvage, Deconstruction

    Continuous Footings¶

    Long strips of concrete beneath a wall that spread its load along the full length. They are also called strip footings.

    Example: A 16 in. wide, 8 in. thick strip of concrete sits under a basement wall.

    See also: Spread Footings, Footing Reinforcement

    Continuous Insulation¶

    Insulation installed in an unbroken layer over the structural members of an assembly with no gaps from framing. It reduces thermal bridging.

    Example: A layer of foam board over wall studs covers the framing.

    See also: Thermal Bridging, Rigid Foam Insulation

    Control Joints¶

    Planned grooves or breaks placed in concrete or masonry to give cracking from shrinkage a straight, hidden line. They are not intended to allow large movement.

    Example: Saw cuts every 10 ft in a driveway slab channel cracking along the cut.

    Contrast with: Expansion Joints

    Control Layers¶

    The planes within the enclosure that manage the flow of water, air, vapor, and heat. Each layer has a distinct job and is most effective when it is unbroken.

    See also: Water Control Layer, Air Control Layer, Thermal Control Layer

    Convection¶

    The transfer of thermal energy by the movement of a fluid, such as air or water, which carries energy from one place to another. It can occur naturally or be driven by fans or pumps.

    Example: Warm air rising inside an uninsulated wall cavity carries energy to the cold upper area.

    See also: Stack Effect

    Cool Roofs¶

    Roofs with highly reflective and emissive surfaces that absorb less solar heat than standard roofs. They reduce cooling loads in warm weather.

    Example: A white membrane on a store roof lowers its summer surface temperature.

    See also: Roofing Membranes, Green Roofs

    Cooling Systems¶

    Equipment that removes heat and often moisture from indoor air and releases it outdoors. It includes air conditioners, heat pumps, and chillers.

    Example: A chiller supplies cold water to cooling coils in a hospital.

    See also: Air Conditioning

    Corrosion¶

    The chemical or electrochemical deterioration of metal, such as rusting of iron and steel, caused by moisture, oxygen, and salts. It can weaken structural members and fasteners.

    Example: Road salt in a Minnesota parking ramp attacks the steel inside the concrete.

    See also: Reinforcing Steel

    Cracking¶

    The formation of splits in materials such as concrete, masonry, plaster, and drywall because of stress, shrinkage, or movement. Cracks vary from cosmetic to structural.

    Example: Hairline cracks in a new slab are caused by drying shrinkage.

    See also: Control Joints, Settlement

    Crawl Spaces¶

    Shallow areas between the ground and the first floor, with limited headroom, enclosed by short foundation walls. They give access to pipes and wiring beneath the floor.

    Example: A one-story house may have a 3 ft crawl space under the floor framing.

    See also: Foundation Systems

    Creep and Shrinkage¶

    The slow, time-dependent changes in size of a material, where creep is gradual deformation under constant load and shrinkage is volume loss from drying. Both affect concrete and wood.

    Example: A long concrete slab shortens slightly as it dries and a wood beam sags a little more over the years under steady load.

    See also: Cracking, Control Joints

    Critical Path¶

    The longest chain of dependent tasks in a schedule, which sets the shortest possible project duration. A delay in any task on this chain delays the finish date.

    Example: If concrete curing delays the foundation by a week, framing, roofing, and finishes may all shift a week.

    See also: Construction Sequencing

    Cross-Laminated Timber¶

    A large solid panel made of layers of lumber stacked at right angles and glued together. Panels act as walls, floors, and roofs in mass timber buildings.

    Example: A 5-layer panel forms the floor of a multistory residence hall.

    See also: Mass Timber

    Curing¶

    The maintenance of moisture and temperature in fresh concrete so that cement continues to react and strength develops. Cold weather requires added protection.

    Example: Workers cover a winter slab with insulated blankets to keep it from freezing.

    See also: Concrete Placement, Creep and Shrinkage

    Current¶

    The rate at which electric charge flows past a point in a conductor, measured in amperes. It is what heats wires and trips breakers.

    Example: A 1,200 watt heater on 120 volts draws 10 amperes.

    See also: Voltage, Conductors

    Curtain Wall¶

    A lightweight exterior wall system, often aluminum and glass, hung from a building's structure and carrying no floor loads. It resists wind and keeps out weather.

    Example: The glass front of an office tower is usually a curtain wall.

    See also: Glazing, Cladding

    Data and Communications¶

    The cabling and equipment, such as network wiring, phone lines, and wireless access points, that carry information within and into a building. They share pathways with other systems.

    Example: Category 6 cable runs from a closet to wall jacks in each classroom.

    See also: Low-Voltage Systems

    Daylight¶

    Natural light from the sun and sky that enters a building through windows, skylights, and other openings. It reduces electric lighting use and affects occupant well-being.

    See also: Daylighting

    Daylighting¶

    The planned use of natural light to illuminate interior spaces, using windows, skylights, light shelves, and reflective surfaces. It cuts electric lighting and improves well-being.

    Example: Tall windows and light-colored ceilings bring daylight deep into a classroom.

    See also: Daylight, Lighting Controls

    Dead Loads¶

    The permanent weight of a building's own materials and fixed equipment. They act constantly and are calculated from material densities.

    Example: The weight of framing, roofing, and drywall in a house is dead load.

    Contrast with: Live Loads

    Deconstruction¶

    The careful, systematic dismantling of a building to recover materials for reuse or recycling, instead of demolishing it. It reduces waste and preserves resources.

    Example: Workers remove doors, flooring, and timbers by hand for resale.

    See also: Reuse and Salvage, Construction Waste

    Deep Foundations¶

    Foundation elements that reach far below the surface to bear on strong soil or rock or to develop friction along their length. They include piles and drilled piers.

    Example: A bridge on soft river soils rests on steel piles driven 80 ft down.

    See also: Piles, Drilled Piers

    Deflection¶

    The amount a loaded structural member bends or moves from its original position. Codes limit it as a fraction of span to protect finishes and comfort.

    Example: A floor joist spanning 12 ft with a limit of L/360 may sag no more than 0.4 in.

    Reference: IBC 2018 Chapter 16, Table 1604.3 (ICC)

    See also: Stiffness

    Density¶

    The mass of a material per unit of volume, often stated in pounds per cubic foot (pcf). It affects weight, strength, and thermal behavior.

    Example: Normal-weight concrete weighs about 150 pcf, while lumber is roughly 30 to 40 pcf.

    Design Development¶

    The design phase that refines the approved schematic concept by choosing materials, structural systems, and mechanical and electrical systems. Drawings gain more detail and coordination.

    Example: During this phase the team decides between a steel frame and a wood frame and sizes the major ducts.

    See also: Construction Documents

    Design Process¶

    The sequence of steps in which a design team turns an owner's needs into drawings and specifications. It moves from broad ideas to precise instructions.

    Example: A school district's request for a new gym passes through sketches, refined plans, and finally detailed drawings.

    See also: Project Phases

    Design-Bid-Build¶

    A project delivery method in which a complete design is finished first, contractors then bid on it, and the winner builds it. The owner holds separate contracts with the designer and the builder.

    Contrast with: Design-Build

    Design-Build¶

    A project delivery method in which one entity holds a single contract with the owner for both design and construction. Overlap of the two efforts can shorten the schedule.

    Example: A contractor with in-house architects delivers a fire station under one agreement.

    Contrast with: Design-Bid-Build

    Details¶

    Enlarged drawings that show how specific parts of a building are assembled and joined. They give the exact layers, fasteners, and dimensions needed for construction.

    Example: A window-head detail shows the flashing, sealant, and framing around the top of a window.

    See also: Sections

    Dew Point¶

    The temperature at which air becomes saturated with vapor and liquid water begins to form on a surface. It depends on the amount of vapor in the air.

    Example: If indoor air has a dew point of 50 °F, a window surface at 45 °F will collect droplets.

    See also: Condensation, Relative Humidity

    Diaphragms¶

    Horizontal or sloped plates, such as floors and roofs, that collect lateral forces and distribute them to vertical resisting elements. They act like a deep, thin beam laid flat.

    Example: A plywood roof deck passes wind force to the shear walls below.

    See also: Shear Walls

    Direct Current¶

    Electric current that flows steadily in a single direction through a circuit. Batteries and solar panels produce it, and many electronic devices run on it.

    Example: A phone charger converts the alternating current from an outlet to direct current.

    Contrast with: Alternating Current

    Doors¶

    Movable barriers set in wall openings that provide passage, security, and weather protection for rooms. Exterior units also resist air and water leakage.

    Example: An insulated steel entry door has a foam core and weatherstripping.

    See also: Means of Egress, Air Leakage

    Drain-Waste-Vent System¶

    The piping network that carries wastewater and sewer gas out of a building, with vents admitting air to keep water flowing and trap seals intact. It relies on slope and gravity.

    Example: A 3 in. vent stack extends through the roof above a bathroom.

    See also: Plumbing Fixtures, Plumbing Code

    Drilled Piers¶

    Deep foundation shafts formed by drilling a hole, placing reinforcing, and filling it with concrete. Their diameter is usually larger than that of driven piles.

    Example: A 30 in. diameter pier drilled 25 ft deep supports a heavy column.

    See also: Deep Foundations

    Ductility¶

    The ability of a material to stretch or deform substantially before it fractures. Ductile materials give visible warning before failure.

    Example: Mild steel bends and stretches before breaking.

    Contrast with: Brittleness

    Ductwork¶

    The sheet-metal or flexible channels that carry heated, cooled, or ventilation air between equipment and rooms. Sealed, insulated ducts avoid energy loss.

    Example: Round supply ducts run from a furnace to floor registers.

    See also: Air Distribution

    Durability¶

    The ability of a material or assembly to maintain its performance and appearance over time while exposed to weather, wear, and use. It underlies long service life.

    Example: Properly flashed brick can last a century.

    See also: Service Life, Weathering

    Efflorescence¶

    A white, powdery deposit of salts left on the surface of masonry or concrete after water carries the salts out and evaporates. It indicates moisture movement.

    Example: White staining on a brick wall points to water passing through.

    See also: Capillary Action, Masonry

    Electric Vehicle Charging¶

    Equipment that supplies electricity to recharge a car battery, from a standard outlet to a high-power station. Codes address circuit size and installation.

    Example: A 240 volt, 40 amp circuit in a garage charges a car overnight.

    See also: Branch Circuits

    Electrical Design Phases¶

    The ordered steps of electrical design, from load estimate to layout, calculation, drawing, and coordination, that align with the project design phases. Detail increases at each step.

    See also: Electrical Plans

    Electrical Designer¶

    The engineer or qualified designer who lays out power distribution, lighting, and low-voltage systems for a building, often working under a licensed electrical engineer who takes professional responsibility for the design. The work includes sizing wires, panels, and protective devices.

    See also: Electrical Systems, National Electrical Code

    Electrical Energy¶

    The total amount of electricity consumed over a period, measured in kilowatt-hours. Utilities bill customers according to this quantity, so it drives operating cost.

    Example: A 100 watt bulb left on for 10 hours uses 1 kilowatt-hour.

    See also: Electrical Power

    Electrical Load Calculations¶

    The step-by-step computation of the total power demand of a building using code rules and demand factors. Results set the service size.

    Example: A load calculation for a 2,000 sq ft house may require a 200 amp service.

    See also: Electrical Loads, National Electrical Code

    Electrical Loads¶

    The devices and equipment that draw power from a system, such as lights, motors, heaters, and computers. Their sum determines the size of wires and panels.

    Example: A range, a dryer, and a furnace are separate loads on a house service.

    See also: Electrical Load Calculations

    Electrical Plans¶

    Drawings that show the location of panels, outlets, lights, switches, and wiring paths in a building. They guide the electrician and inspector.

    Example: A power plan marks each outlet with a symbol and a circuit number.

    See also: One-Line Diagram

    Electrical Power¶

    The rate at which electrical energy is used or delivered, measured in watts, and equal to voltage multiplied by current. Larger loads need larger services.

    Example: A 15 amp circuit at 120 volts can carry 1,800 watts.

    See also: Electrical Energy

    Electrical Systems¶

    The wiring, devices, and equipment that bring electricity into a building and distribute it safely to lights, outlets, and machines.

    See also: Service Entrance, Panelboards, Branch Circuits

    Electricity¶

    The flow of electric charge in a conductor, used to carry energy for lighting, heat, motors, and electronics. It is produced at power plants or by solar panels.

    Example: Electricity from a utility line powers the lights in a house.

    See also: Voltage, Current

    Elevations¶

    Drawings that show the exterior or interior face of a building as seen straight on from the side. They convey heights, materials, window placement, and overall appearance.

    Example: The south elevation of a house shows window heights, siding, and roof slope.

    See also: Plans, Sections

    Elevators¶

    Powered lifting machines that carry people and goods between floors in a car guided by rails. Codes require accessibility, safety devices, and fire service operation.

    Example: A traction elevator in a mid-rise building uses cables and a counterweight.

    See also: Accessibility Standards

    Embodied Carbon¶

    The greenhouse gas emissions, in carbon dioxide equivalent, released during the extraction, manufacture, transport, and installation of building materials. Concrete and steel are major sources.

    Example: A ton of cement produces roughly a ton of carbon dioxide.

    Contrast with: Operational Carbon

    Embodied Energy¶

    The total energy consumed to extract, process, manufacture, transport, and install a building material or product. It is measured in megajoules or Btu per unit.

    Example: Aluminum has far greater embodied energy per pound than lumber.

    See also: Embodied Carbon

    Emergency Power¶

    The backup electrical supply that powers life-safety loads, such as exit lights and fire pumps, when normal service fails. It starts within seconds.

    Example: A hospital's generator restores power to critical rooms within 10 seconds.

    Reference: NEC Article 700 and NFPA 110 Type 10 summary (Corex Engineers)

    See also: Generators, Uninterruptible Power Supply

    Energy Code Compliance¶

    The demonstration that a building design and construction meet energy code requirements, through prescriptive tables, performance modeling, or testing. Inspectors verify the installed work.

    Example: A builder shows insulation levels and a passing blower door result to satisfy the code.

    See also: Minnesota Energy Code, Blower Door Test

    Energy Efficiency¶

    The use of less energy to deliver the same comfort and function, achieved by better enclosure, equipment, and controls. It lowers cost and emissions.

    Example: Adding attic insulation and sealing leaks cuts a home's heating bills.

    See also: High-Performance Buildings, Energy Code Compliance

    Engineered Wood Products¶

    Structural building products manufactured by bonding wood strands, veneers, or lumber with adhesives into larger or more uniform members. They offer predictable strength and long spans.

    Example: I-joists and laminated veneer lumber beams are engineered wood.

    See also: Glulam, Plywood and OSB

    Equilibrium¶

    The state in which all forces and moments acting on a body balance, so the body stays at rest. Static structures depend on this balance.

    Example: A beam on two supports remains still because the upward support reactions equal the downward loads.

    See also: Forces

    Excavation¶

    The removal of soil or rock to create openings for foundations, basements, utilities, and drainage. Digging may require shoring to keep the sides from collapsing.

    Example: A backhoe digs a trench for continuous footings below the frost line.

    See also: Site Preparation

    Expansion Joints¶

    Gaps filled with compressible material that let building elements grow and shrink with temperature changes without cracking. They separate adjoining parts of a structure or finish.

    Example: A flexible filler strip lets a long brick wall expand in summer.

    Contrast with: Control Joints

    Feeders¶

    The conductors that carry power from the service equipment to a subpanel or distribution equipment. They sit between the service and the branch circuits.

    Example: A feeder runs from the main switchboard to a floor panel in an office.

    See also: Branch Circuits, Panelboards

    Fiberglass Insulation¶

    Insulation made from fine glass fibers, sold as batts, rolls, or loose-fill. It has an R-value of about 2.9 to 3.8 per inch and is not combustible.

    Example: R-13 batts fit between 2x4 wall studs, which are 3.5 in. deep.

    Reference: U.S. DOE Energy Renovations: Insulation guide

    See also: Insulation

    Fire Alarm Systems¶

    Equipment that detects smoke or heat and warns occupants through horns, strobes, and signals to the fire department. It includes detectors, pull stations, and a control panel.

    Example: A pull station and smoke detectors connect to a panel in the lobby.

    See also: Fire Protection, Life Safety

    Fire Code¶

    The model code that governs the use and maintenance of buildings, including exit lighting, extinguishers, hazardous storage, and alarm testing. It applies after construction.

    Example: A fire inspector checks for blocked exits in a restaurant.

    See also: Fire Protection

    Fire Damage¶

    The harm to a structure and its contents caused by flames, heat, and smoke, including charring, weakening, and water damage from suppression. Severity affects reuse.

    Example: Structural steel keeps only about half of its room-temperature strength at about 1,100 °F.

    Reference: AISC Engineering FAQ 11.2, Steel Exposed to Fire

    See also: Fire Resistance, Fire Protection

    Fire Protection¶

    The combination of active and passive measures that detect fire, limit its spread, and protect people and property. Active ones include sprinklers; passive ones include rated walls.

    See also: Sprinkler Systems, Fire Alarm Systems

    Fire Resistance¶

    The ability of a material or assembly to keep its strength, contain flames, and limit heat passage when exposed to fire. It is measured in standardized fire tests.

    Example: A wall with two layers of Type X gypsum board can resist fire for one hour.

    See also: Fire-Resistance Ratings

    Fire Separations¶

    Rated walls, floors, and barriers that divide a building into compartments to slow fire spread. Openings in them use rated doors and sealed penetrations.

    Example: A one-hour wall separates a garage from living space.

    See also: Fire-Resistance Ratings, Life Safety

    Fire-Resistance Ratings¶

    The time, in hours, that a building element can withstand a standard fire test while carrying load and containing the fire. Typical ratings are 1, 2, or 3 hours.

    Example: A 2-hour wall remains intact for two hours in the test furnace.

    See also: Fire Resistance, Fire Separations

    Flashing¶

    Thin, waterproof material, such as metal or membrane, installed at joints and openings to direct water out and away from a wall or roof. It is lapped so water flows over each layer.

    Example: Metal flashing above a window sends rain over the frame.

    See also: Water Control Layer, Water Intrusion

    Floor Systems¶

    The assemblies of joists, beams, subfloor, and supports that form the horizontal surfaces of a building. They carry live loads and resist vibration.

    Example: I-joists, plywood subfloor, and a bearing beam form a typical house floor.

    See also: Joists, Girders

    Footing Reinforcement¶

    The steel bars placed in a footing to resist tension and cracking caused by bending and soil movement. Placement is typically near the bottom, with concrete cover around the bars.

    Example: Two continuous #4 bars run lengthwise in a footing under a tall wall.

    See also: Reinforcing Steel, Continuous Footings

    Forces¶

    Pushes or pulls that act on an object and can change its motion or shape. In buildings they are measured in pounds or kips and include weight, wind, and soil pressure.

    Example: The weight of a roof pushes down on the walls that support it.

    See also: Stress, Equilibrium

    Formwork¶

    The temporary molds that shape and support fresh concrete until it hardens enough to carry itself. They are made of wood, steel, aluminum, or plastic.

    Example: Plywood panels braced with ties form a basement wall.

    See also: Cast-in-Place Concrete

    Foundation Drainage¶

    The system of perforated pipe, gravel, and drainage boards that collects groundwater near footings and carries it away. It reduces the pressure of water against walls.

    Example: A perforated pipe wrapped in fabric sits beside the footing and drains to a sump or daylight.

    See also: Groundwater, Foundation Waterproofing

    Foundation Systems¶

    The structural elements that transfer the weight of a building into the ground. They include footings, walls, slabs, piles, and piers matched to site soils.

    See also: Shallow Foundations, Deep Foundations

    Foundation Walls¶

    Walls below or near grade that support the building above and resist soil pressure from the sides. They are typically poured concrete or concrete block.

    Example: An 8 in. poured concrete wall forms the sides of a basement.

    See also: Basements, Retaining Walls

    Foundation Waterproofing¶

    The membranes, coatings, and sheets applied to the outside of below-grade walls and slabs to stop liquid water entering. It works with drainage to keep interiors dry.

    Example: A rubberized asphalt coating is rolled onto the outside of a basement wall.

    See also: Foundation Drainage

    Freeze-Thaw Damage¶

    The cracking, flaking, and spalling of porous materials when absorbed water freezes, expands, and thaws repeatedly. It is a major concern in Minnesota.

    Example: The surface of a concrete step peels after winters of salt and ice.

    See also: Concrete Mix Design, Porosity

    Frost Depth¶

    The depth to which soil freezes in winter at a given location. Minnesota rules set the minimum footing depth at 42 in. in most of the state and 60 in. in the northern counties.

    Example: A footing in Duluth must reach 60 in. below grade, while one in Minneapolis may stop at 42 in.

    Reference: Minnesota Rules 1303.1600 Footing Depth for Frost Protection (UpCodes)

    See also: Frost Heave

    Frost Heave¶

    The upward movement of soil and structures caused by freezing water in the ground forming ice lenses. It can lift and crack footings, slabs, and driveways.

    Example: An unheated porch slab on shallow footings rises and cracks after a cold winter.

    See also: Frost Depth, Frost-Protected Foundations

    Frost-Protected Foundations¶

    Shallow foundations that rely on insulation placed around the perimeter to keep soil beneath the footing from freezing. They allow footings above the normal frost depth.

    Example: Horizontal wing insulation extends outward from a slab-on-grade edge in a cold climate.

    See also: Frost Depth, Slab-on-Grade

    Furnace¶

    A fuel-burning or electric appliance that heats air and distributes it through ducts. Efficiency is rated by annual fuel utilization efficiency (AFUE).

    Example: A 96% AFUE gas furnace turns nearly all its fuel energy into heat.

    See also: Ductwork, Heating Systems

    General Contractor¶

    The firm that holds the main construction contract and directs the entire building process. It hires subcontractors, schedules work, orders materials, and answers to the owner for cost and quality.

    See also: Subcontractor, Project Owner

    Generators¶

    Machines that convert engine or turbine energy into electricity, used as an emergency or standby source. They run on diesel, natural gas, or propane.

    Example: A diesel unit outside a building starts when the grid goes down.

    See also: Emergency Power

    Girders¶

    Large primary beams that support smaller beams or joists and carry load to columns or walls. They are usually the heaviest horizontal members in a floor frame.

    Example: A built-up wood girder supports the floor joists across a basement.

    See also: Beams, Joists

    Glazing¶

    The transparent or translucent panes, usually glass, installed in windows, doors, and curtain walls. Modern units use two or three layers with sealed, gas-filled spaces between them.

    Example: Double glazing with argon gas between panes is common in Minnesota houses.

    See also: Low-E Coatings, Windows

    Glulam¶

    A structural member built by gluing layers of dimension lumber together with the grain running along the length. Large curved or straight members can be formed this way.

    Example: A curved glulam arch spans a church or arena.

    See also: Engineered Wood Products, Mass Timber

    Grading¶

    The shaping of the ground surface to desired slopes and elevations, usually to direct water away from buildings. It involves cutting, filling, and compacting soil.

    Example: Soil around a house slopes down at least 6 in. over the first 10 ft to send rain away from the foundation.

    Reference: IRC R401.3 Drainage (ICC)

    See also: Site Drainage

    Gravity Load System¶

    The set of members that carry vertical loads, such as floors, beams, columns, and bearing walls, down to the foundation. It resists weight rather than sideways forces.

    Contrast with: Lateral Load System

    Green Building Rating Systems¶

    Programs that score buildings on sustainability criteria and award certification levels. They include LEED, Passive House, and Minnesota's B3 guidelines.

    Example: A project selects a rating system to guide its design goals.

    Reference: Minnesota Sustainable Buildings (B3 Guidelines), Minnesota Department of Administration

    See also: LEED Certification

    Green Roofs¶

    Roof assemblies covered with a growing medium and plants over a waterproof membrane. They manage stormwater, insulate, and reduce heat absorbed.

    Example: A downtown building has a sedum-planted roof above its waterproofing.

    See also: Stormwater Management, Cool Roofs

    Grounding and Bonding¶

    The connection of electrical systems and metal parts to the earth and to each other to limit shock, voltage surges, and fire risk. A low-resistance path lets breakers trip during a fault.

    Example: A copper rod driven into the earth connects to the main panel's ground bar.

    See also: National Electrical Code

    Groundwater¶

    The water that fills the spaces in soil and rock below the surface. Its level changes with season and weather and affects excavations and basements.

    Example: A high spring water table can push water into an unprotected basement floor.

    See also: Foundation Drainage

    Grout¶

    A fluid mixture of cement, sand or fine gravel, and water poured into masonry cores or gaps to bond reinforcing steel and strengthen walls. It is more fluid than mortar.

    Example: A mason fills selected block cores containing rebar with grout.

    See also: Reinforced Masonry, Mortar

    Gypsum Board¶

    A panel with a gypsum core between paper faces, used to cover walls and ceilings. Also called drywall, it provides a smooth surface and a measure of fire resistance.

    Example: 5/8 in. Type X board gives one-hour fire resistance in rated walls.

    Reference: Gypsum Association: What Is a Type X Gypsum Panel?

    See also: Interior Finishes, Fire Resistance

    Headers¶

    Horizontal framing members placed over openings, such as windows and doors, to carry loads around the opening. They rest on shorter studs at each side.

    Example: Two 2x10 boards with a spacer form a header over a 4 ft window.

    See also: Wall Studs

    Heat¶

    The form of energy that moves from a warmer body to a cooler one because of a temperature difference. In US building practice it is measured in British thermal units (Btu).

    Example: A furnace supplies heat to replace what escapes through the walls on a January night.

    See also: Heat Transfer

    Heat Pump¶

    A device that moves thermal energy from a cooler place to a warmer one using refrigerant, providing heating and cooling. It delivers more energy than the electricity it uses.

    Example: A cold-climate air-source unit heats a home at below 0 °F outdoors.

    See also: Heating Systems, Cooling Systems

    Heat Recovery Ventilator¶

    A ventilation device that moves air in and out while transferring thermal energy from the outgoing stream to the incoming one. It cuts the cost of heating fresh air.

    Example: In January, a unit preheats 0 °F supply air to near 60 °F using exhaust warmth.

    See also: Mechanical Ventilation

    Heat Transfer¶

    The movement of thermal energy from one place to another by conduction, convection, or radiation. It always flows from higher to lower temperature.

    Controlling this movement is the core of enclosure design in cold climates.

    See also: Conduction, Convection, Radiation

    Heating and Cooling Loads¶

    The rates of heat energy, in Btu per hour, that a building gains or loses at design conditions. Equipment is sized to meet these rates.

    Example: A house in Minneapolis may have a design heating load of 35,000 Btu/h.

    See also: HVAC Systems

    Heating Systems¶

    Equipment that adds warmth to a building, such as furnaces, boilers, heat pumps, and electric resistance units. They burn fuel or use electricity to supply thermal energy.

    Example: A natural gas furnace warms a Minnesota house.

    See also: Furnace, Boiler, Heat Pump

    High-Performance Buildings¶

    Buildings designed and built to perform far above code minimums in energy, comfort, durability, and health. They rely on careful enclosure and integrated systems.

    Example: A school with an airtight envelope and heat recovery ventilation uses half the energy of a typical one.

    See also: Passive House, Net-Zero Energy

    Humidity¶

    The amount of water vapor present in air. It is commonly reported as grains or pounds of moisture per pound of dry air, or as a percentage of what the air can hold.

    See also: Relative Humidity, Dew Point

    HVAC Systems¶

    The equipment and distribution networks that heat, cool, ventilate, and condition the air in buildings. The letters stand for heating, ventilation, and air conditioning.

    Example: A furnace, ductwork, and central air conditioner form a typical house system.

    See also: Heating Systems, Cooling Systems, Ventilation

    Ice Dams¶

    Ridges of ice that form at roof eaves when heat from the building melts snow, and the meltwater refreezes at the cold edge. They back water under shingles.

    Example: Icicles and a thick ice ridge at a gutter indicate a leaky, warm attic.

    See also: Attic Ventilation, Air Sealing

    Indoor Air Quality¶

    The condition of the air inside buildings with respect to contaminants, humidity, and temperature, which affects health and comfort. Sources include cooking, materials, and outdoor pollution.

    Example: Good bathroom exhaust and low-emitting paint keep indoor air healthy.

    See also: Ventilation, Volatile Organic Compounds

    Industrial Construction¶

    The building of facilities for manufacturing, processing, power generation, or heavy storage. These projects emphasize large open spans, heavy floor loads, and specialized utilities.

    Example: A food-processing plant with washdown floors and large refrigeration equipment is industrial work.

    Inspection and Testing¶

    The examination of work and the measurement of materials against the drawings, specifications, and codes. It includes visual checks, sampling, and laboratory or field tests.

    Example: A technician breaks concrete cylinders to confirm the mix reached its specified strength.

    See also: Code Inspections, Material Testing

    Institutional Construction¶

    The building of facilities that serve public or community purposes, such as schools, hospitals, government offices, and places of worship. Projects usually involve high occupant loads, strict life-safety requirements, and long expected service lives.

    Example: A new elementary school with fire alarms, sprinklers, and accessible entrances is institutional work.

    See also: Life Safety

    Insulation¶

    A material or assembly that resists heat flow and so reduces energy loss from a building. Common forms include batts, boards, loose-fill, and spray foam.

    Example: A 2x6 wall with R-21 batts holds in warmth during winter.

    See also: R-Value, Thermal Control Layer

    Integrated Project Delivery¶

    A delivery approach in which the owner, designers, and builders sign one agreement and share risk and reward from the start. Early collaboration aims to reduce waste and conflicts.

    See also: Interdisciplinary Coordination

    Interdisciplinary Coordination¶

    The process of aligning the work of architects, engineers, and trades so that systems fit together without conflict. Regular reviews of overlapping drawings catch clashes early.

    Example: The plumber and structural engineer agree on where pipes may pass through joists.

    See also: Building Information Modeling

    Interior Finishes¶

    The surface materials applied to interior walls, floors, and ceilings, such as paint, tile, flooring, and trim. They affect appearance, cleanliness, acoustics, and fire spread.

    Example: Finish codes limit the flame spread of materials in exit corridors.

    See also: Gypsum Board

    International Building Code¶

    The model code, published by the International Code Council, that sets requirements for most buildings except small houses. It covers structure, fire safety, egress, and accessibility.

    Example: A four-story apartment building follows this code.

    See also: Model Codes, Residential Code

    Joists¶

    Closely spaced, repetitive horizontal members that support floors, ceilings, or roof decks. They are typically spaced 12 to 24 in. apart.

    Example: 2x10 floor joists at 16 in. spacing carry the subfloor.

    See also: Girders, Floor Systems

    Lateral Load System¶

    The set of members that resist horizontal forces from wind or earthquakes and carry them to the foundation. Examples include shear walls, braced frames, and moment frames.

    Contrast with: Gravity Load System

    LEED Certification¶

    A third-party recognition from the U.S. Green Building Council that a project met points-based criteria for sustainability. Levels are Certified, Silver, Gold, and Platinum.

    Example: A library earns Gold by meeting energy, water, and materials goals.

    See also: Green Building Rating Systems

    Life Safety¶

    The set of building features and code requirements aimed at protecting occupants from fire, smoke, and other emergencies. It includes exits, alarms, sprinklers, and emergency lighting.

    See also: Means of Egress, Fire Protection

    Life-Cycle Assessment¶

    A method that totals the environmental impacts of a product or building across its life, from raw material extraction through use and disposal. It permits fair comparisons.

    Example: An assessment compares a concrete and a wood floor over 60 years.

    See also: Product Declarations (EPD), Embodied Carbon

    Life-Cycle Cost¶

    The total cost of owning a building or system over its life, including purchase, energy, maintenance, and replacement. Comparison may favor higher first costs.

    Example: A more efficient boiler costs more at first but less over twenty years.

    See also: Service Life, Building Maintenance

    Light-Gauge Steel Framing¶

    Framing made of thin cold-formed steel studs, tracks, and joists, used for walls and floors. It is noncombustible and dimensionally stable.

    Example: Metal studs frame interior partitions in an office building.

    See also: Thermal Bridging

    Lighting Controls¶

    The switches, dimmers, sensors, and timers that turn lights on, off, or down to match need. They reduce energy use.

    Example: An occupancy sensor turns off the lights in an empty restroom.

    See also: Lighting Systems, Energy Code Compliance

    Lighting Systems¶

    The fixtures, lamps, and controls that give illumination to a building's spaces for tasks and safety. Modern systems use LEDs for high efficiency.

    Example: Recessed LED fixtures light a classroom to a set level.

    See also: Lighting Controls, Daylighting

    Live Loads¶

    The changing weights from occupants, furniture, and movable items that act on a building. Codes set minimum values by use, such as 40 psf for most living areas of homes and 30 psf for sleeping rooms.

    Example: People gathering in a classroom add live load to the floor.

    Reference: IBC 2018 Chapter 16, Minnesota edition (UpCodes)

    Contrast with: Dead Loads

    Load Combinations¶

    The prescribed groupings of different load types, each factored, that designers check to find the worst case a structure could face. They account for the chance that loads occur together.

    Example: One combination adds dead load and snow load, while another adds dead load and wind load.

    See also: Dead Loads, Live Loads

    Load Path¶

    The continuous route that a force follows from where it is applied through members and connections down to the soil. Any break in the route can lead to failure.

    Example: Roof snow load travels through rafters, a top plate, studs, a floor system, and a footing to the ground.

    See also: Structural Connections

    Local Materials¶

    Products extracted and manufactured near the building site, which reduce transport emissions and support regional economies. Common benchmarks are 100 miles in LEED v4 and 500 miles in older LEED versions.

    Example: Minnesota granite and regionally milled lumber qualify.

    Reference: GBRI: LEED v4 regional materials criteria, 100 miles vs 500 miles

    See also: Sustainable Materials

    Low-E Coatings¶

    Thin, nearly invisible metallic layers on glass that reflect long-wave heat while letting visible light through. They reduce radiant heat loss in winter and heat gain in summer.

    Example: A low-emissivity layer on the inner pane keeps warmth in the room.

    See also: Glazing, Solar Heat Gain Coefficient

    Low-Slope Roofs¶

    Roofs with a slope of 3:12 or less, covered by continuous membranes and drained to interior or edge drains. They are common on commercial buildings.

    Example: A flat-looking store roof covered by a single-ply membrane is low-slope.

    Reference: Archtoolbox: Steep-Sloped Roofing Systems (NRCA 3:12 dividing line)

    Contrast with: Steep-Slope Roofs

    Low-Voltage Systems¶

    Wiring and equipment operating at less than 50 volts, such as alarm, thermostat, security, and communication circuits. They carry signals or small amounts of power.

    Example: A doorbell and thermostat run on low-voltage wire.

    Reference: NEC Article 720, Circuits and Equipment Operating at Less Than 50 Volts (UpCodes)

    See also: Data and Communications

    Lumber¶

    Wood sawn from logs into boards and dimensional members of standard sizes and grades. Nominal sizes are larger than actual finished sizes.

    Example: A 2x4 stud measures actually 1.5 in. by 3.5 in.

    Reference: NIST Voluntary Product Standard PS 20, American Softwood Lumber Standard

    See also: Wood Moisture Content

    Masonry¶

    Construction using individual units, such as brick, block, or stone, bonded with mortar. It is durable, fire-resistant, and strong in compression.

    Example: A brick school wall is built unit by unit with mortar joints.

    See also: Brick, Mortar

    Mass Timber¶

    A family of large, engineered wood products, such as cross-laminated timber and glulam, used to build floors, walls, and frames of mid-rise buildings. It offers fire resistance through charring.

    Example: A seven-story office in Minneapolis uses a mass timber frame.

    See also: Cross-Laminated Timber, Glulam

    Mat Foundations¶

    Thick, continuous concrete slabs that extend under an entire building and spread its load over a large soil area. They suit weak soils or heavy loads.

    Example: A tall building on soft clay can rest on a mat 4 ft thick.

    Contrast with: Spread Footings

    Material Health¶

    The degree to which building products are free from chemicals that harm occupants, workers, or ecosystems. Ingredient disclosure helps designers choose safer options.

    Example: A designer avoids products containing halogenated flame retardants.

    See also: Volatile Organic Compounds, Indoor Air Quality

    Material Properties¶

    The measurable characteristics of a substance, such as strength, density, stiffness, conductivity, and permeability, that describe how it behaves under loads and conditions. Designers compare these values when choosing products.

    See also: Strength, Material Selection

    Material Selection¶

    The process of choosing products for a building by weighing performance, cost, availability, appearance, durability, and environmental impact. Informed choices reduce risk and life-cycle expense.

    Example: A designer chooses closed-cell foam below grade because it resists water absorption.

    See also: Material Properties

    Material Testing¶

    The measurement of a material's properties through standardized laboratory or field procedures. Results confirm that a product meets project requirements.

    Example: A lab pulls steel samples until they break to confirm tensile strength.

    See also: ASTM Standards

    Means of Egress¶

    The continuous, unobstructed path from any point in a building to a public way, made up of an exit access, an exit, and an exit discharge. It enables safe escape.

    Example: A room door, corridor, stair, and exterior door form a path.

    See also: Travel Distance, Life Safety

    Mechanical Code¶

    The code that sets rules for the installation of heating, cooling, ventilation, and exhaust equipment, including ducts, venting, and combustion air.

    Example: The code requires a furnace flue to vent exhaust gases to the outdoors.

    See also: HVAC Systems

    Mechanical Engineer¶

    A licensed professional who designs heating, cooling, ventilation, plumbing, and fire protection systems. The engineer calculates loads and selects equipment to keep spaces comfortable and safe.

    See also: HVAC Systems, Plumbing Systems

    Mechanical Ventilation¶

    The use of fans and ducts to bring outdoor air into a building and exhaust stale air. It gives controlled airflow in tight buildings.

    Example: A continuously running exhaust fan meets the Minnesota whole-house ventilation requirement.

    See also: Heat Recovery Ventilator, Ventilation

    Metal Panels¶

    Factory-formed sheets of steel, aluminum, or other metals that are attached to a wall as cladding or to a roof. They are lightweight and low in maintenance.

    Example: A school entry wall is clad in corrugated aluminum panels.

    See also: Cladding, Metal Roofing

    Metal Roofing¶

    Roofing made of steel, aluminum, or copper panels with raised seams or exposed fasteners. It sheds snow well and has a long service life.

    Example: Standing-seam steel panels are common on Minnesota barns and houses.

    See also: Steep-Slope Roofs, Ice Dams

    Mineral Wool Insulation¶

    Insulation made from spun molten rock or slag, available in batts and boards. It is water-repellent, fire-resistant, and provides about R-3.7 to R-4.3 per inch.

    Example: Mineral wool boards provide exterior insulation behind brick veneer.

    Reference: U.S. DOE Energy Renovations: Insulation guide

    See also: Insulation, Fire Resistance

    Minnesota Energy Code¶

    The part of the state building code that sets minimum energy efficiency for new buildings, including insulation, air tightness, windows, and equipment. Requirements reflect cold-climate conditions.

    Example: The code sets minimum wall and attic R-values for Minnesota homes.

    See also: Energy Code Compliance

    Minnesota State Building Code¶

    The set of statewide construction rules, administered by the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry, that apply to buildings throughout the state. It includes adopted model codes with state amendments.

    See also: Minnesota Energy Code, Building Official

    Model Codes¶

    The code documents written by standards organizations, such as the International Code Council, that states and cities adopt and amend. They give a common base for local law.

    Example: The International Building Code is a model code adopted with changes in Minnesota.

    See also: International Building Code, Building Codes

    Modulus of Elasticity¶

    The ratio of stress to strain in the elastic range of a material, showing how stiff it is. Steel has a value near 29,000,000 psi.

    Example: Wood has a much lower modulus, around 1,500,000 psi, so a wood beam bends more than a steel beam of equal size.

    Reference: AISC 360-16 Specification for Structural Steel Buildings; USDA Wood Handbook, Chapter 5

    See also: Stress, Strain

    Moisture¶

    Water in liquid, vapor, or solid form within or on building materials and in the air. It affects strength, thermal performance, and durability.

    Example: Rain on siding, vapor in indoor air, and ice in a gutter are all forms of moisture in a building.

    See also: Humidity

    Mold¶

    A fungal growth that develops on damp surfaces, appearing as fuzzy or discolored patches. It can harm indoor air and finish materials.

    Example: Black patches appear on a bathroom ceiling that is rarely ventilated.

    See also: Indoor Air Quality, Water Intrusion

    Moment Frames¶

    Structural frames in which beams and columns are rigidly connected, so the joints resist rotation and carry lateral loads through bending. They allow open bays without diagonal braces.

    Example: A multistory steel frame with welded beam-to-column joints forms a moment frame.

    See also: Lateral Load System, Structural Connections

    Mortar¶

    A paste of cement, lime, sand, and water used to bed and join masonry units. It bonds the units and seals the gaps between them.

    Example: A mason spreads a layer about 3/8 in. thick between rows of brick.

    See also: Masonry, Grout

    National Electrical Code¶

    The model standard, published by the National Fire Protection Association as NFPA 70, for safe installation of electrical wiring and equipment. Minnesota adopts it by reference in Minnesota Rules chapter 1315.

    Example: The code sets the minimum number of receptacles in a kitchen.

    Reference: Minnesota Rules Chapter 1315, Electrical Code (Revisor of Statutes)

    See also: Grounding and Bonding, Model Codes

    Net-Zero Energy¶

    A building that produces as much renewable energy on site over a year as it uses. It pairs very low demand with solar or other generation.

    Example: A house with a large roof array and high insulation yields a zero annual utility balance.

    See also: Photovoltaic Systems, Energy Efficiency

    Occupancy and Closeout¶

    The final project phase in which inspections are passed, documents and warranties are delivered, and the owner takes possession of the building. It ends with the final payment to the contractor.

    See also: Certificate of Occupancy

    Occupancy Classification¶

    The code category assigned to a building or space based on its use, such as assembly, business, educational, or residential. It determines many fire and egress rules.

    Example: A school is group E, and an office is group B.

    See also: Building Types, Construction Types

    One-Line Diagram¶

    A simplified drawing that shows the major parts of an electrical system and how power flows between them, using single lines and symbols. It shows the whole distribution at a glance.

    Example: A diagram shows the utility, transformer, main switch, and panels as boxes joined by lines.

    See also: Electrical Plans

    Operational Carbon¶

    The greenhouse gas emissions released by energy a building uses during its occupied life for heating, cooling, lighting, and equipment. It depends on efficiency and the energy source.

    Example: A gas-heated house in Minnesota emits carbon every winter.

    Contrast with: Embodied Carbon

    Panelboards¶

    Wall-mounted cabinets that hold circuit breakers or fuses and divide electrical power into branch circuits. They provide a safe point for disconnecting.

    Example: A 200 amp panel in a basement feeds all the circuits in a house.

    See also: Circuit Breakers, Branch Circuits

    Passive House¶

    A rigorous voluntary building standard that achieves very low heating and cooling demand through high insulation, airtightness, quality windows, and heat recovery ventilation. Limits cover energy use and air leakage.

    Example: A certified home limits air leakage to 0.6 ACH at 50 Pascals.

    Reference: Passive House Accelerator: five principles of Passive House design (airtightness 0.6 ACH50)

    See also: High-Performance Buildings

    Passive Solar Design¶

    An approach that uses building orientation, glazing, and thermal mass to collect and store winter sun, and to shade summer sun, without mechanical equipment.

    Example: A house with large south windows and a concrete floor stays warm on sunny winter days.

    See also: Thermal Mass, Solar Heat Gain Coefficient

    Permeability¶

    The ability of a material to allow a fluid, such as water vapor or liquid water, to pass through it. In buildings it is often rated in perms for vapor flow.

    Example: Polyethylene film has very low vapor permeability, while unpainted gypsum board is high.

    See also: Vapor Diffusion

    Photovoltaic Systems¶

    Arrays of solar panels, inverters, and wiring that convert sunlight directly into electricity. They can power a building or feed the grid.

    Example: A 7 kW roof array produces much of a home's yearly electricity.

    See also: Net-Zero Energy, Direct Current

    Piles¶

    Long, slender columns of steel, concrete, or timber driven or placed into the ground to carry building loads to deeper strata. They carry load by end bearing, friction, or both.

    Example: Treated timber piles support a dock in weak lakeshore soil.

    See also: Deep Foundations

    Plan Review¶

    The examination of construction documents by the building department to check compliance with codes before a permit is issued. Reviewers mark corrections to be resolved.

    Example: A reviewer notes that a stair is narrower than the code minimum.

    See also: Building Permits

    Plans¶

    Drawings that show a horizontal slice through a building or site as seen from above. They locate walls, doors, rooms, equipment, and fixtures.

    Example: A floor plan shows where each wall sits and how wide each doorway is.

    See also: Elevations, Sections

    Platform Framing¶

    A wood framing method in which each story is built as a separate platform, with walls set on the floor deck below. It is the most common method for houses.

    Example: Crews frame a first-floor deck, stand the walls, and then build the second-floor deck on top.

    See also: Wall Studs, Floor Systems

    Plumbing Code¶

    The adopted rule set that establishes minimum requirements for water supply, drainage, venting, and fixtures in buildings. It protects public health from contaminated water and sewer gas.

    Example: The code gives the minimum slope for a drain pipe.

    See also: Plumbing Systems

    Plumbing Fixtures¶

    The devices that receive water and discharge waste, such as sinks, toilets, tubs, and showers. Each connects to supply and drain piping.

    Example: A lavatory has a trap that holds water to block sewer gas.

    See also: Water Supply, Drain-Waste-Vent System

    Plumbing Systems¶

    The pipes, fittings, fixtures, and equipment that supply potable water and carry wastewater from a building. They protect public health.

    See also: Water Supply, Drain-Waste-Vent System

    Plywood and OSB¶

    Structural sheet panels made from wood veneers (plywood) or compressed strands (oriented strand board) bonded with adhesive. They serve as sheathing, subfloor, and roof deck.

    Example: A 7/16 in. OSB sheet is nailed to wall studs as sheathing.

    See also: Wall Sheathing, Diaphragms

    Porosity¶

    The proportion of a material's volume that is empty space, in the form of tiny voids or channels. Greater void content generally increases water absorption and reduces strength.

    Example: Common brick is more porous than dense granite.

    See also: Capillary Action

    Precast Concrete¶

    Concrete members cast and cured in a factory, then transported and erected on site. Factory control improves quality and speeds construction.

    Example: Hollow-core planks are lifted by crane onto bearing walls to form a floor.

    Contrast with: Cast-in-Place Concrete

    Prestressed Concrete¶

    Concrete in which high-strength steel strands are tensioned so the concrete is held in compression before loads are applied. It permits longer spans and thinner members.

    Example: Precast double-tee floor planks in a parking ramp are prestressed.

    See also: Precast Concrete

    Preventive Maintenance¶

    Scheduled inspection and servicing performed before problems occur, aimed at avoiding breakdowns and costly repairs. Written records help track equipment condition and recurring issues.

    Example: Replacing furnace filters each season and testing alarms yearly.

    See also: Building Maintenance

    Product Declarations (EPD)¶

    Standardized, third-party-verified reports that disclose the life-cycle environmental impacts of a specific product. Designers use them to compare similar products fairly.

    Example: A concrete supplier publishes an EPD listing the carbon per cubic yard.

    See also: Life-Cycle Assessment

    Programming¶

    The early project phase in which the owner's needs, space requirements, budget, and goals are gathered and written down. It sets the target that later design work aims to meet.

    Example: A program for a clinic might list twelve exam rooms, a waiting area, and a budget of $6 million.

    See also: Schematic Design

    Project Owner¶

    The person or organization that commissions, pays for, and ultimately holds title to a building project. The owner sets goals and budget and makes major decisions.

    Example: A school district is the owner when it hires a team to build a new gymnasium.

    See also: Architect, General Contractor

    Project Phases¶

    The ordered stages through which a building project advances, such as programming, design, bidding, construction, and closeout. Each stage ends with decisions that shape the next one.

    See also: Programming, Schematic Design, Design Development

    Psychrometric Chart¶

    A graph that plots the properties of moist air, including temperature, humidity, and dew point, on a single set of axes. Designers use it to predict condensation and size cooling and dehumidification.

    Example: Finding 70 °F and 40% relative humidity on the chart shows a dew point near 45 °F.

    See also: Dew Point, Relative Humidity

    R-Value¶

    A rating of resistance to conductive thermal flow for a material or assembly, expressed in square-foot degrees Fahrenheit hours per Btu (ft²·°F·h/Btu). Higher numbers indicate better insulating performance.

    Example: A 3.5 in. fiberglass batt is rated about R-13, but the framing in a wall built with it lowers the wall's effective R-value, so the whole assembly must be calculated rather than read from the label.

    See also: U-Value, Insulation

    Radiant Heating¶

    A heating method in which warm surfaces, such as floors, walls, or panels, deliver heat mostly by radiation. It gives even warmth with little air movement.

    Example: Hot-water tubing in a concrete slab warms a garage floor.

    See also: Boiler, Radiation

    Radiation¶

    The transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves, which needs no material between source and receiver. Surfaces emit and absorb it depending on temperature and finish.

    Example: Warmth from sunlight through a south window or from a hot radiator panel reaches skin without heating the air first.

    See also: Low-E Coatings

    Radon Mitigation¶

    The set of measures, such as sub-slab pipes and sealed penetrations, that reduce the entry of radon gas from soil into a building. Minnesota has high radon potential in many counties.

    Example: A vent pipe through the roof draws gas from beneath the basement slab.

    See also: Indoor Air Quality

    Rainscreen¶

    A wall system with a ventilated air gap behind the cladding that drains and dries water getting past the outer layer. The gap also limits direct pressure on the wall behind.

    Example: Wood furring strips behind siding create a drainage cavity.

    See also: Cladding, Water Control Layer

    Receptacles¶

    Wall outlets that connect plugs of portable devices to a branch circuit. Codes require tamper-resistant and ground-fault types in certain places.

    Example: A kitchen counter outlet has ground-fault protection.

    See also: Branch Circuits

    Recycled Content¶

    The share of a product made from reused materials, such as post-consumer waste or industrial byproducts. It reduces demand for raw resources.

    Example: Steel rebar often contains about 97% recycled steel.

    Reference: CRSI: Durable, Resilient, Sustainable

    See also: Sustainable Materials

    Referenced Standards¶

    The outside documents, such as ASTM, NFPA, or ANSI standards, that a code cites to define tests, products, or methods. They carry the same force as the code text.

    Example: The building code cites an ASTM test for fire resistance.

    See also: ASTM Standards

    Reinforced Concrete¶

    Concrete combined with embedded steel so the concrete carries compression and the steel carries tension. The two materials work together as one.

    Example: A concrete beam has steel bars near its bottom face where bending causes tension.

    See also: Reinforcing Steel, Prestressed Concrete

    Reinforced Masonry¶

    Masonry containing steel bars placed in grouted cores or joints to resist tension, bending, and lateral loads. It performs better under wind and seismic forces than unreinforced walls.

    Example: Vertical bars grouted into a block wall help it resist wind pressure.

    See also: Grout, Reinforcing Steel

    Reinforcing Steel¶

    Steel bars, wires, or mesh embedded in concrete or masonry to carry tension and control cracking. Deformed bars have ridges that grip the surrounding material.

    Example: A #5 bar is five-eighths of an inch in diameter.

    See also: Reinforced Concrete, Tensile Strength

    Relative Humidity¶

    The ratio, expressed as a percentage, of the water vapor in air to the most vapor the air could hold at the same temperature. Warm air can hold more vapor than cold air.

    Example: Indoor air at 70 °F and 30% relative humidity feels dry, while 60% feels damp.

    See also: Dew Point, Psychrometric Chart

    Renewable Materials¶

    Building materials from sources that regrow or replenish within a human timescale, such as wood, bamboo, and straw. They can store carbon while growing.

    Example: Sustainably harvested lumber is a renewable material.

    See also: Mass Timber, Carbon Sequestration

    Renovation and Retrofit¶

    The alteration or upgrading of an existing building to improve its function, safety, or efficiency, such as adding insulation or replacing mechanical equipment. It keeps the main structure.

    Example: A 1960s house receives new attic insulation and an air-sealing upgrade.

    See also: Adaptive Reuse, Building Assessment

    Requests for Information¶

    Formal written questions from a contractor to the design team seeking clarification of unclear, conflicting, or missing information in the construction documents. Answers are recorded for the project.

    Example: A contractor asks which of two conflicting wall details governs at a window.

    Residential Code¶

    The model code that covers one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses up to three stories. It provides prescriptive rules for framing, foundations, and systems.

    Example: A single-family house is designed under this code, not the commercial building code.

    See also: International Building Code

    Residential Construction¶

    The building of dwellings in which people live, ranging from single-family houses to large apartment buildings. In the United States, the work is guided by the residential code for small buildings and by the commercial code for larger ones.

    Example: A contractor framing a three-bedroom house in Plymouth is performing residential work.

    Contrast with: Commercial Construction

    Resistance¶

    The opposition to the flow of electric current in a material, measured in ohms. It turns electrical energy into heat.

    Example: A thin wire has more of it than a thick wire of the same length.

    See also: Voltage, Current

    Retaining Walls¶

    Walls built to hold back soil where the ground level changes abruptly. They resist lateral earth pressure and water pressure.

    Example: A concrete block wall holds up a sloped yard beside a driveway.

    See also: Foundation Walls

    Reuse and Salvage¶

    The recovery of building components for use again, either in place or in another project, instead of throwing them away. It conserves resources and embodied energy.

    Example: Old-growth beams from a demolished warehouse become flooring.

    See also: Deconstruction, Construction Waste

    Rigid Foam Insulation¶

    Insulation boards made of expanded polystyrene, extruded polystyrene, or polyisocyanurate, with R-values of about 3.6 to 6.5 per inch. They are used on walls, roofs, and below slabs.

    Example: Two inches of polyiso on a wall adds roughly R-12.

    Reference: Insulation R-value comparison by material (insulationrvalues.com)

    See also: Continuous Insulation, Insulation

    Roof Assemblies¶

    The complete set of layers that make up a roof, from structure to covering, including deck, insulation, underlayment, and the roofing surface. They shed water, resist wind, and limit heat flow.

    See also: Low-Slope Roofs, Steep-Slope Roofs

    Roof Drainage¶

    The gutters, downspouts, scuppers, and drains that collect water from a roof and carry it to the ground or a storm system. Adequate capacity prevents ponding and leaks.

    Example: Gutters send water to downspouts that discharge away from the foundation.

    See also: Stormwater Management

    Roof Framing¶

    The members that form the structure of a roof, such as rafters, trusses, ridge, and bracing. They carry snow and wind loads to the walls.

    Example: Prefabricated trusses at 24 in. spacing frame a gable roof.

    See also: Trusses, Snow Loads

    Roofing Membranes¶

    Continuous sheets of rubber, plastic, or modified bitumen laid over low-slope roof decks to keep water out. Seams are welded, glued, or taped.

    Example: A white single-ply sheet is rolled across a flat roof and seamed by hot air.

    See also: Low-Slope Roofs, Cool Roofs

    Rot and Decay¶

    The breakdown of wood by fungi that feed on its cellulose, requiring wood to stay wet, generally above 25 to 30% moisture content. It reduces strength and may go unseen inside walls.

    Example: A deck post in constant contact with wet soil rots at its base.

    Reference: USDA Wood Handbook, Chapter 14, Biodeterioration of Wood

    See also: Wood Moisture Content, Mold

    Scale and Dimensions¶

    The relationship between a drawing's size and the real object, together with the measured lengths written on the drawing. Scale lets a large building fit on a sheet, while dimensions state exact sizes.

    Example: At 1/4 in. = 1 ft, a 40 ft wall appears 10 in. long on the sheet.

    See also: Construction Drawings

    Schedules¶

    Tables in the construction documents that list repeated items, such as doors, windows, finishes, or equipment, with their sizes and properties. They organize information that would clutter the drawings.

    Example: A door schedule lists each door's number, width, fire rating, and hardware.

    Schematic Design¶

    The first design phase, in which the architect produces rough layouts, site plans, and massing studies that show the general form of a building. Sketches are tested against the program and budget.

    See also: Design Development, Programming

    Sealants¶

    Flexible, adhesive materials, such as silicone, polyurethane, or butyl, applied in joints to block water and air. They stretch and compress with building movement.

    Example: A bead of sealant around a window frame closes the gap against rain.

    See also: Air Sealing, Flashing

    Sections¶

    Drawings that show a vertical slice cut through a building or assembly, revealing the layers and heights inside. They explain how parts connect from foundation to roof.

    Example: A wall section shows the footing, foundation wall, framing, insulation, and cladding in one view.

    See also: Details

    Seismic Loads¶

    The horizontal and vertical forces induced in a structure by earthquake ground shaking. Their size depends on building weight and local seismic hazard.

    Example: Minnesota has low seismic hazard, so design values there are small compared with California.

    See also: Lateral Load System

    Service Entrance¶

    The point where utility power enters a building, including the conductors, meter, main disconnect, and overcurrent protection. It marks the start of the building's own wiring.

    Example: A meter and main panel on the outside wall of a house form its service entrance.

    See also: Panelboards, Utility Service

    Service Life¶

    The length of time that a building or component continues to perform its intended function before replacement. It varies by material and exposure.

    Example: Asphalt shingles last about 20 to 30 years, while a masonry wall may last over 100.

    See also: Durability, Life-Cycle Cost

    Settlement¶

    The downward movement of a building or part of it as soil under the foundation compresses. Uneven sinking can crack walls and jam doors.

    Example: A house on soft fill drops an inch more at one corner than the other.

    See also: Soil Bearing Capacity, Cracking

    Shallow Foundations¶

    Foundations that spread building loads to soil close to the surface, usually within a few feet. They suit sites with firm, near-surface soil.

    Example: A house on spread footings and a foundation wall rests on a shallow foundation.

    Contrast with: Deep Foundations

    Shear Strength¶

    The maximum sliding stress a material can bear before one part slips relative to the adjacent part. It matters at connections and near supports.

    Example: A bolt in a steel plate connection can fail by shearing across its cross-section.

    See also: Bolted Connections

    Shear Walls¶

    Walls designed to resist horizontal forces in their plane, usually from wind or earthquakes. They are typically sheathed with plywood, OSB, or made of concrete or masonry.

    Example: Plywood nailed to a wall frame stiffens it against sideways racking.

    See also: Lateral Load System, Diaphragms

    Shingles¶

    Small overlapping units of asphalt, wood, slate, or other material laid in rows on steep roofs to shed water. Each row covers the joints of the one below.

    Example: Asphalt shingles nailed in courses cover most American homes.

    See also: Steep-Slope Roofs

    Siding¶

    Exterior cladding made of boards or panels, such as wood, vinyl, fiber-cement, or metal, applied in overlapping rows. It sheds rain and gives a finished look.

    Example: Horizontal lap boards of fiber-cement cover a typical house wall.

    See also: Cladding

    Site Analysis¶

    The study of a site's soils, slopes, water, climate, utilities, access, and legal limits before design begins. It reveals opportunities and hazards for the project.

    Example: A survey shows that a lot slopes toward the planned house and has a high water table.

    See also: Soil Testing, Groundwater

    Site Drainage¶

    The arrangement of slopes, swales, drains, and pipes that moves surface and storm water away from buildings. Good arrangement protects foundations and landscaping.

    See also: Grading, Stormwater Management

    Site Preparation¶

    The work of clearing, stripping, and leveling a site and installing temporary controls so that construction can begin. It includes removing vegetation, topsoil, and obstructions.

    See also: Excavation, Grading

    Slab-on-Grade¶

    A foundation or floor consisting of a concrete slab poured directly on prepared ground, often thickened at the edges. It has no basement or crawl space below.

    Example: A garage floor is usually slab-on-grade.

    See also: Frost-Protected Foundations, Concrete Slabs

    Smoke Control¶

    Systems that limit smoke movement during a fire by pressurizing stairs or exhausting smoke from large spaces. They keep exit routes clear.

    Example: Fans pressurize a stairwell so smoke does not enter from the corridor.

    See also: Means of Egress, Life Safety

    Snow Loads¶

    The weight of accumulated snow and ice on a roof, expressed in pounds per square foot. Minnesota ground snow values are 50 psf in most of the state and 60 psf in the northern counties.

    Example: Minnesota's residential rules multiply ground snow load by 0.7, so a 50 psf ground load gives a 35 psf roof design load.

    Reference: Minnesota DLI ground snow load map, Minnesota Rules 1303.1700

    See also: Roof Framing

    Soil Bearing Capacity¶

    The maximum pressure that soil can safely carry from a foundation without excessive settlement or failure. It is expressed in pounds per square foot.

    Example: The building code assumes 1,500 psf for clay and silt when no soil test exists, which controls footing width.

    Reference: IBC Table 1806.2 Presumptive Load-Bearing Values (UpCodes)

    See also: Spread Footings, Settlement

    Soil Testing¶

    The sampling and analysis of ground materials to learn their type, strength, moisture, and chemistry. Borings and lab tests supply data for foundation design.

    Example: A geotechnical firm drills test holes and reports the soil layers and water level.

    See also: Soil Bearing Capacity

    Soil Types¶

    The categories of ground material, including gravel, sand, silt, clay, and organic soil, defined by particle size and behavior. Each type affects bearing, drainage, and frost behavior.

    Example: Clay drains slowly and can swell, while sand drains quickly.

    See also: Soil Bearing Capacity

    Solar Heat Gain Coefficient¶

    The fraction of solar radiation, from 0 to 1, that enters a building through a window as heat. A lower value blocks more solar energy.

    Example: A south window in a cold climate may use 0.45 to admit useful winter sun.

    See also: Low-E Coatings, Passive Solar Design

    Sound Transmission¶

    The passage of sound energy through walls, floors, and ceilings from one space to another. It is rated with measures such as an STC number for airborne noise.

    Example: A double-stud wall with insulation between apartments blocks more conversation than a single thin partition.

    Specifications¶

    The written descriptions of the materials, quality standards, and workmanship required for a project. They complement drawings by stating what the drawings cannot show.

    Example: A specification for concrete can state the compressive strength, air content, and slump required.

    See also: Construction Documents

    Spray Foam Insulation¶

    Insulation formed by spraying liquid chemicals that expand into foam on a surface. Closed-cell types give about R-6 per inch and also stop air flow.

    Example: Closed-cell foam sprayed on a rim joist seals leaks and insulates in one step.

    Reference: U.S. DOE Building America: Which Spray Foam Is Right For You?

    See also: Insulation, Air Sealing

    Spread Footings¶

    Enlarged concrete bases that distribute the load of a column or wall over a wider soil area. Their size depends on the load and the soil bearing capacity.

    Example: A column carrying 60,000 lb on soil rated at 1,500 psf needs 40 sq ft of bearing area, a square footing about 6.5 ft on a side.

    See also: Soil Bearing Capacity, Continuous Footings

    Sprinkler Systems¶

    Piping networks with heat-activated nozzles that discharge water on a fire. Each head opens only when the air near it is hot enough.

    Example: A sprinkler head with a glass bulb bursts at 155 °F and sprays the room.

    Reference: Archtoolbox: Types of Fire Sprinkler Heads

    See also: Fire Protection

    Stack Effect¶

    The upward movement of warm indoor air in a tall space, drawn out at the top while cold outdoor air enters at the bottom. It is driven by temperature differences and is strongest in cold weather.

    Example: In a Minnesota winter, cold air whistles in at basement doors while warm air escapes through the attic.

    See also: Air Leakage, Air Pressure Differences

    Steel Decking¶

    Corrugated steel sheets laid across beams to form a floor or roof surface, often topped with concrete. The ridges give stiffness over spans.

    Example: Composite steel deck with a 3 in. concrete topping forms an office floor.

    See also: Structural Steel, Diaphragms

    Steel Shapes¶

    The standard rolled cross-sections of structural steel, such as wide-flange (W), angle (L), channel (C), and tube (HSS). Each is designated by its type, depth, and weight per foot.

    Example: A W12x26 is a wide-flange beam about 12 in. deep that weighs 26 lb per foot.

    See also: Structural Steel

    Steep-Slope Roofs¶

    Roofs with a slope greater than 3:12 that shed water through overlapping units such as shingles, tiles, or metal panels. They are typical of houses.

    Example: A gabled house roof at 6:12 with asphalt shingles is steep-slope.

    Reference: Archtoolbox: Steep-Sloped Roofing Systems (NRCA 3:12 dividing line)

    Contrast with: Low-Slope Roofs

    Stiffness¶

    The resistance of a member or material to bending, stretching, or other deformation under load. A stiffer member moves less under the same force.

    Example: A deep steel beam deflects less than a shallow wood plank of the same span.

    See also: Modulus of Elasticity, Deflection

    Stone Masonry¶

    Wall construction made of natural stone units laid with or without mortar. It is very durable and often used for foundations, facades, and landscape walls.

    Example: Limestone blocks form the facade of an historic courthouse.

    See also: Masonry

    Stormwater Management¶

    The control of rain and snowmelt runoff through slopes, drains, ponds, and permeable surfaces to prevent flooding and pollution. Local rules limit runoff leaving a site.

    Example: A rain garden and underground storage basin catch parking lot runoff.

    See also: Site Drainage, Roof Drainage

    Strain¶

    The change in length of a material divided by its original length when it is loaded. It is a unitless ratio.

    Example: A 100 in. steel rod that stretches 0.01 in. has a strain of 0.0001.

    See also: Stress, Modulus of Elasticity

    Strength¶

    The largest stress a material can carry before it fails or deforms permanently. Different values apply to compression, tension, and shear.

    Example: Concrete may have a strength of 4,000 psi in compression.

    See also: Compressive Strength, Tensile Strength

    Stress¶

    The internal force per unit of area that develops inside a material when loads act on it. It is expressed in pounds per square inch (psi).

    Example: A column carrying 20,000 lb on a 4 in. by 4 in. cross-section has an average stress of 1,250 psi.

    See also: Strain, Strength

    Structural Connections¶

    The joints that attach structural members to each other and transfer forces between them. They use nails, bolts, welds, screws, or metal connectors.

    Example: A joist hanger carries a floor joist's load into a beam.

    See also: Bolted Connections, Welded Connections

    Structural Engineer¶

    A licensed professional who calculates loads and designs the foundation, frame, and connections that keep a building standing. The engineer sizes members such as beams, columns, and footings.

    Example: A structural engineer selects the steel beam size to carry a large opening in a bearing wall.

    See also: Structural System

    Structural Steel¶

    Steel rolled or fabricated into shapes, plates, and tubes that serve as beams, columns, and braces in buildings. It has high strength and ductility.

    Example: A wide-flange beam carries the roof of a gymnasium.

    See also: Steel Shapes, Bolted Connections

    Structural System¶

    The arrangement of connected elements that carries loads from the roof and floors to the foundation and ground. It includes frames, walls, floors, and connections.

    See also: Load Path, Gravity Load System

    Stucco¶

    A cement-based plaster applied in coats over a lath or backing to form a durable exterior surface. It is usually installed in three layers.

    Example: A stucco wall may be sprayed over metal lath with a drainage mat behind it.

    See also: Cladding, Cracking

    Subcontractor¶

    A specialty firm hired by a general contractor to perform a defined portion of the work, such as electrical, plumbing, roofing, or concrete. Such firms bring specialized tools and trained crews.

    Example: An electrical firm wires a building under a contract with the general contractor.

    See also: General Contractor

    Submittals¶

    The documents, product data sheets, shop drawings, and samples that a contractor sends to the design team for review before ordering or installing items. They confirm that proposed products match the specifications.

    Example: A steel fabricator sends shop drawings of beam connections for the engineer to check.

    See also: Construction Administration

    Sustainability¶

    The practice of meeting present needs while protecting the environment, economy, and community for the future. In buildings it addresses energy, water, materials, and health.

    See also: Sustainable Materials, Energy Efficiency

    Sustainable Materials¶

    Building products chosen for lower environmental impact over their life, considering source, manufacture, use, and disposal. They often have recycled or renewable content.

    Example: Recycled-content insulation and responsibly harvested wood qualify.

    See also: Embodied Carbon, Recycled Content

    Switchgear¶

    A group of switches, fuses, and breakers in an enclosure that controls, protects, and isolates electrical equipment in large buildings. It handles high currents.

    Example: A hospital has a switchgear lineup in a basement electrical room.

    See also: Circuit Breakers, Transformers

    Symbols and Abbreviations¶

    The standardized graphic marks and shortened words used on drawings to save space and keep meaning consistent. A legend on the drawing set explains each one.

    Example: "GWB" is a common abbreviation for gypsum wallboard, and a dashed line may mean hidden or overhead items.

    Tensile Strength¶

    The maximum pulling stress a material can bear before it breaks. Steel has high values, while concrete has very low ones, which is why concrete needs reinforcing.

    Example: A steel rebar can carry tension of 60,000 psi at yield, while plain concrete cracks at a few hundred psi.

    Contrast with: Compressive Strength

    Thermal Bridging¶

    The passage of thermal energy through a highly conductive element that bypasses the surrounding insulation. It lowers real assembly performance and can cause cold spots.

    Example: Steel studs in a wall conduct energy outward, reducing the effect of cavity insulation.

    See also: Continuous Insulation, Conduction

    Thermal Comfort¶

    The condition in which occupants feel neither too warm nor too cool, determined by air temperature, surface temperatures, humidity, air movement, clothing, and activity. It shapes design targets for heating and cooling.

    Example: A room at 70 °F can still feel chilly near a cold window because of radiant heat loss.

    See also: Psychrometric Chart

    Thermal Control Layer¶

    The layer or group of layers that resists heat flow through the enclosure, typically insulation. Continuity matters as much as the R-value.

    Example: Rigid foam on the exterior forms an unbroken layer that covers studs.

    See also: Insulation, Continuous Insulation

    Thermal Expansion¶

    The increase in size of a material when its temperature rises, and the matching shrinkage when it cools. It is described by a coefficient per degree.

    Example: A 100 ft steel beam grows about 0.8 in. when it warms 100 °F.

    See also: Expansion Joints

    Thermal Mass¶

    The ability of a heavy material to absorb, store, and later release thermal energy, which slows temperature swings. Concrete, masonry, and water have high capacity.

    Example: A concrete slab warmed by winter sun releases stored energy in the evening.

    See also: Passive Solar Design

    Thermal Resistance¶

    The measure of how strongly a material or assembly opposes the flow of thermal energy. A higher value means less energy passes through for a given temperature difference.

    See also: R-Value, U-Value

    Toughness¶

    The ability of a material to absorb energy and resist cracking under impact or sudden loading. It combines strength and the capacity to deform.

    Example: Steel stays tough in moderate cold, but some grades become brittle at very low temperatures.

    See also: Ductility

    Transformers¶

    Devices that raise or lower alternating-current voltage using coils and a magnetic core. They allow efficient transmission and safe use.

    Example: A pad-mounted transformer steps utility voltage down to 120/240 volts for a neighborhood.

    See also: Voltage

    Travel Distance¶

    The length along the path of travel from a point in a building to the nearest exit, limited by code. It depends on occupancy and sprinkler protection.

    Example: An office with sprinklers may allow a 300 ft limit.

    Reference: IBC Table 1017.2 summary (National Fire Sprinkler Association)

    See also: Means of Egress

    Tributary Area¶

    The portion of floor or roof surface whose load is assigned to a particular beam, column, or footing. Multiplying the area by the load per square foot gives the load on that member.

    Example: A column with a 20 ft by 20 ft tributary area under 50 psf carries 20,000 lb.

    See also: Load Path

    Trusses¶

    Assemblies of straight members arranged in triangles that carry loads over long spans with little material. Members are mainly in tension or compression.

    Example: Factory-built wood roof trusses span a 28 ft wide house.

    See also: Roof Framing

    U-Value¶

    The rate at which thermal energy passes through one square foot of an assembly for each degree Fahrenheit of temperature difference, equal to the reciprocal of its total R-value. Lower numbers indicate better performance.

    Example: A wall with total R-20 has a U-value of 0.05 Btu/(h·ft²·°F).

    See also: R-Value, Window U-Factor

    Uninterruptible Power Supply¶

    A battery-backed device that gives instant power to sensitive equipment during an outage and conditions the power. It bridges the gap until a generator takes over.

    Example: A small unit keeps a server running for fifteen minutes.

    See also: Emergency Power

    Units of Measurement¶

    The standard quantities used to express length, area, volume, force, temperature, and energy in construction. In US practice they are feet, inches, pounds, and Btu, with SI units used for conversions and international work.

    Example: A framing plan may give a room as 12 ft 6 in., while a European supplier lists the same room as 3.81 m.

    Utility Service¶

    The supply of electricity, gas, water, or other resources to a building from a public or private provider. It includes the lines and meters at the property.

    Example: An overhead or underground power line connects a house to the utility grid.

    See also: Service Entrance

    Vapor Control Layer¶

    The layer that limits the diffusion of water vapor through an assembly. Its permeance is chosen to suit the climate and the assembly.

    Example: In Minnesota walls, a smart membrane or polyethylene film on the interior side limits vapor entering the wall.

    See also: Vapor Retarder, Vapor Diffusion

    Vapor Diffusion¶

    The movement of water vapor through a material driven by differences in vapor pressure, without any bulk airflow. Different materials allow different rates.

    Example: Vapor slowly passes from a humid bathroom through drywall toward drier air.

    See also: Vapor Retarder, Permeability

    Vapor Retarder¶

    A material that slows the diffusion of water vapor into building assemblies, classified by permeance as Class I, II, or III. The class choice depends on climate.

    Example: The kraft paper facing on fiberglass batts is a Class II retarder, which slows vapor but does not stop it.

    See also: Vapor Control Layer, Permeability

    Variances and Appeals¶

    The formal processes by which an owner can request relief from a code requirement or contest an official's decision. A board decides on the request.

    Example: An owner of a historic building asks for an alternative to a stair width rule.

    See also: Building Official, Code Enforcement

    Ventilation¶

    The supply of outdoor air to indoor spaces and removal of stale air to dilute contaminants and moisture. It may be natural or mechanical.

    Example: Opening a window or running a bathroom fan ventilates a room.

    See also: Mechanical Ventilation, Indoor Air Quality

    Volatile Organic Compounds¶

    Carbon-based chemicals that evaporate easily at room temperature and are found in paints, adhesives, and finishes. Some irritate eyes or harm health.

    Example: A low-VOC paint gives off less of these vapors in a new classroom.

    See also: Indoor Air Quality, Material Health

    Voltage¶

    The electrical pressure that pushes charge through a circuit, measured in volts. It is the energy per unit of charge.

    Example: A typical US household outlet supplies 120 volts.

    See also: Current, Resistance

    Wall Assemblies¶

    The complete set of layers in a wall, from interior finish to exterior cladding, including framing, insulation, sheathing, barriers, and cladding. Each layer plays a role in performance.

    Example: A wall of drywall, 2x6 studs with batts, OSB, housewrap, and siding is one assembly.

    See also: Wall Sheathing, Cladding

    Wall Sheathing¶

    The structural or protective panels fastened to the exterior of wall framing, which stiffen the wall and provide a base for water-control layers and cladding. Common types are OSB, plywood, and gypsum-based boards.

    Example: 7/16 in. OSB is nailed on the outside of 2x6 studs.

    See also: Plywood and OSB, Weather-Resistive Barrier

    Wall Studs¶

    Vertical framing members, typically spaced 16 or 24 in. apart, that form the skeleton of a wall and carry vertical loads. They support sheathing and interior finishes.

    Example: 2x6 studs at 24 in. on center give room for 5.5 in. of insulation.

    See also: Headers, Platform Framing

    Water Control Layer¶

    The surface within a wall or roof that sheds or blocks liquid water and directs it outward. It is often the sheathing membrane behind the cladding.

    Example: Housewrap lapped like shingles keeps rain that gets past siding from reaching the sheathing.

    See also: Weather-Resistive Barrier, Flashing

    Water Efficiency¶

    The reduction of water use in and around buildings through low-flow fixtures, efficient appliances, and smart irrigation. It conserves supply and energy.

    Example: A toilet using 1.28 gallons per flush saves water compared to older 3.5 gallon models.

    Reference: U.S. EPA WaterSense tank-type toilet specification summary

    See also: Plumbing Fixtures

    Water Heating¶

    The process and equipment for raising the temperature of potable water for washing and other uses. Systems may be tank or tankless and use gas, electricity, or heat pumps.

    Example: A 50 gallon tank holds hot water for a family home.

    See also: Water Supply

    Water Intrusion¶

    The unwanted entry of liquid water into a building enclosure through leaks, capillary pull, or vapor condensation. It is the leading cause of damage to buildings.

    Example: Missing flashing above a window lets rain soak the wall framing.

    See also: Flashing, Mold

    Water Supply¶

    The pressurized piping that brings drinking water from a utility main or well to fixtures and appliances. It includes hot and cold lines and valves.

    Example: A 3/4 in. copper line serves the water heater and fixtures.

    See also: Plumbing Systems, Water Heating

    Water-Cement Ratio¶

    The weight of mixing water divided by the weight of cement in a concrete mix. A lower value produces stronger, denser, and more durable concrete.

    Example: A mix with 300 lb of water and 600 lb of cement has a ratio of 0.50.

    See also: Concrete Mix Design, Durability

    Weather-Resistive Barrier¶

    A material installed behind cladding to keep liquid water from reaching the structure while allowing vapor to escape. It drains water that passes the siding.

    Example: Housewrap or felt paper is stapled over sheathing before siding goes on.

    See also: Water Control Layer, Flashing

    Weathering¶

    The gradual breakdown of exposed materials caused by sun, rain, wind, temperature changes, and freezing. It alters color, surface, and strength over time.

    Example: Untreated cedar siding turns gray and develops surface cracks after years outdoors.

    See also: Durability

    Welded Connections¶

    Joints that fuse steel members together by melting metal at the interface and adding filler. They create strong, continuous joints but require skilled labor and inspection.

    Example: A welder fuses a base plate to a steel column in the shop.

    See also: Bolted Connections

    Wind Loads¶

    The pressures and suction forces that moving air applies to the surfaces of a building. They depend on wind speed, height, shape, and exposure.

    Example: Strong gusts push on a wall and pull upward on a roof.

    See also: Lateral Load System

    Window U-Factor¶

    The rate of heat transfer through a whole window per square foot per degree Fahrenheit, as rated by NFRC. Lower numbers mean better insulation.

    Example: A triple-pane window may have a rating of 0.20 while a single pane is near 1.0.

    See also: U-Value, Windows

    Windows¶

    Glazed openings in walls or roofs with frames and sashes that admit light and air and give views. They are a main source of heat gain and loss.

    Example: A double-hung window opens at the bottom and top.

    See also: Glazing, Window U-Factor

    Wood Framing¶

    The system of building frames with dimensional lumber or engineered wood members, such as studs, joists, and rafters. It is the dominant method for houses in North America.

    Example: A typical Minnesota house is framed with 2x6 walls and wood trusses.

    See also: Platform Framing, Lumber

    Wood Moisture Content¶

    The weight of water in wood expressed as a percentage of the wood's oven-dry weight. It controls shrinkage, strength, and decay risk.

    Example: Framing lumber is typically dried to 19% or less before installation.

    Reference: NIST Voluntary Product Standard PS 20, S-DRY at 19% maximum

    See also: Rot and Decay

    Zoning¶

    The local land-use regulation that controls what may be built where, including use, height, setbacks, and density. It is separate from the building code.

    Example: A residential zone may bar a factory and require houses to sit 25 ft from the street.

    See also: Building Permits
