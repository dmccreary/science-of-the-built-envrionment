    Infographic Poster Ideas¶

    This page proposes 20 infographic posters that give students a holistic, systems-level view of the science of the built environment. Each idea is grounded in a framework or diagram that has shaped how building professionals think, and each includes a detailed image description that an illustrator, an AI image generator, or a MicroSim author can work from.

    How This List Was Built¶

    I searched for the diagrams and frameworks that practitioners, researchers, and standards bodies use to explain buildings as systems rather than as piles of parts. Two honest caveats:

    • No single canonical "holistic poster" exists. The field's most influential visuals are individual diagrams (the control layers, the shearing layers, the life-cycle stages) rather than one master poster. This list takes those diagrams and turns each into a full poster concept.
    • These are original designs, not reproductions. I worked from the published descriptions of each framework, not from copies of the artwork. Every poster below should be drawn fresh, and the source framework should be credited in the poster footer. Numeric claims should be re-checked against the cited source before a poster is finalized.

    Common Design Standards¶

    Use these for every poster so the set reads as one family.

    Element Standard
    Format Landscape, 16:9 (1920 × 1080 px for the web, 36 × 24 in for print)
    Palette Safety orange #FF6A13 for focal points, chestnut brown for structure, cream for backgrounds, slate blue for water and moisture, brick red for heat, sage green for sustainability
    Typography One bold sans-serif title, one clean sans-serif for labels, and a minimum 18 px label size at web resolution
    Footer Chapter links, the source framework credit, and the line "The Science of the Built Environment"
    Mascot Beau the Beaver may appear once per student-facing poster, in the pose that matches the job (thinking, warning, tip). Instructor-facing versions omit Beau.
    Accessibility Never use color alone to carry meaning; pair every color with a label, pattern, or icon

    Posters 1, 2, 3, 7, and 20 are the most holistic. If only a few are produced, start there.

    Poster 1: The House as a System¶

    Influence: The U.S. Department of Energy's Building America program treats the house as a system whose performance depends on a balance of the envelope, the mechanical systems, and the occupants. All three affect the flow of heat, air, and moisture into and out of the building.

    Core message: A building is not a collection of products. Change one part and the whole system responds.

    Chapters: 3, 4, 19

    Image description:

    • Layout: A cutaway cross-section of a two-story Minnesota house sits in the center, filling about 60% of the canvas.
    • Three actors: Three large labeled rings overlap in a Venn arrangement behind the house: Envelope (chestnut), Mechanical Systems (slate blue), and Occupants (safety orange). The overlap zone behind the house reads "Building Performance."
    • Flows: Three families of arrows cross the house. Red arrows show heat flow, blue arrows show air flow, and teal droplet-arrows show moisture flow. Heat arrows leave through the walls, roof, and windows. Air arrows enter low (leaks at the sill) and exit high (leaks at the attic). Moisture arrows rise from the shower and kitchen.
    • Callouts: Each ring gets three short callouts. Envelope: insulation, air sealing, windows. Mechanical: furnace, ventilation, water heater. Occupants: thermostat setting, showers and cooking, windows opened.
    • Footer strip: "Heat. Air. Moisture. Three flows, one system."
    • Beau: Thinking pose, lower right, pointing at the overlap.

    Poster 2: The Perfect Wall¶

    Influence: Joseph Lstiburek's "Perfect Wall" from Building Science Corporation (BSI-001). A wall must control rain, air, vapor, and heat. In order of importance the layers are the rain control layer, the air control layer, the vapor control layer, and the thermal control layer. Lstiburek's slogan is to keep the outside out, and the best place for the layers is outside the structure.

    Core message: Control layers cascade in priority. If you cannot keep the rain out, do not worry about the air.

    Chapters: 11, 12

    Image description:

    • Layout: A horizontal wall cross-section runs left to right, from outdoors on the left to indoors on the right, drawn in layers like a stack of slices pulled slightly apart.
    • Layers, outside to inside: Cladding, drainage gap, water-resistive barrier, continuous exterior insulation, sheathing, studs, cavity, drywall.
    • Control layer overlay: Four colored bands run above the wall, each ending in a vertical bracket that marks where that control layer sits. Blue is rain, green is air, yellow is vapor, and red is thermal.
    • Priority ladder: On the right, a vertical ladder numbered 1 to 4 ranks the layers: Rain, Air, Vapor, Thermal. A bold note beside it reads "If layer 1 fails, layers 2 to 4 cannot save you."
    • Material callouts: A small inset shows that one material can serve several layers, such as a liquid-applied membrane that is both the water and air control layer.
    • Sun and snow icons on the exterior side; a warm living room silhouette on the interior side.
    • Beau: Tip pose, with a tape measure held against the thickness of the wall.

    Poster 3: The Six S's of Shearing Layers¶

    Influence: Frank Duffy's layered model of building change, expanded by Stewart Brand in How Buildings Learn into six layers: Site, Structure, Skin, Services, Space Plan, and Stuff. The layers have very different lifespans, and an adaptable building lets them slip past one another.

    Core message: A building is a stack of components on different clocks. Design so the fast layers can change without breaking the slow ones.

    Chapters: 1, 21

    Image description:

    • Layout: A large onion-style exploded diagram of one building, with six concentric or stacked layers peeled apart, from the outermost (Site) to the innermost (Stuff).
    • Per-layer content:
      • Site: the lot and soil, drawn as terrain with a property line. Lifespan label: "Eternal."
      • Structure: foundation and frame. Label: "30 to 300 years."
      • Skin: the exterior surfaces. Label: "About 20 years."
      • Services: wires, pipes, ducts. Label: "7 to 15 years."
      • Space Plan: interior walls and doors. Label: "3 to 30 years."
      • Stuff: furniture and belongings. Label: "Days to years."
    • Time ruler: A logarithmic ruler along the bottom, with each layer's lifespan drawn as a bar, from a short orange bar for Stuff to a very long chestnut bar for Structure.
    • Sidebar: "Slipping layers": two layers drawn with a dashed gap between them, versus two layers fused together with a crack running through them.
    • Beau: Thinking pose.

    Poster 4: Four Ways Moisture Moves¶

    Influence: The DOE and Building Science Corporation moisture-physics literature identifies four transport mechanisms: bulk (liquid) water, capillary action, air-transported moisture, and vapor diffusion. Bulk water is the potentially most damaging mechanism, and air leakage typically moves far more water vapor than diffusion does.

    Core message: Most people worry about diffusion. Air leakage and liquid water do most of the damage.

    Chapters: 4, 11, 21

    Image description:

    • Layout: A four-panel grid, each panel showing the same simple wall-and-foundation section with a different mechanism highlighted.
    • Panel A, Bulk water: Rain running down a wall and pooling at a foundation, with a roof leak and a flashing gap highlighted. Blue arrows.
    • Panel B, Capillary action: A concrete footing wicking groundwater upward into a wall, drawn with a magnified inset of tiny pores and rising water.
    • Panel C, Air-transported moisture: Warm humid indoor air streaming through an electrical outlet and a top-plate gap, condensing on cold sheathing. Wavy air arrows carrying small droplets.
    • Panel D, Vapor diffusion: Individual vapor molecules passing slowly through drywall, drawn as tiny dots drifting through a porous layer.
    • Ranking bar: A horizontal bar under the grid ranks relative damage potential, with the bar for air and bulk water drawn much longer than the bar for diffusion.
    • Beau: Warning pose beside Panel C, with the note "A one-inch hole beats a whole wall of vapor barrier."

    Poster 5: Heat on the Move¶

    Influence: Foundational building-science heat-transfer teaching: conduction, convection, and radiation, and the way thermal bridges let heat bypass insulation. Passive House practice makes thermal-bridge-free detailing one of its core principles.

    Core message: Insulation only works if heat cannot go around it.

    Chapters: 3, 19

    Image description:

    • Layout: Split canvas. The left half shows three heat-transfer modes; the right half shows an infrared-style thermal image of a wall.
    • Left half: Three small vignettes in a column. Conduction: a spoon in hot soup, then a stud conducting heat through a wall. Convection: air rising along a warm radiator and falling at a cold window. Radiation: a sun-warmed floor and a person feeling chill near a cold window.
    • Right half: A false-color thermal view of an exterior wall with warm stripes where studs and rim joists sit, labeled "Thermal bridges." Cooler colors show the insulated cavities.
    • Detail inset: A balcony slab penetrating the wall, with a heat "leak" arrow, versus the same detail with a thermal break.
    • Footer: "R-value is the label. Detailing is the result."
    • Beau: Tip pose with a flashlight, "Look for the studs in the frost."

    Poster 6: The Load Path¶

    Influence: The structural-engineering principle that every load must have a continuous, unbroken path to the ground. This is a standard concept in structural education rather than a single published diagram.

    Core message: Follow the force. Roof, wall, floor, foundation, soil. Break the path and something moves.

    Chapters: 6, 7, 8, 10

    Image description:

    • Layout: A tall wood-frame house section on the left with a bright orange arrow tracing the load from the roof to the soil.
    • Path steps: Numbered stations along the arrow: 1 Roof snow and wind, 2 Rafters and trusses, 3 Headers over openings, 4 Studs and posts, 5 Floor framing, 6 Foundation wall, 7 Footing, 8 Soil.
    • Load types: A color-coded key shows dead load, live load, snow load, wind load, and seismic load, with a small arrow icon for each. Wind is drawn pushing sideways and uplifting the roof.
    • Right panel: Three "what if" thumbnails: a removed bearing wall with a sagging floor, a missing hurricane tie with a roof lifting, and a footing on soft soil settling.
    • Comparison strip: Four miniature building types (wood, steel, concrete, masonry) each with its own load path drawn in orange.
    • Beau: Warning pose, "Check your load path!"

    Poster 7: The Whole Building Design Wheel¶

    Influence: The National Institute of Building Sciences' Whole Building Design Guide (WBDG) diagram: a high-performance building at the center, wrapped by an Integrated Team Process and Integrated Design Approach, with eight design objectives around the outer ring: Accessible, Aesthetics, Cost-Effective, Functional/Operational, Historic Preservation, Productive, Secure/Safe, and Sustainable.

    Core message: Good buildings balance competing objectives. The team that designs together builds better.

    Chapters: 2, 17

    Image description:

    • Layout: A large wheel. The center disc is a simple building icon labeled "High-Performance Building." A ring around it is labeled "Integrated Team Process" and "Integrated Design Approach."
    • Outer ring: Eight equal segments, one per objective, each with an icon: a wheelchair ramp, a facade, a dollar sign, a floor plan, a historic column, a person at a desk, a shield, and a leaf.
    • Spokes: Thin lines connect adjacent objectives, with small "tension" labels, such as a daylight window between Aesthetics and Sustainable, or a fire door between Secure/Safe and Accessible.
    • Team strip: Along the bottom, small portraits of the owner, architect, engineer, contractor, inspector, and electrical designer all pointing at the center.
    • Beau: Thinking pose.

    Poster 8: The Five Principles of Passive House¶

    Influence: The Passive House standard's five principles: continuous super-insulation, airtight construction, high-performance windows and doors, thermal-bridge-free design, and balanced ventilation with heat recovery. Sources describe the approach as holistic and report heating and cooling energy reductions of up to about 90% compared with conventional construction.

    Core message: Five measures, one outcome. Skip one and the others lose power.

    Chapters: 19

    Image description:

    • Layout: A cutaway of a compact two-story house, with five numbered orange tags attached to features.
    • Tags: 1 Insulation wraps the entire envelope, drawn as a thick continuous blanket. 2 A blower-door fan sits in the front doorway with a gauge, labeled airtightness test. 3 Triple-pane windows with a cross-section inset showing the glass layers. 4 A thermal-break detail where the wall meets the foundation. 5 A heat-recovery ventilator in the utility room, with two crossing airstreams swapping heat but not air.
    • Interdependence web: A five-pointed star in the corner connects all five principles with lines, labeled "Remove any point and the star collapses."
    • Result badge: "Up to about 90% less heating and cooling energy." Verify the claim against the current Passive House Institute figure before publishing.
    • Beau: Celebration pose, small, in the corner.

    Poster 9: The Indoor Species¶

    Influence: The National Human Activity Pattern Survey (NHAPS), funded by the U.S. EPA and published by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory in 2001, found that Americans spent about 86.9% of their time indoors, plus about 5.5% in vehicles, the origin of the often-quoted "90% indoors." The Harvard Healthy Buildings program adds the "9 Foundations of a Healthy Building."

    Core message: Because we are an indoor species, the building is our primary environment, and it should be designed like a habitat.

    Chapters: 4, 14

    Image description:

    • Layout: The top third is a 24-hour clock ring colored by location: a large slice for home, work, and school, a small slice for vehicles, and a thin slice for outdoors.
    • Bottom two-thirds: A nine-tile grid, one tile per foundation, each with an icon: ventilation, air quality, thermal health, moisture, dust and pests, safety and security, water quality, noise, lighting and views.
    • Tile content: Each tile carries a one-line student-friendly "what it means" and a chapter link.
    • Source note: "NHAPS 1992 to 1994, U.S. EPA / LBNL." A small footnote points out that the 90% figure is an average of surveyed U.S. adults, not a universal constant.
    • Beau: Thinking pose, "Your building is your habitat."

    Poster 10: Thermal Comfort Is Six Variables¶

    Influence: P. O. Fanger's Predicted Mean Vote (PMV) model, used in ASHRAE Standard 55, which combines environmental variables (air temperature, radiant temperature, air speed, humidity) with personal factors (clothing insulation and metabolic rate). Brager and de Dear's adaptive model adds that acceptable indoor temperatures also shift with the outdoor climate.

    Core message: Comfort is not just the thermostat number.

    Chapters: 4

    Image description:

    • Layout: A central figure of a seated person, with six spokes radiating out to labeled icons.
    • Spokes: Air temperature (a thermometer), mean radiant temperature (a sun-warmed or cold wall), air speed (a draft), relative humidity (a droplet gauge), clothing insulation (a sweater), and metabolic rate (a person walking versus sitting).
    • Scale strip: A horizontal seven-point sensation scale along the bottom: Cold (-3), Cool (-2), Slightly cool (-1), Neutral (0), Slightly warm (+1), Warm (+2), Hot (+3).
    • Case studies: Three small scenes: a person next to a cold window with a 72 °F thermostat reading yet feeling chilly; a gym at the same temperature feeling too hot; a naturally ventilated room in summer where people accept a warmer range.
    • Beau: Tip pose, "The cold window beat the thermostat."

    Poster 11: The Carbon Footprint of the Built World¶

    Influence: Architecture 2030's widely used chart of the built environment's share of global CO₂ emissions. Their published figures are about 42% of annual global CO₂ emissions: roughly 27% from building operations and roughly 15% from the embodied carbon of materials and construction. They also project about 2.6 trillion square feet (241 billion square meters) of new floor area from 2020 to 2060, "the equivalent of adding an entire New York City to the world, every month, for 40 years," and note that three-quarters of the infrastructure that will exist in 2050 has yet to be built. The UNEP Global Status Report for Buildings and Construction 2024/2025 gives a different accounting: 32% of global energy and 34% of CO₂ emissions.

    Core message: Buildings are a leading climate lever, and materials matter as much as operating energy.

    Chapters: 20

    Image description:

    • Layout: A large donut chart on the left shows all human CO₂ emissions, with the building-related slice pulled out. The pulled-out slice splits into two labeled parts: Operational (about 27%) and Embodied (about 15%).
    • Right panel: A skyline silhouette made of stacked blocks. Each block is labeled with a material: cement, steel, aluminum, timber, glass, insulation.
    • Timeline bar: "2020 → 2060: 241 billion m² of new floor area" drawn as a long bar of repeating small city icons.
    • Accounting note: A small footnote explains that different sources draw the boundary differently, comparing the Architecture 2030 and UNEP figures side by side. Never mix the two in one number.
    • Beau: Warning pose, "Check which boundary the number uses."

    Poster 12: Cradle to Grave to Cradle¶

    Influence: The life-cycle stage structure in EN 15978: A1 to A3 product stage, A4 to A5 transport and construction, B1 to B7 use stage, C1 to C4 end of life, and Module D for benefits beyond the system boundary. Embodied carbon covers modules A1 to A5, B1 to B5, and C1 to C4; operational carbon covers B6 and B7.

    Core message: A material's impact does not start at the jobsite or end at the dumpster.

    Chapters: 20

    Image description:

    • Layout: A long horizontal timeline in the shape of a looping ribbon, with four colored stages in order.
    • Stage A, Product and Construction (A1 to A5): Quarry, factory, truck, crane. Highlight A1 to A3 as the "upfront carbon" burst.
    • Stage B, Use (B1 to B7): A building with a wrench, a paint roller, a replaced window, and a meter. B6 and B7 flagged as operational energy and water.
    • Stage C, End of Life (C1 to C4): Demolition, sorting, transport, landfill.
    • Module D, Beyond the boundary: A curved arrow looping from C back to A with icons for reuse and recycling.
    • Overlay bracket: Two brackets over the timeline: "Embodied carbon" and "Operational carbon."
    • Beau: Thinking pose.

    Poster 13: The Building as a Chimney¶

    Influence: The stack effect, as treated in Building Science Corporation's BSI-075 and in fire-protection engineering. Warm indoor air rises and leaves high, drawing cold outdoor air in low. In a fire, stack effect can carry smoke and interfere with smoke-control systems.

    Core message: In winter every tall building is a chimney, and the same physics that wastes heat can spread smoke.

    Chapters: 4, 14, 18

    Image description:

    • Layout: A tall multi-story building cross-section with an elevator shaft and stairwell highlighted as vertical "flues."
    • Airflow: Blue arrows enter low on the cold side and red arrows exit high near the roof. A dashed horizontal line marks the "neutral pressure plane" about halfway up, with pressure plus and minus signs above and below.
    • Winter vs. summer: A smaller mirrored building shows the reversed flow in a hot, air-conditioned summer.
    • Fire inset: A floor-plan view of a smoke plume drawn toward a stairwell, then a second panel with a pressurized stair preventing it.
    • Consequences: Three callouts: door that slams or will not open, whistling at the elevator lobby, uneven heating between floors.
    • Beau: Warning pose.

    Poster 14: The Swiss Cheese Model of Building Failure¶

    Influence: James Reason's Swiss cheese model (1990), which shows that accidents happen when holes in several layers of defense line up. It is widely used in safety engineering, healthcare, and aviation. This poster applies it to buildings, which is a teaching adaptation rather than a published building standard.

    Core message: Buildings rarely fail from one cause. Failure happens when holes in several defenses line up.

    Chapters: 17, 18, 21

    Image description:

    • Layout: Five upright slices of Swiss cheese seen in perspective, with a single orange arrow passing through aligned holes from left to right.
    • Slices, left to right: Design (a code-compliant but unbuildable detail), Materials (substituted product), Construction (a missed flashing), Inspection (skipped or rushed), Maintenance (clogged gutter, ignored leak).
    • End result: A building icon with a damaged corner, with the label "Mold, collapse, or fire spread."
    • Example case strip: Three real failure types shown generically, such as a roof leak, a deck collapse, and a smoke-spread incident, each with its aligned holes.
    • Fix panel: A second set of slices with fewer, offset holes, labeled "Redundancy."
    • Beau: Encouraging pose with a hard hat and a clipboard.

    Poster 15: The Living Building Petals¶

    Influence: The International Living Future Institute's Living Building Challenge, built from seven performance areas, or "petals": Place, Water, Energy, Health and Happiness, Materials, Equity, and Beauty, with 20 imperatives in all (ten core and ten that push performance further).

    Core message: A building can give more than it takes: it can generate energy, treat water, and improve its place.

    Chapters: 20

    Image description:

    • Layout: A large flower with seven petals around a building at the center.
    • Petal contents: Place (a walkable, restored site), Water (a rain cistern and green roof), Energy (solar panels), Health and Happiness (a daylit room), Materials (a non-toxic, transparent materials list), Equity (people of all abilities and incomes sharing a common space), Beauty (a sculptural detail and a mural).
    • Center: A building icon labeled "Living Building."
    • Contrast strip: Along the bottom, a "less bad" building versus a "net positive" building, with arrows showing resources in and out.
    • Beau: Celebration pose.

    Poster 16: Nature in the Room¶

    Influence: Terrapin Bright Green's 14 Patterns of Biophilic Design, published in 2014. A tenth-anniversary edition adds a fifteenth pattern, Awe. The patterns fall into three groups: Nature in the Space, Natural Analogues, and Nature of the Space.

    Core message: Daylight, views, and natural materials are not decoration; they are inputs to occupant health.

    Chapters: 4, 16

    Image description:

    • Layout: A single richly drawn interior (a classroom or office) in the center, with callout lines to features.
    • Three color-coded groups: Nature in the Space (plants, water feature, daylight, breezes), Natural Analogues (wood grain, biomorphic forms, material patterns), Nature of the Space (a prospect view, a sheltered refuge nook, a path that reveals more).
    • Lighting link: A daylight-versus-electric-light strip that ties to the lighting systems in Chapter 16.
    • Footer: "Design for the human body, not just the code."
    • Beau: Neutral pose, relaxed, near a window.

    Poster 17: When the Power Goes Out¶

    Influence: Alex Wilson coined passive survivability in 2005, after Hurricane Katrina, for a building's ability to maintain critical life-support conditions during an extended loss of power, heating fuel, or water. The Resilient Design Institute argues it should be a baseline for residential codes.

    Core message: A good building keeps its occupants safe even when the grid and the furnace fail.

    Chapters: 19, 21

    Image description:

    • Layout: A two-panel before/after of the same Minnesota house in a January blackout.
    • Panel A, Typical house: Thin insulation, leaky envelope, dark windows. A thermometer inside drops quickly from 68 °F toward freezing, with a clock ticking.
    • Panel B, Resilient house: A well-insulated, airtight envelope with a south-facing window letting in low winter sun, and a thermometer that holds a safer temperature much longer.
    • Strategy ring: Icons for insulation, air sealing, passive solar gain, daylighting, natural ventilation, and shading.
    • Question strip: "How many hours until it is unsafe?" with a student-fillable blank that can later become a MicroSim input.
    • Beau: Tip pose with a blanket and a flashlight.

    Poster 18: Minnesota Asks Hard Questions¶

    Influence: U.S. climate-zone classification, in which Minneapolis falls in zone 6A (cold-humid) and northern Minnesota in zone 7, together with the Minnesota Energy Code. The course description emphasizes frost-protected foundations, high-performance insulation, air sealing, and ice-dam and moisture control. Pull any numeric requirements directly from the current adopted code; do not copy values from secondary summaries.

    Core message: Climate is the first design input. Each system in a Minnesota building has a cold-climate answer.

    Chapters: 9, 10, 11, 13

    Image description:

    • Layout: A Minnesota state outline in the center with the 6A and 7 zones shaded, with a house cross-section on the right.
    • Questions and answers: Five "hard question" callouts from the climate, each with a matched design answer on the house. Frost depth (deep or frost-protected footings), freeze-thaw (durable concrete and drainage), long heating season (heavy insulation and air sealing), ice dams (roof air sealing and ventilation), summer humidity (dehumidification and moisture control).
    • Climate data strip: Design-temperature and degree-day figures, to be filled from an authoritative source such as ASHRAE or the code itself.
    • Beau: Warning pose in a parka and hard hat.

    Poster 19: Idea to Occupancy¶

    Influence: The integrated team process in the WBDG and the standard phases of the design and construction process: programming, schematic design, design development, construction documents, bidding, construction, commissioning, and occupancy.

    Core message: A building is created by a sequence of people and decisions, and the earlier a decision is made, the cheaper it is to get right.

    Chapters: 2, 16, 17

    Image description:

    • Layout: A winding road from the left (idea) to the right (occupancy), with eight milestone signs.
    • Swim lanes: Under the road, horizontal bars show who is most active in each phase: owner, architect, structural engineer, mechanical engineer, electrical designer, contractor, code official and inspector.
    • Influence curve: An overlaid curve showing that the ability to influence cost and performance is highest at the start, with a falling cost-of-change line crossing a rising effort line. Draw this as a conceptual curve, not a data plot.
    • Electrical designer highlight: A callout marks where electrical design coordinates with the other trades, which supports Dunwoody's electrical-design audience.
    • Beau: Welcome pose at the start of the road.

    Poster 20: The Building Systems Map¶

    Influence: Research on holistic synergy analysis of building subsystems, together with the systems thinking that runs through Building America, Passive House, and the WBDG. Treat this as the capstone poster that ties the course together.

    Core message: Every system depends on every other. Change one and the others must respond.

    Chapters: 14, 15, 16, 19

    Image description:

    • Layout: A network diagram with seven large nodes arranged in a ring: Structure, Enclosure, HVAC, Plumbing, Electrical, Fire Protection, Occupants.
    • Links: Labeled arrows connect the nodes. Examples: Enclosure to HVAC, a tighter envelope shrinks the heating load and requires mechanical ventilation. HVAC to Electrical, heat pumps raise the electrical service demand. Structure to Fire Protection, the structural material sets the fire-resistance rating and the sprinkler needs. Plumbing to Enclosure, pipes in exterior walls risk freezing and leaks. Occupants to everything, behavior drives load and wear.
    • Ripple example: A highlighted pathway shows one change (adding triple-pane windows) rippling through to a smaller heating system, a smaller electrical service, and lower cost, with an opposing arrow noting added cost and weight.
    • Link density: Line thickness shows how strongly two systems interact.
    • Footer: "No system is an island."
    • Beau: Celebration pose, since this poster summarizes the whole book.

    1. Posters 1, 2, 3, 7, 20: the five most holistic. They frame the whole book.
    2. Posters 4, 5, 6, 10: the core physics posters, tied to Chapters 3 to 6.
    3. Posters 8, 11, 12, 15: the sustainability and performance cluster.
    4. Posters 9, 13, 14, 16, 17, 18, 19: the cross-cutting and applied posters.
