    Quiz: Energy Codes and Building Performance Standards¶

    Test your understanding of the concepts in Appendix H with these review questions.

    1. How often are model energy codes such as the IECC and ASHRAE Standard 90.1 revised?¶

    1. Every year
    2. Roughly every three years
    3. Roughly every ten years
    4. Only when Congress passes a new energy law
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The International Energy Conservation Code and ASHRAE Standard 90.1 are each updated roughly every three years, and each edition generally tightens requirements for insulation, windows, air leakage, lighting, and mechanical equipment. This regular code edition cycle means a building will meet several newer editions during its life. States adopt editions on their own timetables.

    2. Who decides which edition of a model energy code applies to a Minnesota project?¶

    1. The model-code organization, which enforces its edition directly
    2. The federal government, which adopts one edition nationwide for every state
    3. The contractor, who may choose any edition that was current at the time of design
    4. The state, which adopts editions on its own timetable, often with amendments, while local jurisdictions enforce them
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Model codes such as the IECC and ASHRAE 90.1 are written by standards organizations, but they become law only when a state adopts them. Local jurisdictions then enforce the adopted edition, sometimes with local amendments. That is why a project should ask the building official which edition and amendments apply. Chapter 17 explains the adoption chain from model code to local enforcement.

    See: How Codes Change

    3. A building is designed to stand for 75 years, and a new code edition appears every three years. About how many new editions will be published while it stands?¶

    1. 25
    2. 15
    3. 36
    4. 75
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Dividing the building's life by the edition cycle gives 75 / 3 = 25 editions. For a 60-year building the same arithmetic gives about 20. Each edition will probably ask more of a new building than the one before, so a building that just meets today's minimum will be behind the code for most of its life. Option D confuses years with editions.

    Concept Tested: Code Edition Cycle

    See: The Timeline Problem

    4. A heat pump has a service life of 18 years, and the building will stand for 60 years. How many times is the heat pump replaced?¶

    1. 2
    2. 4
    3. 3
    4. 1
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Replacements fall at years 18, 36, and 54, and the building's life ends before a fourth. The formula is the building life divided by the component life, rounded up, minus one: ceil(60 / 18) - 1 = 3. Option B counts the original installation as a replacement. Parts of a building age at very different rates, and the structure and the enclosure's insulation and air barrier may never be replaced.

    5. How does a building performance standard (BPS) differ from an energy code?¶

    1. A BPS applies only to new buildings, and a code applies to existing buildings
    2. A code governs what is built, and a BPS governs how an existing building operates, with limits, reporting, and penalties
    3. A BPS is a model code that is published by the same organization every three years
    4. A BPS applies the same limits to every building, whatever its size
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A code sets requirements for construction. A building performance standard sets limits on energy use or greenhouse gas emissions per square foot for existing buildings above a size threshold, requires owners to measure and report, and applies penalties or requires improvements when a building exceeds its limit. New York City's Local Law 97 is a well-known example, and the standards differ in thresholds, metrics, and schedules.

    See: Performance Standards for Existing Buildings

    6. What does Minnesota law direct for the commercial energy code in effect in 2036 and after?¶

    1. A 20 percent reduction in energy use compared with the current code
    2. Net-zero energy use for every commercial building
    3. A 50 percent reduction in energy use compared with a 1990 baseline
    4. An 80 percent reduction in annual net energy consumption compared with a 90.1-2004 baseline
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Minnesota Statutes section 326B.106 requires the commissioner, beginning in 2024, to adopt each new published edition of ASHRAE 90.1 or a more efficient standard. It also requires that the code in effect in 2036 and afterward achieve an 80 percent reduction in annual net energy consumption compared with a 90.1-2004 baseline, with codes adopted in between moving incrementally toward it. Check the Department of Labor and Industry for current editions.

    7. What does an electric-ready requirement ask of a new building?¶

    1. Install electric heat pumps and remove all gas service
    2. Mount a solar array before occupancy
    3. Include the wiring, panel space, and circuits that a later switch to electric appliances or an electric vehicle charger would need
    4. Provide a battery system sized for the whole building
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Electric-ready provisions look ahead to equipment that has not been installed yet. The building gets the pathway for it, meaning wiring, panel space, and outlets or circuits, so a later switch is easy. Solar-ready provisions do the same for a future array, with unshaded roof area and a protected wiring path. Which provisions apply, and to which buildings, varies by jurisdiction and edition, so confirm with the building official.

    Concept Tested: Electric-Ready and Solar-Ready Requirements

    See: How Codes Change

    8. Why do codes require pathways for equipment that will be installed later?¶

    1. Building the pathway costs little during construction and a great deal afterward, when finished walls must be opened
    2. The builder must install the future equipment before the final inspection
    3. Model codes forbid modifying a building after occupancy
    4. The pathways raise the insulation value of the enclosure
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. A conduit, a spare panel space, or an unshaded roof area added during construction costs very little. The same pathway added later means opening finished walls and ceilings, upgrading the service, or moving equipment. The reasoning matches the lesson of the timeline problem: leave room for later upgrades in the parts of the building that are hard to change.

    Concept Tested: Electric-Ready and Solar-Ready Requirements

    See: The Timeline Problem

    9. Which design conclusion does the timeline of code editions against a building's life support?¶

    1. Build to the bare minimum, because the next code will require replacing everything anyway
    2. Overbuild the parts replaced most often, such as the roof membrane and the heat pump
    3. Delay construction until the final code edition has been published
    4. Build the hard-to-change parts, especially the enclosure, better than the current minimum, and leave room in the electrical service and mechanical spaces
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Parts that are replaced two or three times, such as a roof membrane or a heat pump, can be upgraded when they are replaced. The structure and the enclosure's insulation and air barrier stay in place for the whole life, so a code minimum built today locks in a weak performance for decades. Leaving space in the electrical service and mechanical rooms makes later upgrades practical.

    10. At the start of a project, what should you ask the building official?¶

    1. Whether the newest published model-code edition applies, whether or not the state has adopted it
    2. Which code editions and local amendments apply, and note the date the permit application is filed
    3. Which edition was current when the owner bought the land
    4. Whether the project will be reviewed under the edition in force when it is completed
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A project is generally reviewed under the edition in force at the time of review, so the filing date matters, as do any local amendments to the state's adopted edition. A model code that the state has not adopted has no legal force, and the date the land was bought has nothing to do with the code. Asking early avoids redesign once the plans reach review.
