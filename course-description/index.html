    The Science of the Built Environment¶

    Title: The Science of the Built Environment

    Target Audience: College undergraduate students at Dunwoody College of Technology in Minneapolis, Minnesota, in construction, architecture, and building-systems programs, including future electrical designers

    Prerequisites: None beyond a high-school education. Basic high-school algebra and geometry are helpful.

    Course Overview¶

    This course introduces the properties and applications of common building materials used for foundations, framing, and building systems. It builds a broad understanding of the science of the built environment by showing how materials are selected, combined, and assembled to create buildings that are safe, functional, and durable.

    Students examine how concrete, steel, wood, masonry, glass, insulation, and other materials behave under load, moisture, heat, fire, and time. They learn how those behaviors determine the design of structural systems, enclosure systems, and the mechanical, plumbing, and electrical systems that make a building habitable. Along the way, students learn the vocabulary of the construction industry, the sequence of the design and construction process, and the role that each professional, including the electrical designer, plays from concept through commissioning.

    The course also treats codes, regulations, and sustainability as design inputs rather than afterthoughts. Students learn to apply building codes, to compare materials by performance, cost, and environmental impact, and to justify material choices with evidence. Because the course is taught in Minneapolis, examples emphasize cold-climate construction: frost-protected foundations, high-performance insulation and air sealing, ice-dam and moisture control, and the Minnesota State Building Code and Minnesota Energy Code. The goal is a technically grounded generalist who can read a set of drawings, talk with specialists, and make sound decisions about how buildings are made.

    Main Topics Covered¶

    • The design and construction process: project phases, delivery methods, and the roles of owner, architect, engineer, contractor, and inspector
    • Construction industry terminology, drawings, and specifications
    • Fundamental physics of buildings: loads, forces, stress and strain, heat, moisture, air, light, and sound
    • Properties of common building materials: strength, stiffness, durability, thermal, moisture, and fire behavior
    • Site work, soils, and foundation systems: shallow and deep foundations, slabs, retaining walls, and frost depth in cold climates
    • Structural systems and framing: wood, steel, concrete, and masonry
    • Building enclosure systems: roofs, walls, windows, air and water barriers, and insulation
    • Building systems: mechanical (HVAC), plumbing, fire protection, and vertical transportation
    • Electrical systems and the role of the electrical designer across design and construction phases
    • Building codes, standards, and regulations: the Minnesota State Building Code, the Minnesota Energy Code, permitting, inspection, fire and life safety, and accessibility
    • Sustainable building materials: embodied carbon, life-cycle assessment, recycled content, and green certification
    • Building durability, maintenance, failure modes, and building forensics

    Topics Not Covered¶

    • Detailed structural engineering calculations, such as member sizing and finite element analysis
    • Architectural design theory, aesthetics, and history
    • Construction cost estimating, scheduling, and project finance in depth
    • Detailed electrical circuit design, load calculations, and lighting design beyond an introductory level
    • Civil infrastructure such as bridges, highways, dams, and utilities networks
    • Land development, zoning law, real estate, and urban planning
    • Construction trade skills and hands-on installation techniques
    • Building information modeling (BIM) software operation

    Learning Outcomes¶

    After completing this course, students will be able to:

    Remember¶

    Retrieving, recognizing, and recalling relevant knowledge from long-term memory.

    • List the major phases of the design and construction process in order
    • Name the common building materials and state their primary uses in foundations, framing, and enclosures
    • Define common construction industry terms, such as load path, shear wall, header, flashing, vapor barrier, and R-value
    • Identify the main components of a foundation, a structural frame, and a building enclosure
    • Recall the major model building codes and the agencies that adopt and enforce them, including the Minnesota State Building Code
    • Recognize the roles of the owner, architect, engineer, electrical designer, contractor, and building official

    Understand¶

    Constructing meaning from instructional messages, including oral, written, and graphic communication.

    • Explain how dead, live, wind, seismic, and snow loads travel through a structure to the ground
    • Describe how strength, stiffness, ductility, and durability differ among wood, steel, concrete, and masonry
    • Explain how heat, air, and moisture move through a building enclosure and why control layers matter in a cold climate like Minnesota's
    • Summarize how foundations transfer loads to different soil types
    • Explain how a building code is organized and why code requirements exist
    • Describe the contribution of the electrical designer in each phase of design and construction
    • Explain what embodied carbon and life-cycle assessment measure

    Apply¶

    Carrying out or using a procedure in a given situation.

    • Use construction terminology correctly when reading drawings and specifications
    • Apply code tables and provisions to determine requirements such as occupancy classification, egress, and fire-resistance ratings
    • Calculate simple loads, tributary areas, and basic R-values and U-values for a wall assembly
    • Sequence the activities of a small building project from site preparation through occupancy
    • Select an appropriate material for a given structural or enclosure application using stated criteria
    • Interpret a typical wall section and identify each layer and its function

    Analyze¶

    Breaking material into constituent parts and determining how the parts relate to one another and to an overall structure or purpose.

    • Analyze a building's structural system to identify its load path, lateral force-resisting system, and critical connections
    • Compare the performance of alternative enclosure assemblies for thermal, moisture, air, and fire control
    • Examine how decisions in one system, such as the structure, constrain choices in other systems, such as mechanical and electrical
    • Diagnose likely causes of common building failures, such as cracking, water intrusion, and condensation
    • Break down the design and construction process to show where information is handed off and where coordination fails
    • Differentiate the properties of materials to explain why each suits one application better than another

    Evaluate¶

    Making judgments based on criteria and standards through checking and critiquing.

    • Evaluate a building enclosure for code compliance and expected durability
    • Judge the suitability of a structural system for a given building type, site, and budget
    • Assess alternative materials using performance, cost, constructability, and environmental criteria
    • Evaluate sustainable materials using life-cycle data, certifications, and embodied-carbon values
    • Critique a proposed construction sequence for risks, conflicts, and missing steps
    • Appraise the coordination between electrical design and other disciplines at each project phase

    Create¶

    Putting elements together to form a coherent or functional whole; reorganizing elements into a new pattern or structure.

    • Develop a materials and systems selection matrix for a small building
    • Design a wall assembly that meets stated thermal, moisture, and fire requirements
    • Prepare a construction sequence and design-phase responsibility chart for a building project
    • Compose a code-compliance checklist for a specific building type
    • Propose a more sustainable alternative for a conventional material or assembly, with supporting evidence
    • Capstone project: Select a small existing or proposed building and produce a complete report that describes its foundation, structural frame, enclosure, and building systems. The report evaluates material choices against code and sustainability criteria, and it recommends improvements.
