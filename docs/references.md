# References

This page collects book-level references for *The Science of the Built Environment*.
Chapter-specific sources live in each chapter's **Annotated References** page.
Here we look at the works that shape the whole book, starting with the
standard visual text on how buildings go together.

## Building Construction Illustrated (Francis D.K. Ching)

### Citation

Ching, Francis D.K. *Building Construction Illustrated*. Hoboken, NJ: Wiley.
The sixth edition appeared around 2020 **(verify)**. The book was first
published in 1975 by Van Nostrand Reinhold. Note the spelling of the author's
first name: **Francis**, not Frances.

### What the book is

*Building Construction Illustrated* is a visual introduction to how buildings
are put together. Its distinguishing feature is that the drawings, not the
prose, carry the explanation. Each page pairs hand-drawn pen-and-ink
illustrations with short captions. The book is commonly described as
containing well over 1,000 illustrations. The exact count varies by edition
**(verify)**.

The book follows a building from the ground up:

- **Site and foundations**, including how loads reach the soil.
- **Floor systems**, **wall systems**, and **roof systems**, in wood, steel,
  concrete, and masonry.
- **Openings**: doors and windows.
- **Interior finishes.**

Later editions add material on building codes, sustainability, and
organization by the CSI MasterFormat. The book covers small-scale
construction (light wood frame, masonry, light steel) and larger structural
systems. Its organization by building system helps readers see how
foundations, structure, enclosure, and finishes depend on one another.

### The drawing method

Ching's drawings use a small set of conventions that readers learn once and
then reuse across the whole book:

- **Sections and cutaways** that show layers inside walls, floors, and roofs.
- **Exploded axonometric views** that separate components so each can be
  seen, along with the order in which they are assembled.
- **Consistent line weights and notation**, so a detail on one page reads the
  same way as a detail on another.
- **Captions kept deliberately short**, which forces the drawing to do the
  explaining and makes the book usable by readers whose first language is not
  English.

### About the author

Francis D.K. Ching (born 1943 in Honolulu) is an architect and educator who
trained at the University of Notre Dame and taught for decades at the
University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee, with earlier posts at other universities
including Ohio State and the University of Washington **(verify)**. His
other widely used titles include:

- *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*
- *A Visual Dictionary of Architecture*
- *Architectural Graphics*
- *Building Codes Illustrated*
- *Building Structures Illustrated* (with co-authors)

Together these form a visual curriculum covering design, graphics, codes,
structure, and construction. The third edition of *Building Construction
Illustrated* credits Cassandra Adams as a co-author **(verify)**.

### Impact on construction education

1. **A de facto standard text.** The book is widely assigned in architecture,
   interior design, construction management, and building technology courses,
   and it is a common reference for licensure exam preparation **(verify
   course-adoption claims against syllabi if cited)**.
2. **Drawing as pedagogy.** Ching showed that a well-constructed diagram can
   teach assembly logic: what sits against what, in what order, and why.
   Students learn to read construction drawings, and to produce their own, by
   studying his.
3. **A bridge between design and technology.** The book frames construction
   as part of design thinking rather than as a separate technical subject,
   which helps design students see why details matter.
4. **A shared visual vocabulary.** Many practitioners learned the same
   section and detail conventions from his work, so his style echoes through
   studio projects and firm drawing standards.
5. **Accessibility.** Compact captions and a uniform graphic language work
   for visual learners and for readers working in a second language. The book
   has been translated into many languages **(verify)**.
6. **Longevity.** Regular revisions for code changes, new materials, and
   energy and sustainability concerns have kept the book current for about
   five decades.

### Limitations

Ching's book is an excellent answer to "how is this assembled?" It is a
weaker answer to "why does this behave the way it does?" The limitations
below explain why this book exists alongside it rather than replacing it.

- **Static, print-first medium.** A page cannot show an assembly sequence
  unfolding, a load path changing as a load moves, or heat flowing through a
  wall as indoor and outdoor temperatures change. Readers must infer
  behavior from a fixed picture.
- **Describes construction, not building science.** The book is thin on
  hygrothermal behavior (heat, air, and moisture movement), energy modeling,
  and quantitative structural reasoning. It shows where the air barrier and
  insulation go far better than it explains why they work or what happens
  when they are misplaced.
- **Brevity trades depth for breadth.** Short captions make the book fast to
  scan, but a student who needs the reasoning behind a detail, such as why a
  rainscreen is vented, will need another source.
- **US-centered.** The book emphasizes North American practice: the
  International Building Code, CSI formats, and common US assemblies. Readers
  working under other codes, climates, or traditional building methods will
  find gaps.
- **Code and product currency.** Code references and sustainability material
  age with each code cycle. A student using an older edition may be working
  from outdated requirements.
- **Not interactive and not assessable.** There are no embedded simulations,
  quizzes, or feedback loops. Learners cannot test their understanding
  against the material.
- **Copyright.** The illustrations are copyrighted. This book cannot reuse or
  closely imitate them. Our diagrams and MicroSims must be original work.
- **Count and edition claims.** The "over 1,000 illustrations" figure is a
  commonly repeated approximation and has not been independently verified for
  this page **(verify)**.

### How this book relates to Ching's

*The Science of the Built Environment* is designed to complement *Building
Construction Illustrated*, not compete with it. Ching shows how assemblies
are built. This book uses interactive MicroSims to show how those
assemblies behave: heat transfer through a wall layer, air leakage under
pressure, moisture paths through cladding, and load paths through a frame.
Ching's economy of explanation is still a good model. Each MicroSim aims to
make one idea visible and let the learner change a variable to see what
happens.

A student could use the two together. Read the relevant Ching pages to see
the assembly, then open the matching MicroSim to see why it works.

### Related textbooks cited across chapters

The chapter reference pages also draw on these works. They are listed here
because they complement Ching in different ways:

- Allen, Edward, and Joseph Iano. *Fundamentals of Building Construction:
  Materials and Methods.* Wiley. A material-by-material treatment of
  construction with its own explanatory illustrations.
- Building Science Corporation (buildingscience.com). Essays and research
  reports that explain the physics behind enclosure design. These fill much
  of the building-science gap noted above.
