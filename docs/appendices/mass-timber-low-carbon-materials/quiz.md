# Quiz: Mass Timber and Low-Carbon Materials

Test your understanding of the concepts in [Appendix G](index.md) with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What does Type IV-A construction allow for mass timber buildings under the 2021 International Building Code?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Exposed mass timber up to 12 stories with no added fire protection
2. Mass timber up to 9 stories and 85 feet, the old limit for wood buildings
3. Mass timber up to 18 stories and 270 feet, with fire-resistive protection of the timber
4. Light-frame wood construction up to 18 stories
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The 2021 IBC added Types IV-A, IV-B, and IV-C for tall mass timber buildings. Type IV-A allows up to 18 stories and 270 feet, and the taller types require fire-resistive protection of the timber, including an encapsulation layer, and a minimum one-inch noncombustible topping on floors. Jurisdictions adopt code editions on their own schedules, so availability depends on where the building is.

    **Concept Tested:** Type IV Mass Timber Construction

    **See:** [Mass Timber in the Code](index.md#mass-timber-in-the-code)

#### 2. How do large timber members resist fire?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The outer layer chars and insulates the wood inside, which stays cool and keeps its strength
2. Fire-retardant chemicals throughout the member prevent it from burning
3. The water held in the wood cannot evaporate, so the member never gets hot
4. The member burns quickly at first, and then the fire goes out by itself
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The outer layer burns and forms an insulating char, and the wood inside the char stays cool and keeps its strength. The char depth grows at a roughly steady rate, commonly taken as about 1.5 inches per hour for design. Timber does burn, but its behavior is predictable, which is what lets designers calculate the section that remains after a given fire exposure.

    **Concept Tested:** Timber Charring

    **See:** [The Physics That Does Not Change](index.md#the-physics-that-does-not-change)

#### 3. Using a char rate of 1.5 inches per hour, how deep is the char on each exposed face after a three-hour fire?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 3.0 inches
2. 4.5 inches
3. 6.0 inches
4. 0.5 inches
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Char depth is the rate times the exposure time: 1.5 in/h × 3 h = 4.5 inches on each exposed face. The designer must size the member so that the section left after that loss, plus a thin layer of heat-weakened wood, still carries the load. That is why mass timber members look oversized next to steel of the same capacity.

    **Concept Tested:** Timber Charring

    **See:** [The Physics That Does Not Change](index.md#the-physics-that-does-not-change)

#### 4. Why does steel made in an electric-arc furnace have a lower carbon footprint than steel made from ore?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It skips the rolling mill, which makes up most of the carbon
2. It is certified to burn at a steady char rate
3. It is made from carbon-mineralized concrete
4. It melts scrap steel instead of making new steel from ore
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Electric-arc furnaces melt recycled scrap, so they avoid the energy-intensive step of making iron from ore. The recycled-content figure of a product therefore matters when comparing steel on carbon. An environmental product declaration reports the actual figure for a specific product. Low-carbon steel is one of several strategies, along with low-carbon concrete and mass timber, that change how designers count embodied carbon.

    **Concept Tested:** Low-Carbon Steel

    **See:** [Low-Carbon Concrete and Steel](index.md#low-carbon-concrete-and-steel)

#### 5. In a cross-laminated timber (CLT) warm roof, where does the insulation go, and why?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Above the deck, so the deck stays near room temperature all winter
2. Below the deck, so the timber remains visible as a finished ceiling
3. Inside the deck, between the CLT layers, to save thickness
4. Nowhere, because the thick timber supplies all the insulation needed
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. In a warm roof, the insulation sits above the deck, so the deck stays warm and above the dew point of the room air. A vapor control layer directly on the deck stops room moisture from rising into the insulation. Without the insulation above, the deck would run cold and vapor could condense on the wood. Timber has some R-value, but far less than the roof needs.

    **Concept Tested:** Warm Roof Assembly

    **See:** [Mass Timber and Moisture](index.md#mass-timber-and-moisture)

#### 6. Why is a CLT roof deck vulnerable during construction?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The adhesive between the layers dissolves within hours of any moisture
2. Wood loses all of its strength once it has been wet, even briefly
3. It is a thick, solid panel that absorbs water and dries slowly, and wood held above about 20 percent moisture content can decay
4. It cannot be covered by a membrane until the building is finished
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A thick CLT panel soaks up rain during construction and releases it slowly, and wood that stays above about 20 percent moisture content for long periods can decay. That is why the vapor control layer on top of the deck also sheds rain before the final roof membrane goes on, and why the assembly is designed to keep the deck warm and dry. Wood is durable when it stays dry.

    **Concept Tested:** Mass Timber Moisture Protection

    **See:** [Mass Timber and Moisture](index.md#mass-timber-and-moisture)

#### 7. What is an environmental product declaration (EPD)?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A code-required label that states a product's fire rating
2. A government limit on the embodied carbon of materials it buys
3. A manufacturer's warranty on the product's structural capacity
4. A standardized, third-party-verified report of a product's life-cycle impacts
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Manufacturers publish EPDs, and designers read them like a data sheet to compare products on carbon as they compare them on strength and cost. The government limit in option B is a different tool, a buy-clean procurement policy, which can use EPD numbers to enforce its limits. An EPD reports the impacts, and it does not itself limit anything.

    **Concept Tested:** Buy Clean Procurement

    **See:** [Counting Carbon](index.md#counting-carbon)

#### 8. What is buy-clean procurement?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A rule that builders may purchase only recycled materials
2. A policy in which public owners or governments set limits on the embodied carbon of the materials they buy
3. A manufacturer's pledge to offset emissions after delivery
4. A third-party report of a product's life-cycle impacts
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Under buy-clean policies, some public owners and governments limit the embodied carbon of the materials they purchase, which gives suppliers a reason to publish EPDs and to lower the carbon of their products. State and federal procurement rules are still developing, and the supply of low-carbon products in the Upper Midwest is something to watch. Option D describes an EPD, which supplies the data.

    **Concept Tested:** Buy Clean Procurement

    **See:** [Counting Carbon](index.md#counting-carbon)

#### 9. Why is replacing part of the cement with fly ash, slag, or calcined clay a low-carbon strategy?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. These materials make the concrete lighter, which cuts transport emissions to zero
2. These materials absorb carbon dioxide from the air as the concrete cures
3. Cement is the largest source of concrete's carbon, so using less of it lowers the total, though strength gain changes
4. These materials make the concrete gain strength faster, so slabs can be thinner
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Cement production is the biggest contributor to the carbon in concrete, so replacing some of it with supplementary cementitious materials reduces the total. The change affects how fast the concrete gains strength, which designers must account for. Carbon mineralization is a different strategy, in which captured carbon dioxide is injected into the mix and becomes a mineral.

    **Concept Tested:** Low-Carbon Concrete

    **See:** [Low-Carbon Concrete and Steel](index.md#low-carbon-concrete-and-steel)

#### 10. How should a designer treat the carbon stored in wood, called biogenic carbon?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. As a benefit that depends on forest management, how the wood is used, and how it is accounted for, with standards still being refined
2. As a guaranteed credit equal to all the carbon in the wood, whatever its source
3. As irrelevant, because only concrete and steel carry embodied carbon
4. As identical for every product, because one worldwide accounting rule fixes it
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Wood stores biogenic carbon, but the benefit is not automatic. It depends on how the forest is managed, how the wood is used, and how the accounting is done, and the standards for crediting it are still being refined. Embodied carbon covers every material in a building, including wood. Designers should read the product's EPD and the project's accounting rules, not assume a fixed credit.

    **Concept Tested:** Biogenic Carbon

    **See:** [Counting Carbon](index.md#counting-carbon)
