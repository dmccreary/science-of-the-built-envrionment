# Quiz: Geothermal Heating and Earth-Coupled Air

Test your understanding of the concepts in [Appendix E](index.md) with these review questions.

---

#### 1. Which statement best describes the temperature of soil below about 10 feet?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is about 55°F everywhere in the United States
2. It is nearly constant and close to the local average annual air temperature
3. It equals the warmest summer air temperature at the site
4. It is colder than the coldest winter air temperature at the site
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Deep soil holds a nearly steady temperature, typically between 45°F and 75°F depending on the climate zone, near the local average annual air temperature. That makes it warmer than a winter day and cooler than a summer one. The common figure of 55°F is too warm for the Twin Cities, where deep soil is in the mid-40s to low 50s, so always use the value for the site.

    **Concept Tested:** Ground Temperature Profile

    **See:** [How Deep Does the Season Reach?](index.md#how-deep-does-the-season-reach)

#### 2. How does the seasonal temperature swing of soil change as you go deeper?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It shrinks, and the warmest and coldest times arrive later than at the surface
2. It shrinks, but the extremes still occur on the same days as at the surface
3. It stays the same, but the whole profile shifts to the annual mean
4. It grows, because deeper soil traps more heat
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Soil conducts heat slowly, so the seasons reach down gradually. The swing shrinks by a factor of about 2.7 for every damping depth, and the timing slips by about 58 days per damping depth. At 10 feet in the Twin Cities the coldest soil of the year arrives in early April, not January. The ground is therefore not at one fixed temperature at every depth.

    **Concept Tested:** Seasonal Temperature Swing

    **See:** [How Deep Does the Season Reach?](index.md#how-deep-does-the-season-reach)

#### 3. At a site where the surface swing is ±28°F and the damping depth is about 8 feet, what is the swing at a depth of 16 feet?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. ±14°F
2. ±10°F
3. ±8°F
4. ±4°F
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Sixteen feet is two damping depths, and the swing shrinks by a factor of e, about 2.7, for each one. After two depths the factor is about 7.4, so 28 / 7.4 is about 3.8°F, or roughly ±4°F. Option A treats each damping depth as a halving, and option B is the swing after only one depth. The appendix gives about ±8°F at 10 feet and ±2°F at 20 feet.

    **Concept Tested:** Damping Depth

    **See:** [How Deep Does the Season Reach?](index.md#how-deep-does-the-season-reach)

#### 4. Why can a ground-source heat pump reach a higher possible COP than an air-source machine on a 5°F day?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It uses a different refrigerant that has a higher Carnot limit
2. The soil is warmer than the building's supply air
3. The loop water is much warmer than the outdoor air, so the lift is smaller
4. The ground loop makes heat instead of moving it
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. With 100°F supply air, loop water at 40°F gives a lift of 33.3 K and a Carnot limit of 9.3, while 5°F air gives a lift of 52.8 K and a limit of 5.9. The ground source is about 1.6 times higher. The soil is still colder than the supply air, so the machine must still move heat uphill. A ground loop also keeps its performance at the coldest hour of the year.

    **Concept Tested:** Ground-Source Heat Pump

    **See:** [The Physics That Does Not Change](index.md#the-physics-that-does-not-change)

#### 5. An earth tube draws 150 cfm of outdoor air at 10°F through buried pipe, and the air leaves the pipe at 40°F. What preheat does it provide?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 4,860 Btu/h
2. 6,480 Btu/h
3. 1,620 Btu/h
4. 48,600 Btu/h
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The heat rule is Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT, so Q = 1.08 × 150 × 30 = 4,860 Btu/h. Option B uses 40°F as the temperature change, and option D is a decimal slip. The value is real but modest, which is why an earth tube is normally a supplement to a heat recovery ventilator and not a replacement for one.

    **Concept Tested:** Earth Tube

    **See:** [The Physics That Does Not Change](index.md#the-physics-that-does-not-change)

#### 6. For the Minnesota house in the appendix, a 200-foot earth tube costing $10,000 saves about $117 a year. What does this show?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Earth tubes should replace heat recovery ventilators in Minnesota
2. The yearly savings would double if the pipe were twice as long
3. Heat from the ground is free, so the payback is short whatever the installed cost
4. The yearly saving is small compared with the installed cost, so the payback takes many decades
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The simple payback is the installed cost divided by the yearly savings, $10,000 / $117, or about 85 years, and the 30-year return is -65 percent. The estimate even favors the earth tube, because it leaves out fan energy and upkeep. Savings level off as the pipe gets longer while the cost keeps rising, so doubling the length does not double the savings.

    **Concept Tested:** Earth Tube

    **See:** [Does an Earth Tube Pay for Itself?](index.md#does-an-earth-tube-pay-for-itself)

#### 7. Why can an earth tube grow mold in summer?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Dry soil releases mold spores directly into the airstream
2. Cool soil can chill humid summer air below its dew point, and the condensate can feed mold
3. The pipe slopes toward a drain, which traps moisture inside
4. The filter at the intake adds moisture to the air
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Cool soil can cool humid summer air below its dew point, so water condenses inside the pipe, and that condensate can feed mold. Designers slope the pipe to a drain, keep it smooth and cleanable, and filter the air, and many now prefer closed-loop systems that never put ground air in the ventilation stream. Sloping to a drain removes water, so it helps and does not harm.

    **Concept Tested:** Earth Tube

    **See:** [What Is Changing](index.md#what-is-changing)

#### 8. Why must a Minnesota ground loop be sized for long-term balance?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Soil conducts heat too quickly in winter
2. The antifreeze solution in the loop loses strength every year
3. The loop removes more heat from the soil each winter than it returns in summer, so the soil can cool over decades
4. Summer cooling adds more heat to the soil than winter heating removes
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. In a heating-dominated climate, the net heat flow is out of the ground, so the soil around the loop can slowly cool. Designers size the loop for the long term, check the soil's conductivity and groundwater flow, and sometimes add heat from solar thermal collectors or cooling-season heat rejection. Option D describes a cooling-dominated climate, which is the reverse of Minnesota.

    **Concept Tested:** Ground Loop

    **See:** [Ground Loops Need Balance](index.md#ground-loops-need-balance)

#### 9. What is networked geothermal?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A single loop dedicated to one building and its heat pump
2. A buried air duct that preheats ventilation air for a group of buildings
3. A grid of geothermal power plants that sells electricity to utilities
4. A shared loop that serves many buildings, each with its own heat pump, so buildings needing cooling can give heat to those needing heating
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. In networked geothermal, a shared loop connects many buildings, and each has its own heat pump. Heat rejected by a building that needs cooling becomes a source for a neighbor that needs heating. Utilities have built pilot projects, and the approach is a candidate for replacing aging gas distribution networks. It uses water or antifreeze in a loop, not air ducts or power plants.

    **Concept Tested:** Networked Geothermal

    **See:** [Three Ways to Use the Ground](index.md#three-ways-to-use-the-ground)

#### 10. Why does the soil at 10 feet reach its coldest temperature in early April and not in January?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Snowmelt in March chills the soil all at once
2. The sun's angle is lowest in April
3. Soil conducts heat slowly, so the seasonal signal arrives about 58 days later for each damping depth
4. Heating systems keep the soil warm until April
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The coldest air of the year reaches the surface in January, but the cold travels down through the soil slowly. At a depth of 10 feet, about 1.3 damping depths, the delay is about 73 days, which puts the coldest soil in early April. A ground loop buried at that depth therefore sees a gentle, delayed cycle and not a fixed temperature.

    **Concept Tested:** Ground Temperature Profile

    **See:** [How Deep Does the Season Reach?](index.md#how-deep-does-the-season-reach)
