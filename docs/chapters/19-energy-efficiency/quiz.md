# Quiz: Energy Efficiency and High-Performance Buildings

Test your understanding of the concepts in this chapter with these review questions, one for each of the 14 concepts covered.

#### 1. Option X scores 5, 3, and 2 on Environment, Society, and Economy, and option Y scores 3, 4, and 4. With weights of 50 percent, 20 percent, and 30 percent, which option wins?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Option Y, with 3.7 against 3.5 for option X
2. Neither, because both options tie at 3.6
3. Option Y, because it has the higher unweighted average of 3.67
4. Option X, with 3.7 against 3.5 for option Y
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The weighted total is each score times its weight, summed. Option X scores 0.5 × 5 + 0.2 × 3 + 0.3 × 2 = 3.7, and option Y scores 0.5 × 3 + 0.2 × 4 + 0.3 × 4 = 3.5. With equal weights, Y would win with 3.67 versus 3.33. The weights are an owner's values and drive the answer, so they should be agreed on before the design is judged.

    **Concept Tested:** Sustainability

    **See:** [Sustainability](index.md#sustainability)

#### 2. Why do designers reduce the load by improving the enclosure before they size equipment or add renewable energy?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The enclosure is the cheapest of all building systems, so it is always done first for budget reasons alone
2. Every unit of load removed shrinks every system downstream, including equipment, wiring, and the solar array
3. Renewable energy cannot be installed until the whole enclosure has been completed and tested
4. Equipment is not rated until the enclosure is tested, so sizing of the equipment must wait for the results
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Efficiency delivers the same useful service with less energy, unlike conservation, which reduces the service itself. The order is to reduce the load, meet the remaining load efficiently with equipment such as heat pumps and heat recovery, control systems so they run only when needed, and then supply the remainder from renewables. Annual heat loss follows Q = (A/R) × HDD × 24, so each added layer of R saves less.

    **Concept Tested:** Energy Efficiency

    **See:** [Energy Efficiency](index.md#energy-efficiency)

#### 3. How are high-performance buildings defined?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. By results that substantially exceed the code minimum on several measures, such as energy, comfort, and air quality
2. By a single feature, such as rooftop solar panels or a particular type of insulation, installed on the building
3. By the certification plaque that they carry, regardless of the performance that is measured in operation
4. By their height and structural system, since taller buildings always use less energy per occupant each year
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Practices that produce them recur: an integrated design process that brings engineers, owner, and contractor into the conversation early, a compact form with a continuous, well-insulated, airtight enclosure, right-sized mechanical systems, controls that match operation to need, and commissioning to verify the result. A high-performance design usually costs more to design but less to operate, so the owner's life-cycle view matters.

    **Concept Tested:** High-Performance Buildings

    **See:** [High-Performance Buildings](index.md#high-performance-buildings)

#### 4. Which statement about the Minnesota Energy Code is correct?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is a voluntary standard that applies only to buildings seeking LEED certification or other green ratings
2. It applies to commercial buildings only and sets no requirements for windows, insulation, or air leakage
3. It is part of the Minnesota State Building Code, treats commercial and residential separately, and sets Minneapolis in zone 6
4. It is a federal law that is identical in every state and cannot be amended by the state or the cities
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The commercial code is typically based on ASHRAE 90.1 or the commercial IECC, and the residential code on the IECC, each with Minnesota amendments. It covers the building envelope, lighting power and controls, equipment efficiency, service water heating, and in many cases commissioning and ventilation. Because the edition and amendments change over time, the designer confirms the version with the building official.

    **Concept Tested:** Minnesota Energy Code

    **See:** [Minnesota Energy Code](index.md#minnesota-energy-code)

#### 5. Why do owners verify performance by measuring even when a building has a green building rating?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Rating systems are required by the energy code, so measurements are needed to show compliance after occupancy
2. Rating systems score only the design on paper, so they cannot be applied to completed and occupied buildings
3. Rating systems are only as good as the evidence behind the points, and a plaque does not guarantee low bills
4. Rating systems replace the energy code, so measurements are used to confirm which one applies to the project
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Green building rating systems are voluntary programs that score a building against sustainability criteria and award a level of recognition. They go beyond the code, which sets a legal minimum, and give owners a way to demonstrate achievement. Examples include LEED, Green Globes, the Living Building Challenge, and the ENERGY STAR score. Minnesota's B3 program and Sustainable Building 2030 set energy targets for publicly funded buildings.

    **Concept Tested:** Green Building Rating Systems

    **See:** [Green Building Rating Systems](index.md#green-building-rating-systems)

#### 6. At the summer solstice in Minneapolis the noon sun is about 68.5° above the horizon (tan 68.5° ≈ 2.54). How far down the wall does the shadow of a 2 ft overhang fall?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. About 0.8 ft
2. About 2.0 ft
3. About 1.2 ft
4. About 5.1 ft
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The shadow depth equals the projection times the tangent of the sun's altitude: 2 × 2.54 ≈ 5.1 ft, enough to shade most of a 6 ft window at summer noon. In winter the sun stands only about 21.5° high, so the same overhang casts a shadow of about 0.8 ft and leaves the window in sun. A fixed overhang therefore blocks summer heat and admits winter sun with no moving parts.

    **Concept Tested:** Passive Solar Design

    **See:** [Passive Solar Design](index.md#passive-solar-design)

#### 7. How does the performance path differ from the prescriptive path for energy code compliance?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The performance path lists minimum values for each component, which the reviewer simply compares to the drawings and schedules
2. The performance path uses an energy model to show the design uses no more energy than a reference building, allowing trade-offs
3. The performance path requires no documentation at all, because the inspector measures actual energy use after occupancy
4. The performance path applies only to residential buildings, while the prescriptive path applies only to commercial buildings
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. In the prescriptive path, the design meets minimum values for components such as roof insulation, wall insulation, and window performance. In the performance path a large glazed wall, for example, can be offset by better roof insulation or more efficient lighting. Compliance forms are submitted with the construction documents, and inspectors verify items in the field such as insulation installation and air barrier continuity.

    **Concept Tested:** Energy Code Compliance

    **See:** [Energy Code Compliance](index.md#energy-code-compliance)

#### 8. A building avoids 50 MMBtu of natural gas per year. Using about 117 lb of CO₂ per MMBtu, how much operational carbon is avoided?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. About 5,850 lb of CO₂ per year
2. About 585 lb of CO₂ per year
3. About 58,500 lb of CO₂ per year
4. About 167 lb of CO₂ per year
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Operational carbon is energy used times an emission factor, so 50 × 117 = 5,850 lb. Electricity has no fixed factor, since it depends on the grid's mix of generation and falls as the grid adds clean power. Operational carbon is the counterpart of embodied carbon, which is emitted during manufacturing and construction. Electrifying heating with heat pumps cuts emissions further as the grid gets cleaner.

    **Concept Tested:** Operational Carbon

    **See:** [Operational Carbon](index.md#operational-carbon)

#### 9. Why is mechanical ventilation with heat or energy recovery essential in a Passive House?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The airtight enclosure leaves almost no natural leakage, so stale air and moisture must be removed mechanically
2. The enclosure is so leaky that heat recovery is needed to recover the large amount of heat that is lost
3. The standard forbids windows that open, so fans must provide all of the fresh air to every room
4. The heating system is so large that it needs a supply of fresh air in order to operate properly
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Passive House rests on five principles: very high continuous insulation, airtight construction, high-performance windows, a ventilation system with heat or energy recovery, and minimizing thermal bridges. The classic standard limits heating demand to about 15 kWh per square meter per year and airtightness to about 0.6 ACH50. An airtight enclosure traps moisture and pollutants unless ventilation replaces stale air.

    **Concept Tested:** Passive House

    **See:** [Passive House](index.md#passive-house)

#### 10. A building uses 60,000 kWh per year, PV yields 1,250 kWh per installed kW, and an array needs about 80 ft² of roof per kW. How much roof area is needed to reach net zero?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. About 750 ft²
2. About 4,800 ft²
3. About 60,000 ft²
4. About 3,840 ft²
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The array size is 60,000 / 1,250 = 48 kW, and the roof area is 48 × 80 = 3,840 ft². Using 750 comes from dividing the use by 80 and ignoring the yield. A net-zero building produces as much renewable energy over a year as it uses. The route follows the efficiency order, so cutting a one-story building's energy use intensity can decide whether the array fits on the roof.

    **Concept Tested:** Net-Zero Energy

    **See:** [Net-Zero Energy](index.md#net-zero-energy)

#### 11. A project earns 62 points of about 110 under a recent version of LEED. What level does it achieve?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Silver, which covers 50 to 59 points
2. Gold, which covers 60 to 79 points
3. Certified, which covers 40 to 49 points
4. Platinum, which covers 80 or more points
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. LEED, the U.S. Green Building Council's rating system, awards points in categories such as location and transportation, sustainable sites, water efficiency, energy and atmosphere, materials and resources, and indoor environmental quality, and projects must also meet prerequisites. The thresholds are Certified 40 to 49, Silver 50 to 59, Gold 60 to 79, and Platinum 80 or more. A third-party review confirms the documentation.

#### 12. Why does daylighting save energy only if the electric lighting responds?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Daylight reduces the demand for heating, so the lights must be left on to replace the heat that is lost
2. Daylight is always brighter than electric light, so the lights must be left on to match it in the evening
3. Daylight harvesting sensors must dim or switch off lights near windows, or the lights keep using energy in daylight
4. Daylight is reflected by the glass of the windows, so only electric light ever reaches the room interior
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Daylighting uses natural light so electric lights can be turned off or dimmed. It saves lighting energy, reduces cooling load from lighting heat, and improves the occupants' experience. A rule of thumb is that daylight from a side window usefully reaches about twice the window head height, and a clerestory spreads light deeper while reducing glare. Glass also loses heat, can cause glare, and can overheat a space.

    **Concept Tested:** Daylighting

    **See:** [Daylighting](index.md#daylighting)

#### 13. Why is the benefit of a cool roof smaller in Minnesota than in a hot climate?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. White roofs are illegal under the Minnesota Energy Code, so none can be installed on any commercial building
2. Reflective surfaces absorb more heat in cold weather, so they raise heating costs throughout the winter
3. The reflective surface also reflects winter sun that could help heating, snow covers the roof, and heating dominates
4. Cool roofs stop working below freezing, so they need heating cables to keep the membrane reflective
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A cool roof reflects a high share of sunlight and radiates absorbed heat efficiently, keeping the roof cooler and reducing heat entering the building. They help most in cooling-dominated climates, and codes tend to require them in the warmest zones. In Minnesota the overall effect depends on the balance of cooling and heating loads. A building with substantial summer cooling loads may still benefit.

    **Concept Tested:** Cool Roofs

    **See:** [Cool Roofs](index.md#cool-roofs)

#### 14. Which measures are typical examples of water efficiency?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Larger water heaters, higher-pressure supply, more fixtures per restroom, and longer pipe runs in the building
2. Thicker pipe insulation, larger drain pipes, bigger sump pumps, and additional hose bibs around the building
3. Heavier roofing, tighter air barriers, larger window areas, and additional skylights over the large rooms
4. Low-flow fixtures such as WaterSense models, landscaping needing little irrigation, and metering that reveals leaks
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Water must be pumped, treated, and heated, and heating water is itself a large energy use, so saving water also saves energy. Federal standards set about 1.6 gallons per flush for toilets, and WaterSense models use about 1.28 gallons per flush or less. In the Riverbend example, 120 flushes per day over 260 operating days save about 9,984 gallons a year.

    **Concept Tested:** Water Efficiency

    **See:** [Water Efficiency](index.md#water-efficiency)
