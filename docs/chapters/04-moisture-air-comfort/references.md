# References: Moisture, Air Movement, and Thermal Comfort

1. [Dew point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dew_point) - Wikipedia - Defines dew point as the temperature at which air reaches 100 percent relative humidity, with formulas and measurement methods. Explains why moist indoor air condenses on cold windows, sheathing, and framing in winter.

2. [Psychrometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychrometrics) - Wikipedia - Covers the properties of air-water vapor mixtures, including dry-bulb temperature, humidity ratio, and enthalpy. Its psychrometric chart section shows how to read humidity, dew point, and relative humidity from a single diagram.

3. [Stack effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_effect) - Wikipedia - Explains buoyancy-driven air movement through buildings, including the pressure-difference equation and the role of the neutral pressure plane. Directly relevant to air leakage and winter infiltration in tall or heated cold-climate buildings.

4. Building Science for Building Enclosures (1st Edition) - John Straube and Eric Burnett - Building Science Press - Straube and Burnett organize moisture control into rain, air, vapor, and thermal control layers, and clearly separate vapor diffusion from air-transported moisture. Their wetting-versus-drying framework is the standard way to analyze wall assemblies.

5. Thermal Comfort: Analysis and Applications in Environmental Engineering (1st Edition) - P. Ole Fanger - Danish Technical Press - Fanger derived the Predicted Mean Vote and Predicted Percentage Dissatisfied from a human heat-balance model. This unified air temperature, radiant temperature, air speed, humidity, activity, and clothing in one framework that underlies ASHRAE Standard 55.

6. [BSD-106: Understanding Vapor Barriers](https://buildingscience.com/documents/digests/bsd-106-understanding-vapor-barriers) - Building Science Corporation - Joseph Lstiburek explains vapor retarder classes by permeance and why wall assemblies must be allowed to dry. Features illustrated wall sections for different climate zones, including cold climates like Minnesota.

7. [BSD-014: Air Flow Control in Buildings](https://buildingscience.com/documents/digests/bsd-014-air-flow-control-in-buildings) - Building Science Corporation - Describes the three drivers of air movement (wind, stack effect, and mechanical equipment) and the requirements for an air barrier system. Stresses continuity of the air barrier and contrasts air barriers with vapor barriers.

8. [CBE Thermal Comfort Tool](https://comfort.cbe.berkeley.edu/) - Center for the Built Environment, UC Berkeley - Free online calculator that computes thermal comfort from air temperature, radiant temperature, air speed, humidity, metabolic rate, and clothing. Lets students watch a psychrometric chart change as inputs vary, following ASHRAE 55.

9. [Mold and Moisture Dynamics](https://www.wbdg.org/resources/mold-and-moisture-dynamics) - Whole Building Design Guide (WBDG) - Federal design guidance on how moisture enters buildings as liquid water, vapor, and air-carried humidity, and how mold growth follows. Useful for connecting condensation, capillary action, and air leakage to real building failures.

10. [Your Northern Home: Attics/Air leakage/Stack effect](https://basc.pnnl.gov/videos/your-northern-home-atticsair-leakagestack-effect) - Building America Solution Center (PNNL) - Short video from the Cold Climate Housing Research Center showing how warm, moist air escapes into cold attics through stack effect. Practical cold-climate air-sealing strategies reinforce this chapter's air leakage and condensation concepts.
