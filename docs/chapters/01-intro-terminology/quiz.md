# Quiz: Introduction to the Built Environment and Construction Terminology

Test your understanding of the concepts in this chapter with these review questions, one for each of the 22 concepts covered.

#### 1. Which description best defines the built environment?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The collection of all structures that enclose a roof and walls, excluding roads, utilities, parks, and open spaces
2. The set of natural landscapes and ecosystems that surround a city and supply it with water and energy
3. Every human-made space where people live, work, and move, including buildings, infrastructure, and open spaces
4. The group of professionals and firms that design and construct buildings in a given region
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The built environment spans buildings plus the roads, utilities, and open spaces that connect them, which is why it differs from the natural environment even though the two exchange energy, water, and materials. Restricting it to enclosed structures leaves out infrastructure, and describing the professionals who make it confuses the environment with the construction industry.

#### 2. According to the chapter, what distinguishes a building from other human-made structures such as bridges, towers, and dams?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A building is always supported by a steel or concrete frame
2. A building is enclosed and roofed to shelter people, activities, or goods
3. A building is the only structure that must carry gravity and wind loads
4. A building is the only structure that requires a permit before construction
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A building is an enclosed, roofed structure that shelters people, activities, or goods. Bridges and dams carry loads too, but they do not enclose usable interior space, so they fall under different standards. Frame material does not define a building, and carrying load is shared by every structure, so neither can be the distinguishing feature.

#### 3. Why does a designer select concrete for a foundation wall against wet soil but dimensional lumber for a small house's floor structure?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Each material is chosen for the job it must do, and no single material is best at every task
2. Concrete is always stronger than lumber, so it is used wherever load must be carried
3. Lumber cannot carry any load once it is placed in contact with the ground
4. Building codes require concrete below grade and lumber above grade in every building
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The chapter stresses that materials are chosen for a job. Concrete resists moisture and is strong in compression, which suits a buried wall, while lumber is light, inexpensive, and easy to install, which suits a floor. Choosing the single strongest material everywhere ignores trade-offs in cost, weight, durability, and moisture behavior.

    **See:** [Building Materials](index.md#building-materials)

#### 4. Which statement correctly describes the construction industry?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A single group of general contractors that builds every project from start to finish without subcontractors
2. It is a government agency that reviews plans and inspects work for code compliance
3. It is the set of manufacturers that supply materials and products to building sites
4. A network of firms and professionals that plan, design, build, and maintain the built environment
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The construction industry is a large, fragmented network of owners, architects, engineers, contractors, subcontractors, building officials, and suppliers. Describing it as only contractors, only the building official, or only suppliers names a single participant rather than the whole network of people who plan, design, build, and maintain buildings.

#### 5. Why must designers from every discipline coordinate when one building system is changed?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Each system is built by the same crew, so a change requires rescheduling that crew
2. Building codes treat the six systems as a single system for permitting purposes
3. Changing a system always requires a new permit from the building official
4. The six systems are interdependent, so a change in one ripples into the others
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Systems are interdependent: the enclosure determines how big the mechanical system must be, and mechanical and electrical runs need openings through the structure. The leaky-wall example shows one defect touching four systems. Systems are usually built by different trades, and permits are not the reason designers coordinate.

#### 6. A framing note reads "Provide a double header over the window opening, bearing on jack studs at each side." What should the framer build?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A single horizontal board below the window that rests on the floor
2. A two-piece beam above the opening resting on short vertical studs at each side
3. Two full-height studs placed in front of the glass at each side, with no horizontal member above
4. A pair of diagonal braces that connect the window frame to the roof
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A header is a horizontal member that spans an opening, "double" means two pieces, "bearing" means resting on something, and jack studs are the short vertical members that support the header. Together the note describes a two-piece beam carrying loads around the opening instead of through the glass.

#### 7. A length is written 10'-6" on a drawing. What is it in decimal feet for an engineering calculation?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 10.6 ft
2. 10.06 ft
3. 10.5 ft
4. 11.0 ft
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. To convert feet-inches to decimal feet, divide the inches by 12 and add the result to the feet: 6 / 12 = 0.5, so the length is 10.5 ft. The mistake of writing 10.6 treats inches as tenths of a foot. The result 10.06 comes from dividing by 100 instead of 12, and 11.0 rounds the inches up to a whole foot.

    **Concept Tested:** Units of Measurement

    **See:** [Units of Measurement](index.md#units-of-measurement)

#### 8. A wall measures 3 in on a plan drawn at 1/4" = 1'-0". What is its real length?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 12 ft
2. 0.75 ft
3. 3 ft
4. 24 ft
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. At 1/4" = 1'-0" the scale is 0.25 in per foot, so the real length is 3 / 0.25 = 12 ft. The answer 0.75 ft comes from multiplying instead of dividing, 3 ft ignores the scale entirely, and 24 ft would result from using 1/8" = 1'-0". Always divide the drawn length by the scale in inches per foot.

    **Concept Tested:** Scale and Dimensions

    **See:** [Scale and Dimensions](index.md#scale-and-dimensions)

#### 9. Why are construction drawings organized into disciplines with letter prefixes such as A, S, M, P, and E?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. So each sheet can be located quickly and cross-referenced by trade within a large set
2. So each discipline can use a different scale without having to state it on each sheet
3. So the building official can approve each discipline in a separate permit
4. So specifications can be removed from the drawings and issued separately
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Drawings are the main way a design is communicated to builders. Letter prefixes group sheets by discipline (architectural, structural, mechanical, plumbing, electrical), and numbered, titled, cross-referenced sheets let each trade find its work. Sheets still must state their scale, and the prefixes have nothing to do with permit approvals or specifications.

    **Concept Tested:** Construction Drawings

    **See:** [Construction Drawings](index.md#construction-drawings)

#### 10. Which difference between drawings and specifications is correct?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Drawings state the quality of materials and the standards they meet, while specifications show their locations
2. Drawings show where and how big, while specifications state what quality and by what standard
3. Drawings and specifications convey the same information in different formats
4. Drawings govern the work, while specifications are advisory only
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A drawing may show a 4 in slab, while the specification adds the required strength, air entrainment, and curing. Neither document tells the builder everything alone. They are complementary, not duplicates, and when they appear to disagree the contract documents state which governs, so the builder should ask instead of guessing.

    **Concept Tested:** Specifications

#### 11. On what basis are buildings classified into building types, also called occupancy?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. What material the frame is made from
2. How tall the building is
3. Who owns the building
4. What people do in the building
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Buildings are grouped by what people do in them, a classification called building types or occupancy. The type controls which code provisions apply, how many exits are required, and how much fire resistance is needed. Frame material, height, and ownership may influence design, but they are not the basis of the occupancy classification.

#### 12. A plan view is best described as which of the following?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A view of a building face seen straight on without perspective
2. A view of an imaginary vertical cut through the building that shows the layers of walls, floors, and roofs
3. A view from directly above a horizontal cut through a building, about four feet above the floor
4. An enlarged view of a small joint drawn at a large scale
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A plan is a horizontal cut seen from above and answers the question "what is where?" It shows walls, doors, windows, and room layouts. The straight-on face view is an elevation, the vertical cut is a section, and the enlarged joint view is a detail.

#### 13. Why do most Minnesota residential buildings use wood framing?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Wood framing is the only material allowed by code for buildings where people sleep in Minnesota
2. Wood framing offers low cost and fast construction for buildings where people live
3. Wood framing resists fire better than steel or concrete frames
4. Wood framing needs no protection from moisture in cold climates
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Residential construction covers buildings where people live and is the largest sector by number of projects. Wood framing dominates because of its cost and speed. It is not the only code-permitted option, wood does not outperform steel or concrete in fire resistance, and wood absorbs moisture, so moisture protection remains necessary.

#### 17. Which question does an elevation answer?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. What is located where on the floor?

3. What are the sizes of every door in the building?
4. What does the building face look like, and how tall is it?

#### 18. Why is a section the view used to show floor-to-floor heights and how roof and wall layers fit together?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It shows the building from directly above, so every wall is visible at once
2. It shows an imaginary vertical cut, so the interior is revealed as if the building were sliced open
3. It shows a table of repeated items, so every layer is listed by mark number
4. It shows the exterior face straight on, so all heights are measured from outside the building without cutting
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A section is a vertical cut through the building. Because the cut exposes the interior, it reveals vertical relationships such as foundation depth and the layering of walls and roofs. The view from above is a plan, the table is a schedule, and the straight-on exterior view is an elevation.

#### 19. Why do many building-science failures trace back to details rather than to overall plans?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A detail specifies how a joint is built, so poor drawing or building of it fails at the connection
2. A detail is drawn at a small scale, so dimensions on it are often misread
3. A detail replaces the specification entirely, so the quality requirements are missing from the sheets
4. A detail is only drawn for exterior walls, so interior joints are never checked
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Details are enlarged views, such as 3" = 1'-0", where the construction of a joint is specified. Failures like leaks and air gaps usually occur at connections between components, so a poorly drawn or poorly built detail is a common cause. Details use large scales, do not replace specifications, and are drawn for many kinds of joints.

    **See:** [Details](index.md#details)

#### 20. On a floor plan a door is shown only as the tag "D5." Where should a builder look to find that door's width, height, material, and fire rating?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>

2. In the symbol legend, which explains the tag
3. In the door schedule, which lists each door by mark number

</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A schedule is a table that lists repeated items with their sizes and properties, so drawings can show only the mark and avoid clutter. The door schedule links tag D5 to a row of specifics. The symbol legend explains what symbols mean, and the title block holds sheet information, not the properties of individual doors.

#### 22. Which description best defines building science?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The set of rules that classify buildings by occupancy and control permits
2. The craft of reading drawings and specifications to understand what each trade must build for a project
3. The study of how the construction industry is organized into trades
4. The applied discipline that explains how buildings carry load, move heat and moisture, and age
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Building science draws on physics, materials science, and engineering to link design and construction choices to performance during occupancy. That is why the book treats forces, heat, and moisture before specific construction systems. Occupancy rules, drawing interpretation, and industry organization are important topics, but they are not what the discipline explains.
