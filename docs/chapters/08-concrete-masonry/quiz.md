Test your understanding of the concepts in this chapter with these review questions, one for each of the 23 concepts covered.

#### 1. Why can concrete harden under water, and why does concrete that dries out too early stop gaining strength?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It hardens by evaporation of the mixing water, so a wet environment only slows the process down
2. It hardens by cooling of the cement paste, so added water speeds up the change of state
3. It hardens by hydration, a chemical reaction that needs moisture, so drying out stops the reaction
4. It hardens by compaction of the aggregates, so moisture only affects the surface finish of the slab
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Hydration is the combination of cement with water to form interlocking crystals that bind the mixture. It releases heat and continues for weeks, as long as moisture and moderate temperatures are available. Hydration is not drying, so water is an ingredient and not a solvent that must leave. Concrete that dries too early stops gaining strength, which is why the goal after the pour is keeping the water in.

    **See:** [Concrete](index.md#concrete)

#### 2. What is the role of cement in concrete?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is the granular material that makes up most of the volume and limits shrinkage of the paste
2. It is the binder that reacts with water to form a paste that glues the aggregates together
3. It is the steel that carries the tension the concrete cannot carry on its own in a beam
4. It is the chemical added in small doses to change the workability or the set time of a mix
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Portland cement is made by heating limestone and clay in a kiln and grinding the clinker with a little gypsum. It reacts with water in hydration, so cement is the glue and not the product. A sidewalk is concrete, never cement. The granular material is aggregate, the tension carrier is rebar, and the small-dose chemicals are admixtures.

    **Concept Tested:** Cement

    **See:** [Cement](index.md#cement)

#### 3. A #4 reinforcing bar has a cross-sectional area of 0.20 in² and a yield strength of 60 ksi. About how much tension can it carry at yield?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 0.33 kips
2. 120 kips
3. 60 kips
4. 12 kips
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Force equals area times stress: 0.20 in² × 60 ksi = 12 kips. The value 0.33 kips divides the stress by the area, 120 kips overstates by a factor of ten, and 60 kips simply repeats the yield strength. Rebar provides in a thin bar the tension capacity that concrete, at about 10 percent of its compressive strength, cannot provide at all.

    **Concept Tested:** Reinforcing Steel

#### 4. In masonry vocabulary, what is a wythe?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A vertical layer of masonry one unit thick
2. A horizontal layer of units laid in a wall
3. The pattern in which vertical joints are staggered
4. The thin facing of brick attached to a structural backup
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A course is a horizontal layer of units, a wythe is a vertical layer one unit thick, and the bond is the pattern in which vertical joints are staggered from course to course, commonly running bond. A thin facing attached to a structural backup is a veneer. Masonry is strong in compression and weak in tension, like concrete.

    **Concept Tested:** Masonry

#### 5. A wall form has a narrowest dimension of 8 in., rebar with a 3 in. clear spacing, and an adjoining slab 6 in. deep. Using the one-fifth, three-quarters, and one-third rules, what maximum aggregate size controls?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 2.0 in., from one-third of the 6 in. slab depth
2. 2.25 in., from three-quarters of the clear bar spacing
3. 3.0 in., from the clear spacing between the bars
4. 1.6 in., from one-fifth of the narrowest form dimension
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The limits are 8/5 = 1.6 in. for the form, 0.75 × 3 = 2.25 in. for the bars, and 6/3 = 2.0 in. for the slab. The smallest result controls, because a larger stone would jam and leave voids. Aggregates make up 60 to 75 percent of concrete's volume and should be hard, clean, and well graded.

#### 6. A concrete mix contains 600 lb of cement and 270 lb of water per cubic yard. What is its water-cement ratio?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 0.45
2. 2.22
3. 0.25
4. 0.55
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The water-cement ratio is the mass of water divided by the mass of cement: 270 / 600 = 0.45. The value 2.22 inverts the ratio, and 0.25 is only about what hydration itself needs by mass. A lower ratio produces stronger, denser, less permeable concrete, and exposed Minnesota concrete commonly needs 0.45 or lower, so adding water at the chute can push it out of specification.

    **Concept Tested:** Water-Cement Ratio

    **See:** [Water-Cement Ratio](index.md#water-cement-ratio)

#### 7. In the absolute volume method of mix design, why does the aggregate volume equal whatever remains after the other ingredients are accounted for?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The aggregate must always make up exactly 60 percent of the mix by mass
2. The masses of all ingredients must add up to 27 lb per cubic foot of concrete
3. The solid volumes of all ingredients must add up to exactly 27 ft³, one cubic yard
4. The cement and water must fill all of the space that the air does not occupy
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Absolute volume is bookkeeping: each ingredient's volume is its mass divided by its specific gravity times 62.4 lb/ft³, and the totals must equal 27 ft³. In the example, cement, water, and air take 8.56 ft³, so the aggregate fills 18.44 ft³. Aggregates usually occupy 60 to 75 percent of the volume, and the mix's mass totals thousands of pounds, not 27.

    **Concept Tested:** Concrete Mix Design

    **See:** [Concrete Mix Design](index.md#concrete-mix-design)

#### 8. Why do form designers either space ties closer together or use stronger ties near the bottom of a tall wall pour?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Concrete hardens first at the bottom, so the ties must resist its early shrinkage
2. Fresh concrete acts like a heavy liquid, so pressure on the form increases with depth
3. The top of the pour carries the weight of the crew, so the bottom must be braced
4. Vibration is applied only at the base, so the pressure there is higher than anywhere else
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Fresh concrete behaves like a fluid, so the pressure at the base of an 8 ft pour is at most about 150 × 8 = 1,200 psf, falling to zero at the top. A tie one foot above the base on a 2 ft grid could see about 4,200 lb. A form designed for less than full fluid pressure can burst. Slower pours and cooler concrete reduce the actual pressure.

    **Concept Tested:** Formwork

    **See:** [Formwork](index.md#formwork)

#### 9. Which three reasons explain why steel and concrete work well together in reinforced concrete?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Deformed bars bond to the concrete, both expand by nearly the same amount, and concrete's alkalinity protects steel from rust
2. Steel is lighter than concrete, both have the same tensile strength, and concrete's porosity absorbs the steel's heat
3. Steel stiffens the aggregates, both shrink at the same rate, and concrete's acidity cleans the steel surface of rust
4. Steel is cheaper than concrete, both resist compression equally, and concrete's moisture hydrates the steel bars
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Ribs on deformed bars create a strong bond, steel and concrete expand about 6 millionths per °F so they do not pull apart with the seasons, and concrete forms a protective film on steel as long as it stays sound. Concrete is weak in tension, steel is strong in tension, and the partnership assigns compression to concrete and tension to steel.

    **Concept Tested:** Reinforced Concrete

    **See:** [Reinforced Concrete](index.md#reinforced-concrete)

#### 10. Why should concrete be placed close to its final position instead of being dropped from a great height or pushed far?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Dropping it speeds hydration, so the concrete sets before it can be consolidated
2. Dropping or pushing it can segregate the mix, so the stone separates from the paste
3. Pushing it allows air bubbles to escape, so the concrete loses its freeze-thaw resistance
4. Dropping it adds water to the mix, so the water-cement ratio rises
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Placement delivers fresh concrete to the forms, spreads it, consolidates it, and finishes it. Segregation weakens the mix. Concrete is also normally discharged within about 90 minutes of mixing because hydration has begun. Consolidation with an internal vibrator releases trapped air, and under-vibration leaves honeycombing while over-vibration causes segregation.

    **Concept Tested:** Concrete Placement

    **See:** [Concrete Placement](index.md#concrete-placement)

#### 11. What is the main advantage of cast-in-place concrete compared with assembled precast pieces?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The concrete is cured in a factory, so its quality does not depend on site weather or labor
2. The forms are not required, so the pour can proceed immediately after the excavation is complete
3. The reinforcement is eliminated, because the concrete cures in its final position in the building
4. The pieces form a single monolithic structure that can carry moment connections and unusual shapes
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Cast-in-place concrete is placed and cured in its final location in forms built on site, which makes it monolithic. Its disadvantages are dependence on weather, site labor, and time, since each pour must cure before it is loaded. Factory curing describes precast, and forms and reinforcement are always needed for cast-in-place work.

    **Concept Tested:** Cast-in-Place Concrete

    **See:** [Cast-in-Place Concrete](index.md#cast-in-place-concrete)

#### 12. From the top down, what is the order of layers in a typical slab on grade in a cold climate?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Concrete slab, rigid insulation, compacted subgrade, vapor retarder, compacted granular base
2. Vapor retarder, concrete slab, compacted granular base, rigid insulation, compacted subgrade
3. Concrete slab, vapor retarder, rigid insulation where needed, granular base, compacted subgrade
4. Compacted subgrade, compacted granular base, concrete slab, rigid insulation, vapor retarder
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A slab on grade rests directly on soil. The vapor retarder sits beneath the slab to block ground moisture, rigid insulation keeps the ground from drawing heat away, and the compacted granular base rests on the compacted subgrade prepared during site work. A suspended slab, by contrast, spans between beams or walls as a structural member and needs reinforcement.

#### 13. Why is mortar deliberately made a little weaker and more flexible than the masonry units?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Weaker mortar bonds better to the units, so it prevents any cracking from forming in the wall
2. Any cracking then occurs in the joint, which can be repaired by repointing, not by replacing units
3. Weaker mortar hydrates faster, so the wall can be loaded sooner after construction is complete
4. Flexible mortar absorbs all of the wall's compressive load, so the units themselves can be thinner
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Mortar bonds the units and seals the joints, and standard types M, S, N, and O range from 2,500 psi down to 350 psi minimum. Type N is common above grade, and Type S is used where higher bond or lateral strength is needed. A softer joint localizes cracks where they can be tooled and repointed. Mortar does not carry all the load, and weakness is a design choice, not a way to prevent cracking.

    **Concept Tested:** Mortar

    **See:** [Mortar](index.md#mortar)

#### 14. A modular brick face needs about 6.75 bricks per square foot of wall. About how many bricks are needed for a 100 ft² wall, before any allowance for breakage?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 675 bricks
2. 15 bricks
3. 1,440 bricks
4. 6,750 bricks
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Multiply the bricks per square foot by the wall area: 100 × 6.75 = 675. The value 15 divides the area by 6.75, 1,440 treats the wall as 144 ft², and 6,750 is a decimal-place error. A typical estimate then adds an allowance such as 5 percent for breakage. Minnesota brick exposed to the weather should be Grade SW, severe weathering.

#### 15. Which description of a standard concrete masonry unit is correct?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Nominally 4 in. by 2⅔ in. by 8 in., with an actual size ⅜ in. more in each direction, solid sides, and no cores
2. Nominally 8 in. by 8 in. by 16 in., with an actual size ⅜ in. more in each direction and no hollow cores
3. Nominally 8 by 8 by 16 in., with an actual size ⅜ in. less in each direction and hollow cores that can be grouted
4. Nominally 12 in. by 12 in. by 24 in., with an actual size equal to the nominal size and a solid face
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. CMUs are hollow or solid units of molded concrete. The actual size is ⅜ in. smaller than nominal in each direction so that the unit plus its mortar joint fits the module. The cores reduce weight and can be filled with grout and rebar, and the thick sides are called face shells. The 4 × 2⅔ × 8 module describes brick with its joint.

#### 16. Why is grout made much more fluid than ordinary concrete, with a slump of 8 to 11 in.?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It must evaporate quickly from the cores so that the units can be loaded on the same day
2. It must stay on the surface of the wall to seal the mortar joints against wind-driven rain
3. It must replace the mortar, so it fills each joint without any other bonding agent being used
4. It must flow into narrow block cores and around bars to bond the steel and add strength
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Grout is a concrete-like mixture of cement, fine aggregate or pea gravel, and water poured into the cores of concrete blocks or between wythes. It is consolidated by vibrator or puddling and placed in lifts of limited height so the pressure does not blow out the block face shells. Mortar bonds units and seals joints, while grout bonds steel and strengthens cells.

    **Concept Tested:** Grout

    **See:** [Grout](index.md#grout)

#### 17. A slab is finished on a hot, dry, windy afternoon, and fine cracks appear within hours. What practice would best prevent this problem?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Let the surface dry quickly so that the excess water leaves the slab before hydration begins
2. Add water to the surface as it stiffens so the finish stays workable for the finishing crew
3. Wait for the 28-day cylinder results to come back before beginning any curing of the slab
4. Keep the surface moist from the start with curing compound, wet burlap, or plastic sheeting
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Hot, dry, or windy conditions pull water from the surface faster than it rises from within, causing plastic shrinkage cracks. Curing keeps freshly placed concrete moist and in a suitable temperature range so hydration continues and the concrete reaches its design strength. Moist curing is commonly required for at least seven days, and adding surface water or waiting for tests does not protect the slab.

    **Concept Tested:** Curing

    **See:** [Curing](index.md#curing)

#### 18. A contractor wants to core-drill an unmarked post-tensioned slab without locating the tendons first. Why is this dangerous?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The tendons carry no load, so drilling through them only damages the slab's insulation layer
2. The tendons hold great stored energy, so cutting one can cause a violent release and weaken the slab
3. The tendons are made of brittle cast iron, so drilling shatters the concrete around them
4. The tendons are filled with grout under pressure, so drilling releases wet concrete into the room below
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. In post-tensioning, high-strength strands run through ducts and are stretched with jacks after the concrete hardens, which keeps the concrete in compression before loads arrive. That stored energy is why an unmarked post-tensioned slab must never be cut or drilled until tendons are located. Prestressing allows longer spans and thinner slabs, as in parking structures and office floors.

    **Concept Tested:** Prestressed Concrete

    **See:** [Prestressed Concrete](index.md#prestressed-concrete)

#### 19. What are the main advantages and limits of precast concrete?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Pieces are cast in their final positions on site, but weather and labor affect quality and schedule
2. Pieces are always unreinforced, but they are cheaper than any cast-in-place alternative at any size
3. Factory conditions give precise forms and consistent quality, but size limits and critical connections apply
4. Pieces are poured on the building's own slab, but they cannot be lifted by a crane or tilted up
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Precast concrete is cast in a plant and lifted into place, so it has precise forms and controlled curing regardless of weather, and many pieces are also prestressed. Hollow-core planks, double-tee beams, wall panels, beams, and columns are common. Connections between pieces, made by welded plates, bolts, or grouted joints, are critical details. Casting on the building's slab describes tilt-up construction.

    **Concept Tested:** Precast Concrete

    **See:** [Precast Concrete](index.md#precast-concrete)

#### 20. Why is reinforced masonry the standard way to build a masonry shear wall?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Unreinforced masonry is brittle and weak against sideways forces, and grouted bars let the wall resist tension and shear
2. Unreinforced masonry is too heavy for lateral loads, and grouted bars reduce the wall's weight enough to resist them
3. Unreinforced masonry cannot resist compression, and grouted bars carry the vertical load of the floors above
4. Unreinforced masonry is not fire resistant, and grouted bars supply the fire-resistance rating of the wall
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Reinforced masonry places steel bars or wires in grout-filled cells or joints. Vertical bars go at corners, openings, and regular spacing; bond beams tie courses together; and joint reinforcement controls cracking. Masonry is strong in compression but weak in tension, so steel is needed where wind or seismic forces are significant. Masonry is already heavy and fire resistant.

    **Concept Tested:** Reinforced Masonry

    **See:** [Reinforced Masonry](index.md#reinforced-masonry)

#### 21. How is stone masonry used in modern buildings?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Mainly as thick walls that carry load by their weight alone, as in historic buildings and old mills
2. Mainly as rough rubble placed in the forms to replace the aggregates in a concrete mix
3. Mainly as a thin veneer anchored to a backup wall, with anchorage designed for the stone's weight
4. Mainly as lightweight panels that weigh less than 50 pcf and are glued to the wall surface
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Historic buildings used very thick stone walls carrying load by weight, but modern buildings use stone mostly as veneer. Stone weighs roughly 150 to 170 pcf, so anchorage must be designed for that weight, and the stone must be chosen for its resistance to freezing when wet. Common building stones include granite, limestone, and sandstone, and Minnesota has quarried limestone and granite for generations.

#### 22. Using the rule of 2 to 3 times the slab thickness in inches, expressed in feet, what spacing of control joints is appropriate for a 6 in. slab?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 6 to 9 ft
2. 24 to 36 ft
3. 2 to 3 ft
4. 12 to 18 ft
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The rule multiplies the thickness in inches by 2 to 3 and reads the answer in feet: 6 × 2 = 12 ft and 6 × 3 = 18 ft. A control joint is a deliberately weakened line, sawn to about one-quarter of the slab depth within hours of finishing, that directs shrinkage cracking to a straight, controlled line. A restrained slab would otherwise crack at random.

    **Concept Tested:** Control Joints

    **See:** [Control Joints](index.md#control-joints)

#### 23. What does the rule "clay expands and concrete shrinks" imply for joints in a brick veneer over a concrete block backup?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Brick veneer needs control joints directing its shrinkage cracks, while concrete block needs full-depth expansion joints
2. Brick veneer needs full-depth expansion joints, while concrete block needs control joints directing shrinkage cracks
3. Neither material needs joints, because both change size by the same amount with temperature and moisture
4. Both materials need only expansion joints, because both of them shrink as they dry and then stay stable
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Clay brick expands slowly over its life as it absorbs moisture, while concrete products shrink as they dry. An expansion joint is a full-depth gap with compressible filler that lets two parts move independently, and it divides long runs of brick veneer. A control joint is a partial-depth weakened line that directs shrinkage cracks, which suits concrete and block.

    **Concept Tested:** Expansion Joints

    **See:** [Expansion Joints](index.md#expansion-joints)
