## The Design Process

The **design process** is the iterative method by which a project team converts an owner's needs into a documented solution that others can price, permit, and build. Its inputs are the owner's goals and budget, the characteristics of the site, and the requirements of the building code. Its outputs are the drawings and specifications introduced in Chapter 1. Between input and output lies a long chain of decisions, and the quality of the building depends on how well those decisions are made and in what order.

Design is *iterative* because no one can choose the best answer on the first try. The team repeats a four-step cycle: define the problem, generate several candidate solutions, evaluate each against stated criteria, and select and refine one. The cycle then repeats at a finer level of detail. The first pass decides where the building sits on the site, a later pass decides how the roof is framed, and a later one still decides which breaker feeds a particular outlet. Design is also *convergent*, since each decision removes alternatives and constrains the next. Once the roof is framed in wood, for example, the options for the ceiling, the ductwork, and the lighting shrink accordingly.

Chapter 1 observed that a building supports, separates, serves, and protects, and that these jobs compete. The design process is where those conflicts are resolved on paper before they are resolved in materials. The table below restates the four steps with a Riverbend example for each.

| Step | Question the team asks | Riverbend example |
|------|------------------------|-------------------|
| Define | What must be solved, and what limits apply? | The multipurpose room needs daylight without overheating or heat loss |
| Generate | What are the plausible options? | Large clerestory windows, a few modest windows, or skylights |
| Evaluate | How does each option score on the criteria? | Daylight, winter heat loss, glare, cost |
| Select and refine | Which option do we carry forward, and how do we improve it? | Modest south-facing clerestory with shading and high-performance glazing |

**Worked example: iterating on daylight.** The owner wants the multipurpose room to feel bright. The architect first sketches three options and rates each on four criteria. Large clerestory windows score best on daylight but worst on winter heat loss and glare. Skylights score well on daylight but worry the team about leaks and snow. A few modest windows are cheap and tight but leave the room dim. The team selects a narrow south-facing clerestory, then checks it with the mechanical engineer, who reports that the added heat loss is acceptable if the glazing is high-performance. The solution is neither the first idea nor the best on any single criterion. It is the best balance of all four, reached after two passes through the cycle.

### The Architect

The **architect** is a licensed design professional who leads the design team and takes primary responsibility for the design of the building. Licensing exists because design errors endanger the public: a state licensing board verifies that the architect has the education, experience, and examination record to be trusted with life-safety decisions. The architect typically holds the design contract with the owner, and the architect's seal on the drawings states that a responsible professional prepared them.

The architect rarely designs everything alone. Structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and civil engineers join the team as consultants, each responsible for the systems in their own discipline, and the architect coordinates their work so that the drawings agree with each other. The electrical designer, who is the focus of later chapters, works within this team, either as part of an electrical engineering firm or as part of a contractor's design-build services. During construction the architect usually continues in a second role as the owner's representative, which we examine under Construction Administration.

## The Construction Process

The **construction process** is the coordinated sequence of buying, scheduling, assembling, inspecting, and documenting that turns the construction documents into a physical building. Design is carried out with drawings, and the cost of a mistake is an eraser mark. Construction is carried out with concrete, steel, and lumber, and the cost of a mistake is demolition and rework. This asymmetry explains why the industry invests so heavily in planning before the first shovel enters the ground.

Three flows must be managed simultaneously on every project. *Materials* must be ordered, delivered, and stored so that they arrive when needed and not before. *Labor* must be scheduled so that the right trades are on site in the right order. *Information* must move accurately among owner, designers, and builders, because a trade that works from an outdated drawing builds the wrong thing. The contractor is responsible for coordinating all three flows. The table below lists the main contractor responsibilities that we examine in this chapter.

| Responsibility | What it means on site |
|----------------|-----------------------|
| Procurement | Buying materials and hiring subcontractors, including long-lead items |
| Scheduling | Sequencing trades and tracking progress against the plan |
| Coordination | Resolving conflicts among trades before work is covered up |
| Safety | Protecting workers and the public on the site |
| Quality | Building to the documents and passing inspections |
| Documentation | Keeping records of submittals, questions, changes, and payments |

**Worked example: one day on the Riverbend slab.** On the morning of the slab pour, the general contractor confirms that concrete trucks are scheduled for 7:00. The reinforcing steel has been placed, and the building inspector has approved it the afternoon before. The electrical subcontractor's underground conduit is in place, and the plumbing subcontractor's underground pipes have been tested and inspected. Once the concrete covers those pipes and conduits, a missing sleeve means saw-cutting the slab. The contractor therefore walks the site with each trade before the first truck arrives. A pour that appears to be a single task is actually the end point of five trades' work, and the contractor's real job is to make sure all of them have finished before it starts.

### The General Contractor

The **general contractor** (GC) is the firm that holds the prime contract with the owner to construct the project and is responsible for all of the work, including work done by others. The GC provides a project manager, who handles the paperwork and money, and a superintendent, who runs the site day to day. The GC decides *means and methods*, meaning how the work is carried out and in what order, because the construction documents describe the finished result and not the process of getting there. This responsibility is why the architect, who observes the work, does not supervise it.

### The Subcontractor

A **subcontractor** is a specialty firm hired by the general contractor to perform a portion of the work, such as concrete, framing, roofing, plumbing, mechanical, or electrical. On most commercial buildings, subcontractors perform the majority of the physical work, and the GC acts as the manager who coordinates them. A subcontractor has a contract with the GC and none with the owner, so questions and payments flow through the GC. Some trades are regulated; in Minnesota, for example, electrical work is performed by electricians and electrical contractors licensed through the state. Large subcontractors may hire their own sub-tier firms, and the general contractor remains responsible for the entire chain.

## Project Phases

**Project phases** are the standard stages through which a building project moves from first idea to occupied building. Each phase answers a different question, produces defined deliverables, and ends with the owner's approval before the next phase begins. The approval, often called a phase gate, protects the owner and the team from building on decisions that have not been confirmed.

The eight phases used in this chapter are programming, schematic design, design development, construction documents, bidding and procurement, construction (with construction administration), commissioning, and occupancy and closeout. The first four are the *design phases*, in which the building exists only on paper. The last four are the *construction and delivery phases*, in which the building takes physical form and is handed over. The following sections define each phase in turn, so the table below is best read as a map to return to rather than a lesson on its own.

| Phase | Main question | Principal deliverable | Electrical designer's contribution |
|-------|---------------|-----------------------|------------------------------------|
| Programming | What does the owner need? | Written program, budget | Identify special power needs |
| Schematic design | What overall form works? | Site plan, floor plans | Estimate service size |
| Design development | How will it be built? | Refined plans, system selections | Locate panels, lay out lighting |
| Construction documents | Exactly what is to be built? | Drawings, specifications | Produce E-series sheets and Division 26 specifications |
| Bidding and procurement | Who will build it, and for how much? | Signed construction contract | Answer bidder questions |
| Construction | Is it built as designed? | Completed work | Review submittals, answer questions |
| Commissioning | Does it work as intended? | Test reports | Verify controls and lighting |
| Occupancy and closeout | Is it ready to use? | Certificate of occupancy, records | Deliver record drawings |

The reason for the phase structure is the relationship between decisions and the cost of changing them. Early in a project, the team can still influence nearly everything about the building, and changes cost almost nothing because they are lines on paper. As the project proceeds, the team's influence falls while the cost of changing course rises, since each decision is built on by the next and finally embodied in physical work.

**Worked example: moving one wall.** Suppose the owner of Riverbend asks to enlarge a classroom by moving a wall four feet. During schematic design, the architect moves a line on one floor plan, which takes an hour. During construction documents, the change must be coordinated across the architectural, structural, mechanical, plumbing, and electrical sheets, and may take several days of work by several people. After the wall has been framed and the electrical conduit installed, the change means demolishing finished work, re-routing wiring, revising the drawings, and issuing a change order, a process that could take weeks and a considerable sum. The wall is the same four feet in every case. Only the phase differs.

### The Project Owner

The **project owner** is the person or organization that commissions the project, pays for it, and has the authority to make final decisions about its goals, budget, and schedule. Owners may be private companies, nonprofit organizations, government agencies, or individuals. Public owners are usually bound by procurement laws that require competitive bidding, while private owners have more freedom to choose how they select a builder.

The owner sets the direction at the start and approves each phase gate along the way. The most common source of delay and expense on projects is slow or reversed owner decisions, which is why a team should identify at the outset a single person with authority to speak for the owner. For Riverbend, the owner is a nonprofit organization, and its executive director holds that role. Experienced owners also write down the performance they expect from the building, in a document often called the *owner's project requirements*, which the commissioning process later uses as the test of success.

## Programming

**Programming** is the first design phase, in which the team documents what the building must do before anyone draws what it looks like. The result is the *program*, a written statement of the spaces required, their sizes, how they relate to each other, the performance expected of each, the budget, and the schedule. The architect gathers this information through interviews with the people who will use the building, visits to similar facilities, and analysis of the site.

A program is useful only if it can be checked, so it is expressed in numbers. Each space gets an area, and the areas are added to give the *net area*, which is the usable floor space inside rooms. A building also needs corridors, walls, mechanical rooms, and shafts, which are not part of any room. The ratio of net area to the total building area is the efficiency, and the gross area follows from it:

\[ \text{gross area} = \frac{\text{net area}}{\text{efficiency}} \]

**Worked example: sizing Riverbend.** The owner lists the spaces below. The net area totals 6,300 ft². Assume an efficiency of 0.70, a plausible value for a building of this type, so the gross area is \( 6{,}300 / 0.70 = 9{,}000 \) ft². With an illustrative budget of \$4.5 million, the target is \( 4{,}500{,}000 / 9{,}000 = \$500 \) per gross square foot. If similar buildings in the region have been costing more than that, the team learns it now, while the cure is to trim the program or raise the budget, rather than after the drawings are finished.

| Space | Count | Area each (ft²) | Total (ft²) |
|-------|-------|-----------------|-------------|
| Multipurpose room | 1 | 2,400 | 2,400 |
| Classrooms | 3 | 600 | 1,800 |
| Kitchen | 1 | 500 | 500 |
| Offices | 3 | 120 | 360 |
| Storage | 1 | 340 | 340 |
| Lobby and restrooms | 1 | 900 | 900 |
| **Net total** | | | **6,300** |

The electrical designer contributes at this stage by asking what equipment the spaces will contain. A commercial kitchen, a stage lighting system, or planned electric vehicle charging all affect the size of the electrical service, and each is far easier to include now than to add later.

## Schematic Design

**Schematic design** is the phase in which the architect explores the overall form of the building: where it sits on the site, its shape and height, and how the rooms are arranged. The deliverables are a site plan, preliminary floor plans, rough elevations or a simple massing model, a short description of the proposed structure and building systems, and a preliminary cost estimate. The drawings are sketchy by intention, since the purpose is to compare ideas and not to specify materials.

Good schematic design presents the owner with a small number of genuinely different options and the evidence to choose among them. The shape of the building matters because the exterior surface, which includes walls, roof, and floor, is where heat escapes, water enters, and costs accumulate. A long, thin building has more exterior wall for the same floor area than a compact one, but it may also have better daylight and simpler structure.

**Worked example: three options for Riverbend.** The architect develops three schemes and brings them to the owner with a comparison.

| Option | Description | Strengths | Concerns |
|--------|-------------|-----------|----------|
| A | One-story rectangular bar, 120 ft by 75 ft | Simplest structure, easy accessibility, lowest cost | Large roof and foundation, less daylight in the center |
| B | L-shaped one-story building around a south-facing courtyard | Daylight, outdoor space for programs | More exterior wall, higher cost |
| C | Compact two-story block | Smaller roof and footprint, saves site area | Needs an elevator and stairs, adds cost and complexity |

The owner, balancing the budget against the wish for outdoor space, chooses a hybrid of A and B: a one-story bar with a small south-facing entry court. At the end of the phase, the owner signs off on the plan, and the team agrees not to revisit the basic layout without a formal decision. The electrical designer uses the approved scheme to estimate the building's electrical demand and to contact the electric utility about the service connection, since a utility may need months to deliver a transformer.

## Design Development

**Design development** is the phase in which the team takes the approved schematic design and decides how it will actually be built. Structure, enclosure, mechanical, plumbing, electrical, and fire-protection systems are chosen and sized, materials and assemblies are selected, dimensions are fixed, and equipment locations are coordinated. The deliverables are larger-scale plans, sections, and elevations, outline specifications naming the principal materials and products, and an updated cost estimate.

Design development is where the building's science is largely settled. The choices made here, such as the type of roof structure, the insulation in the walls, or the kind of heating system, determine how the building will carry loads and manage heat, air, and moisture for decades. The end of the phase is sometimes called a *design freeze*: after the owner approves, changes become costly because the next phase will turn every decision into detailed drawings.

**Worked example: a long-span room at Riverbend.** The multipurpose room needs a 40 ft clear span with no columns. The structural engineer compares glued-laminated wood beams, steel joists, and wood trusses for cost, weight, and fire behavior, and the team selects glued-laminated beams. The mechanical engineer decides to place the rooftop heating-and-cooling unit near the kitchen, so that the ducts stay short, and checks that the roof can carry its weight. The electrical designer puts the main switchboard in a room adjacent to the kitchen and lays out the gym lighting. The updated estimate arrives at \$4.77 million, which is 6 percent over the \$4.5 million budget. The team now performs *value engineering*, a systematic search for savings that keep the required performance, and trades expensive floor finishes in the classrooms for durable ones that cost less. The structure, the enclosure, and the systems stay as designed.

## Construction Documents

**Construction documents** are the final drawings and specifications that define the work in enough detail for contractors to price it, for the building official to review it for a permit, and for tradespeople to build it. They are the legal core of the construction contract. The drawings are organized by discipline, using the sheet prefixes introduced in Chapter 1 (A, S, M, P, and E), and the written specifications are organized by division. Together with the conditions of the contract and the bidding forms, they make up a bound set often called the *project manual*.

Three audiences read the documents, and each has a different need. Bidders need quantities and quality standards to set a price. The building official needs evidence that the design meets the code, and Chapter 17 treats the permit and plan-review process in detail. The builders need unambiguous instructions. A gap or a conflict in the documents does not disappear; it turns up during construction as a question, a delay, or a change order.

For this reason the team conducts a *coordination review* before the documents are released, in which the drawings from each discipline are overlaid to look for conflicts. Coordination is among the most valuable work done in the phase.

**Worked example: a clash found on paper.** During the coordination review, the engineer notes that a 24 in deep supply duct on the mechanical drawings passes through the same space as a glued-laminated beam on the structural drawings. On site, this discovery would stop the framers, trigger a question to the design team, and probably require a change order. On paper, it costs a revised sheet and a half-hour of coordination: the duct is shifted six inches and routed beneath the beam. A set for a building the size of Riverbend might contain on the order of 60 sheets and several hundred pages of specifications, so a systematic review is the only way to find such conflicts reliably.

## Bidding and Procurement

**Bidding and procurement** is the phase in which the owner selects the builder and secures the materials, equipment, and subcontracted trades needed for the project. *Bidding* is a structured competition in which contractors submit prices for the work described in the documents. *Procurement* is the broader task of buying, and it includes ordering *long-lead items*, which are products such as switchgear, elevators, or custom windows that take months to manufacture and can control the schedule.

The usual bidding sequence is as follows.

1. The owner advertises the project or invites selected contractors to bid.
2. Contractors review the documents, and may attend a pre-bid conference to ask questions.
3. The architect issues *addenda*, which are written changes or clarifications to the documents during bidding.
4. Bidders submit sealed bids on a specified date, and the bids are opened and recorded in a *bid tabulation*.
5. The owner evaluates the bids, and awards the contract to the lowest bidder who is both *responsive* (the bid follows the instructions) and *responsible* (the firm has the capability and record to finish the job).

Many bid forms also include *alternates*, which are priced options the owner may add to or subtract from the base bid, depending on what the budget allows. Public owners are generally required by law to advertise their projects and to award them to the lowest responsive and responsible bidder.

**Worked example: bids for Riverbend.** Three general contractors bid. Alternate 1 adds an upgraded exterior wall finish to the entry court. The owner decides after the bids are opened whether to accept the alternate.

| Bidder | Base bid | Alternate 1 | Base plus Alternate 1 |
|--------|----------|-------------|-----------------------|
| Contractor A | \$4,380,000 | +\$90,000 | \$4,470,000 |
| Contractor B | \$4,400,000 | +\$30,000 | \$4,430,000 |
| Contractor C | \$4,520,000 | +\$50,000 | \$4,570,000 |

Without the alternate, Contractor A has the lowest price. With it, Contractor B does, because B's price for the extra work is lower. Public owners therefore state in advance how alternates will be used to determine the low bidder, so that the result does not depend on the order in which bids are opened. Riverbend accepts the alternate, awards the contract to Contractor B at \$4,430,000, and the project moves into construction.

## Project Delivery Methods

Bidding is only one of several ways to organize a project. A **delivery method** is the contractual arrangement that determines who is under contract with the owner, when the builder joins the team, and how the price is set. Each method allocates risk, speed, and control differently. The four methods covered here run from the traditional and sequential to the highly collaborative.

### Design-Bid-Build

**Design-bid-build** (DBB) is the traditional method, in which the three activities happen in strict sequence. The owner first hires an architect, who completes the design. The owner then bids the finished documents to contractors and awards a construction contract to the low bidder. The builder joins the project only after the design is done. The owner has two separate contracts, one with the architect and one with the general contractor, and the two firms have no contract with each other.

The method's strength is its clarity and its competitive price: every bidder prices the same complete documents, and the owner knows the cost before construction starts. Its weaknesses follow from the same sequence. The builder's knowledge of costs and methods is not available during design, the schedule is long because nothing is built until the design is finished and bid, and the architect and contractor may disagree when problems arise.

**Worked example: Riverbend as design-bid-build.** Riverbend was bid in the Bidding and Procurement section above, so it is a DBB project. Its timeline runs 10 months for design, 1 month for bidding, and 10 months for construction, for a total of 21 months from the start of design to completion. The owner gets a firm price of \$4,430,000 before construction begins, and the team uses the documents as the standard when disputes arise.

### Design-Build

**Design-build** (DB) is a delivery method in which the owner signs a single contract with one entity, usually a contractor, who is responsible for both design and construction. The architect and engineers work as part of the design-build team. The method gives the owner a single point of responsibility and allows construction to begin on early work, such as the foundation, while the rest of the design is still being completed. These overlaps often shorten the schedule, and Riverbend might finish in about 17 rather than 21 months under this method, an illustrative saving. The trade-off is that the owner has less independent oversight of the design, and the price is set earlier, when less is known.

### Construction Manager at Risk

**Construction manager at risk** (CMAR) is a method in which the owner hires a construction manager during design, as an adviser, and then the same firm agrees to build the project for a *guaranteed maximum price* (GMP), which is a ceiling price that the construction manager must not exceed. The owner holds separate contracts with the architect and the construction manager, so the design stays independent. The builder contributes cost and schedule advice early, which helps the team stay within budget, and the construction manager bears the risk of overruns above the guaranteed price. The firm is chosen on qualifications and fee rather than lowest price alone.

### Integrated Project Delivery

**Integrated project delivery** (IPD) is a collaborative method in which the owner, architect, contractor, and key subcontractors sign a single multi-party agreement and share both risk and reward according to the project's results. Key trades such as electrical and mechanical join the team at the start, and decisions are made jointly. The aim is to move decisions earlier, which, as the cost-of-change curve showed, is when they are cheapest. IPD demands trust and early commitment from every party, and it is less common than the other methods. It extends CMAR by bringing the designers and builders under one set of shared incentives rather than separate contracts.

The table below summarizes the four methods that the preceding sections defined.

| Method | Owner's contracts | Builder joins | Price basis | Typical strength |
|--------|-------------------|---------------|-------------|------------------|
| Design-bid-build | Architect and contractor, separately | After design is complete | Lowest bid | Price clarity, competition |
| Design-build | One design-builder | At the start | Negotiated lump sum | Speed, single responsibility |
| CMAR | Architect and construction manager, separately | During design | Guaranteed maximum price | Early cost advice |
| Integrated project delivery | One multi-party agreement | At the start, with key trades | Shared target cost | Collaboration, early decisions |

## Construction Administration

**Construction administration** (CA) is the set of services that the architect and engineers perform during construction to confirm that the work conforms to the construction documents and to administer the contract between owner and contractor. It includes periodic site visits, review of submittals, answers to questions from the contractor, evaluation of proposed changes, review of the contractor's applications for payment, and a determination of when the work is substantially complete.

CA has a built-in limit that students often miss: the design team *observes* the work but does not *direct* it. The contractor remains responsible for means and methods, for the schedule, and for safety on the site. The architect's visits are periodic, and their purpose is to protect the owner by checking that what is being built is consistent with what was designed, not to inspect every detail.

Money is one of the most routine CA tasks. Each month the contractor submits an *application for payment*, which lists the percent of each part of the work completed. The owner usually withholds *retainage*, a percentage of each payment (commonly 5 to 10 percent, set by the contract) that is held back until the work is complete, as an incentive to finish and correct defects. The architect reviews the application, visits the site to check that it is accurate, and certifies the amount due.

**Worked example: a Riverbend payment application.** The contract is for \$4,430,000, retainage is 5 percent, and previous payments total \$600,000. By the end of the fourth month, the architect agrees with the contractor that 30 percent of the work is in place:

\[ \text{earned to date} = 0.30 \times 4{,}430{,}000 = \$1{,}329{,}000 \]

\[ \text{payment due} = 1{,}329{,}000 - (0.05 \times 1{,}329{,}000) - 600{,}000 = \$662{,}550 \]

The retainage withheld to date is \$66,450. The contractor does not receive that amount until the work is complete.

Three kinds of paperwork carry most of the information between the contractor and the design team during construction. We define each in turn and then look at how they travel.

### Submittals

**Submittals** are documents, such as shop drawings, product data sheets, and physical samples, that the contractor sends to the design team for review before ordering or installing certain products. Their purpose is to confirm that the contractor has understood the design and that the proposed product satisfies the specification. A subcontractor prepares the submittal, the general contractor checks it, and the architect or engineer returns it with a status such as approved, approved as noted, revise and resubmit, or rejected. For example, the electrical subcontractor submits the manufacturer's data and dimensions for the main switchboard, and the electrical engineer checks that its ratings and size match the design before it is ordered.

### Requests for Information

A **request for information** (RFI) is a written question from the contractor to the design team, asking for clarification when the documents are unclear, incomplete, or in conflict. The design team answers in writing, and the answer may become a change to the documents. RFIs create a record, which protects both sides: the question, the date, and the answer are on file if a dispute later arises about what was intended.

!!! mascot-tip "Beau's Tip: Send the Answer With the Question"
    ![Beau giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Before you send an RFI, cite the sheet and detail number, state the conflict in one sentence, and propose how you would resolve it. A question that arrives with a suggested answer usually comes back faster, because the designer can simply say yes or no.

### Change Orders

A **change order** is a written modification to the construction contract that adjusts the scope of work, the contract price, the contract time, or any combination of the three, and is signed by the owner, the architect, and the contractor. Changes arise for three common reasons: the owner wants something different, the site holds a surprise such as poor soil, or the documents contain an error or omission. Some change is normal, but a project with many changes is often a project with weak documents or late owner decisions.

**Worked example: soft soil at Riverbend.** While excavating for the footings, the crew finds a pocket of unsuitable fill beneath the kitchen wing. The contractor submits an RFI, and the geotechnical engineer prescribes the removal of the fill and its replacement with compacted gravel. The contractor prices the work at \$38,000 and four additional days, and the three parties sign a change order. The contract sum rises to \$4,468,000, and the contract time grows by four days.

!!! mascot-warning "Watch Out: The Verbal Go-Ahead"
    ![Beau warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A common trap is starting extra work on a verbal go-ahead from someone at the site, because the change looks small and the schedule is tight. The risk is that no one has agreed on the price, so a dispute later may leave the contractor unpaid. Wait for the signed change order or a written directive, even for small changes.

The paths that RFIs, submittals, and change orders take among the project participants are worth studying together, because the same few channels carry them all.

## Construction Sequencing

**Construction sequencing** is the planning of the order in which the activities of a project are carried out. The order is not arbitrary. Some activities must follow others for physical reasons, since walls cannot be framed before the floor exists. Other activities are ordered to protect work already done, to allow inspections before work is covered, or to avoid trades blocking each other. A typical wood-framed building follows this general order: site preparation and excavation; footings and foundation walls; underground utilities; slab and floor structure; wall and roof framing; roof covering and windows, which make the building *dried in* or weather-tight; rough-in of mechanical, plumbing, and electrical systems; insulation and wall finishes; interior finishes; and final testing.

In Minnesota, the calendar shapes the sequence. The contractor tries to complete the foundation and the dry-in before hard winter, so that interior work can proceed in heated, protected space. A project that cannot be dried in before freezing weather faces temporary heat, cold-weather concrete practices, and delays, all of which increase cost.

### The Critical Path

The **critical path** is the longest chain of dependent activities from the start of the project to its finish, and it determines the shortest possible duration of the project. A delay in any critical activity delays the whole project. Other activities have *float*, meaning the amount of time they can slip without delaying completion. Planners find the critical path by calculating each activity's earliest start and earliest finish from its duration and the activities that must precede it.

**Worked example: the Riverbend structure.** Consider the following eight activities, with durations in working days.

| Activity | Duration | Must follow | Earliest start | Earliest finish | Float |
|----------|----------|-------------|----------------|-----------------|-------|
| A. Excavate | 4 | None | 0 | 4 | 0 |
| B. Pour footings | 3 | A | 4 | 7 | 0 |
| C. Foundation walls | 5 | B | 7 | 12 | 0 |
| D. Underground utilities | 4 | C | 12 | 16 | 0 |
| E. Pour slab | 2 | D | 16 | 18 | 0 |
| F. Order roof trusses | 15 | None | 0 | 15 | 9 |
| G. Frame walls | 6 | E | 18 | 24 | 0 |
| H. Set trusses | 3 | G and F | 24 | 27 | 0 |

The activity H cannot start until both G and F are finished, so it starts at day 24, the later of day 24 and day 15. The critical path is A, B, C, D, E, G, H, a total of 27 days. The truss order has 9 days of float, so a delivery delay of up to 9 days has no effect on completion. A delay of 10 days would push H's start to day 25, and the order would become critical. This is why a planner watches long-lead procurement carefully.

## Commissioning

**Commissioning** is the systematic process of verifying that a building's systems have been installed and perform according to the owner's requirements and the design intent. Testing begins during construction and continues after the systems are complete. A commissioning agent, who may be independent of the contractor, reviews the design, observes installation, and then directs *functional tests*, in which each system is operated through its modes to show that it responds correctly. The building systems introduced in Chapter 1 depend on each other, and commissioning is the point at which that interdependence is tested.

**Worked example: testing at Riverbend.** The commissioning agent tests the heating system by lowering the thermostat setpoint and confirming that the rooftop unit starts, the dampers open, and the temperature responds. For the electrical system, the agent interrupts the normal power to confirm that emergency lights switch on and the exit signs remain lit. A common finding is a control sequence that was programmed incorrectly, for example lighting that remains on in an empty classroom. The test produces a list of deficiencies that the contractor must correct before the owner accepts the system.

## Occupancy and Closeout

**Occupancy and closeout** is the final phase, in which the building is handed to the owner and the project is administratively completed. The sequence has several steps. When the work is sufficiently complete for the owner to use, the architect certifies *substantial completion*, and the team prepares a *punch list*, a list of minor items still to be finished or corrected. The building official conducts final inspections, and if the building complies with the code, issues a *certificate of occupancy*, which is the legal permission to use the building.

The contractor also delivers the records the owner needs to operate the building: operation and maintenance manuals, warranties, equipment training, and *record drawings* that show the building as actually built, including changes made along the way. Warranties commonly cover at least one year for the contractor's work. When the punch list is complete and the records have been delivered, the owner makes final payment and releases the retainage. The completed building then enters its long service life, the subject of the final chapter of this book.

## Key Takeaways

- Design is iterative and convergent: each phase repeats the define, generate, evaluate, and select cycle at a finer level of detail, and each decision constrains the next.
- A project moves through programming, schematic design, design development, construction documents, bidding and procurement, construction, commissioning, and occupancy and closeout, with owner approval at each gate.
- Changes are cheapest in the early phases, when they are lines on paper, and cost the most after the work has been built.
- The architect leads design and observes construction. The general contractor holds the contract to build and controls means and methods. Subcontractors perform specialty work under the general contractor.
- Delivery methods differ in whom the owner contracts with, when the builder joins, and how the price is set: design-bid-build, design-build, construction manager at risk, and integrated project delivery.
- Submittals, requests for information, and change orders are the written channels that carry information and changes during construction. Work should not begin on a change until it is authorized in writing.
- The critical path is the longest chain of dependent activities, and any delay on it delays the project. Activities off the path have float until a delay uses it up.
- Commissioning tests that the systems work as intended, and closeout transfers the building, its records, and its warranties to the owner.
