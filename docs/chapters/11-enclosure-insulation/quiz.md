# Quiz: Enclosure Control Layers and Insulation

Test your understanding of the concepts in this chapter with these review questions, one for each of the 20 concepts covered.

#### 1. In the Riverbend example, windows are 5 percent of the enclosure area but account for 36 percent of the conductive heat loss. What does this show?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Windows have the same U-value as walls, but they face the coldest direction of the building
2. The roof loses less heat than windows because it has a smaller temperature difference across it
3. Windows contribute to heat loss only through air leakage, which the table includes as conduction
4. A window loses about eight times as much heat per square foot as the wall, so window area matters
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Conductive loss equals U × A × ΔT. At a U-value of 0.30 versus 0.036 and the same 80°F design-day temperature difference, a window loses about 24 BTU/h per ft² against 2.9 for the wall, a ratio near 8 to 1. The roof is still the biggest single item because of its area, and the table does not include air leakage.

#### 2. In a cold-climate wall, which sequence of layers is correct from the outside in?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Interior finish, vapor control layer, cavity insulation, sheathing and air control layer, exterior insulation, water control layer, cladding
2. Cladding and drainage space, water control layer, exterior insulation, sheathing and air layer, cavity insulation, vapor layer, interior finish
3. Cladding, vapor control layer, exterior insulation, cavity insulation, sheathing and air control layer, water control layer, interior finish
4. Cladding, cavity insulation, sheathing and air control layer, water control layer, vapor control layer, interior finish
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Control layers must be continuous, in the right order for the climate, and may be served by one material doing more than one job. The water layer sits directly behind the cladding, and the vapor control layer sits near the warm interior face, where the wall is warm in winter. The first option reverses the order, and the others misplace the water or vapor layers.

    **See:** [Control Layers](index.md#control-layers)

#### 3. Why do cold-climate energy codes commonly pair cavity insulation with continuous insulation?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Cavity insulation alone loses much of its value to the framing, which acts as a thermal bridge
2. Cavity insulation cannot be installed in 2×6 walls, so exterior insulation is required
3. Continuous insulation is the only insulation that can act as a water control layer
4. Cavity insulation and continuous insulation must always be the same material to meet the code
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A wall assembly is the complete set of layers performing all four control functions along with structure and fire resistance. Performance belongs to the whole, not to any one product. Studs interrupt cavity insulation, so a combined wall has a higher effective R than the cavity alone. Walls are grouped as cavity-insulated, exterior-insulated, combined, or mass walls.

#### 4. A wall has a total R of 20 along the cavity path and 10 along the stud path, with framing occupying 25 percent of the area. What is the effective R-value using the parallel path method?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 17.5
2. 20.0
3. 16.0
4. 30.0
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The parallel path method averages U-values by area: U = 0.75 × (1/20) + 0.25 × (1/10) = 0.0375 + 0.025 = 0.0625, so R_eff = 1/0.0625 = 16.0. Averaging the R-values directly gives 17.5, which overstates the wall. The cavity R of 20 ignores the framing, and 30 adds the paths in series, which is wrong because they sit side by side.

    **Concept Tested:** Thermal Control Layer

    **See:** [Thermal Control Layer](index.md#thermal-control-layer)

#### 5. How does nearly all insulation resist the conduction of heat?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It reflects thermal radiation back into the room using a dense, highly polished metallic surface layer
2. It holds a large volume of still air or low-conductivity gas in a lightweight structure of fibers or cells
3. It stores heat in its mass and releases it slowly after the temperature on the other side drops
4. It blocks vapor diffusion entirely so the wall cavity stays at the indoor temperature all winter
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Air conducts heat poorly but moves easily by convection, so the material's structure must trap it. That is why insulation loses effectiveness when compressed, soaked, or full of gaps. Insulation comes as batts and loose fill, spray-applied foam, and rigid boards, and the R-value of a layer equals its R per inch times its thickness.

    **Concept Tested:** Insulation

    **See:** [Insulation](index.md#insulation)

#### 6. A crew applies weather-resistive barrier courses from the top of the wall down, tucking each upper course behind the lower one. What is the result?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Water runs over the laps and continues down to the flashing, so the wall performs as designed
2. Water is trapped by the laps and evaporates through the siding without reaching the sheathing
3. Water runs behind the lower course onto the sheathing, turning the drainage plane into a funnel
4. Water is blocked completely because the double layer at each lap is thicker than a single layer
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The shingle principle laps every layer over the one below so water always flows over the top of the next piece, never behind it. A flipped lap uses identical materials and looks fine from the street, yet it directs water onto the sheathing. This is why inspectors check the laps before the cladding covers them.

    **Concept Tested:** Water Control Layer

#### 7. An enclosure leaks 500 cfm of air on a design day when the temperature difference is 60°F. Using Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT, how much heat is lost to leakage?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 32,400 BTU/h
2. 30,000 BTU/h
3. 540 BTU/h
4. 3,240 BTU/h
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Substituting gives 1.08 × 500 × 60 = 32,400 BTU/h. The constant 1.08 combines the density and specific heat of air and the 60 minutes in an hour. Omitting it gives 30,000, multiplying only the constant and the airflow gives 540, and 3,240 is a decimal slip. Leakage can exceed conduction through the whole enclosure, and it carries moisture into walls.

    **Concept Tested:** Air Control Layer

    **See:** [Air Control Layer](index.md#air-control-layer)

#### 8. Why is wall sheathing the surface most likely to become wet in a cold climate?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It sits on the warm side of the cavity insulation, where vapor condenses on its inner face
2. It is made of gypsum, which absorbs moisture from the humid indoor air and holds it for months
3. It is fastened with nails that conduct water through the cavity and onto the sheathing surface
4. It sits on the cold side of the cavity insulation, where leaking warm humid air meets a cold surface
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Sheathing is a rigid panel, often 7/16 in OSB, that serves as a structural skin, a substrate, a taped air control layer, and cavity closure. Wood-based sheathing tolerates occasional wetting but decays if it stays wet. Two defenses are used together: air and vapor control layers keep moisture from arriving, and continuous exterior insulation keeps the sheathing warmer so it dries.

    **Concept Tested:** Wall Sheathing

    **See:** [Wall Sheathing](index.md#wall-sheathing)

#### 9. What property must a weather-resistive barrier have in addition to stopping liquid water?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It must be vapor permeable so that a wall that gets wet can dry outward
2. It must be a Class I vapor retarder so that no moisture reaches the sheathing
3. It must be rigid so that it can resist racking from wind and earthquakes
4. It must be thick enough to serve as the thermal control layer of the wall
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The WRB is the sheet or coating over the sheathing or exterior insulation that forms the water control layer behind the cladding. It must pass vapor, often called breathable. Codes commonly require laps of about 2 in horizontally and 6 in vertically, with flashing integrated at windows and doors. Types include building paper, housewrap, fluid-applied membranes, and self-adhered membranes.

    **Concept Tested:** Weather-Resistive Barrier

#### 10. Many materials meet the code's air leakage limit, yet real buildings still leak. What explains this?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Air barrier materials lose their strength over time, so every material eventually fails the code limit
2. Air barriers work only when they are installed on the warm side of the wall, never on the cold side
3. An air barrier system is only as good as its seams and transitions, so the joints decide continuity
4. Air passes more easily through solid sheets than through cracks, so material choice does not matter
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. An air barrier is a material that resists air passage, and the air barrier system joins such materials at their seams to form the air control layer. Every option in the chapter, such as taped OSB, sealed gypsum board, and lapped membranes, depends on its joints. Quality control on every seam, with surfaces clean and dry, matters as much as the material, and cracks leak far more than solid sheets.

    **Concept Tested:** Air Barrier

    **See:** [Air Barrier](index.md#air-barrier)

#### 11. In the Riverbend example, 2 in of XPS (R-10) raised the effective R-value from 16.6 to 27.7, a gain of more than 10. What explains this?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The foam compresses the cavity insulation, so the cavity path gains extra resistance from the squeeze
2. The foam adds more R per inch than the studs lose, so the total is calculated by multiplying the two
3. The foam replaces the studs in the load path, so the stud path is no longer part of the average at all
4. The foam covers the studs as well as the cavities, so its R adds in full to every path and removes the bridge
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Continuous insulation is uninterrupted by framing, so it adds its R to both the cavity path and the stud path. Cavity path R goes to 32.4 and stud path R to 19.3, and the area-weighted U falls to 0.0361. Extra cavity insulation would have done nothing for the studs. The foam also keeps the sheathing about 23°F warmer, though air and vapor control layers are still needed.

    **Concept Tested:** Continuous Insulation

    **See:** [Continuous Insulation](index.md#continuous-insulation)

#### 12. Why is it a mistake to put an impermeable vapor barrier on both sides of a Minnesota wall?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The two layers would double the rate at which vapor diffuses into the cavity
2. Any moisture that enters becomes trapped because the wall has no path to dry
3. The two layers would cancel each other so no vapor could reach the sheathing
4. The wall would lose its air control layer because impermeable layers cannot be sealed
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. In winter, indoor vapor pressure is higher than outdoor pressure, so vapor diffuses outward and the control layer belongs on the warm interior side. The wall must be able to dry as well as resist wetting, so designers control vapor where it enters and leave a path for drying on the other side. Two impermeable layers trap whatever moisture gets in.

    **Concept Tested:** Vapor Control Layer

    **See:** [Vapor Control Layer](index.md#vapor-control-layer)

#### 13. How many inches of XPS board at about R-5 per inch are needed to add R-15 of continuous insulation?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 3 in
2. 0.33 in
3. 10 in
4. 75 in
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The thickness needed equals the target R divided by the R per inch: 15 / 5 = 3 in. Dividing the other way gives 0.33, while 10 and 75 come from subtracting or multiplying. Rigid foam is EPS, XPS, or polyiso board that resists moisture and compression, and polyiso loses part of its R at low temperatures, which matters in Minnesota.

#### 14. How does waterproofing differ from dampproofing for a foundation wall?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Dampproofing is a continuous membrane that resists standing water, while waterproofing is only a thin asphalt coating
2. Dampproofing is applied on the inside of the wall, while waterproofing is applied on the outside face only
3. Dampproofing is a thin coating that resists soil moisture, while waterproofing is a membrane resisting hydrostatic pressure
4. Dampproofing is required only above grade, while waterproofing is required only at the footing and slab edge
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Dampproofing is an asphalt-based coating, commonly required on basement walls, that resists moisture in soil but not standing water. Waterproofing is a self-adhered or fluid-applied membrane typically required where the water table is high. It runs from the footing to above finished grade and is protected from backfill by a drainage board, and it works with the perimeter drain.

#### 15. How does a frost-protected foundation allow a footing to sit above the normal frost depth?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A layer of gravel beneath the footing drains away the water that would otherwise freeze under the footing in winter
2. Heat escaping from the building warms the soil, and rigid foam with a horizontal wing blocks cold from the footing
3. A thick concrete footing stores enough heat during the day to prevent freezing of the soil during the winter
4. A waterproofing membrane stops frost from penetrating the soil under the footing from the wall side and below
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The principle is that heat from a heated building keeps the soil beneath it warm, and insulation placed against the wall and extended outward prevents cold from reaching under the footing from the sides. Footings can be as shallow as 12 to 16 in. It is common for slab-on-grade houses and garages, but not suited to unheated buildings or poorly drained soil.

    **Concept Tested:** Frost-Protected Foundations

    **See:** [Frost-Protected Foundations](index.md#frost-protected-foundations)

#### 16. Which classification and permeance fit a sheet of polyethylene used as a vapor retarder?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Class II, with a permeance above 0.1 perm up to 1 perm
2. Class III, with a permeance above 1 perm up to 10 perms
3. Class IV, with a permeance above 10 perms
4. Class I, with a permeance of 0.1 perm or less
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Codes sort vapor retarders into three classes by permeance. Class I, 0.1 perm or less, includes polyethylene sheet and sheet metal. Class II, up to 1.0 perm, includes kraft paper facing, and Class III, up to 10 perms, includes latex paint on gypsum board. A smart retarder changes permeance with humidity, closing in winter and opening in summer so the wall can dry.

    **Concept Tested:** Vapor Retarder

    **See:** [Vapor Retarder](index.md#vapor-retarder)

#### 17. A crew folds fiberglass batts over wiring and compresses them to fit shallow cavities, and plans no separate air barrier. What problem does this create?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Folding raises the R-value, but the batts lose their fire resistance
2. Compression makes the batts waterproof, but the wall cannot dry
3. The batts become an air barrier, but they lose their ability to hold sound
4. Compression reduces the R-value, and the batts do not stop air movement
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Fiberglass is inexpensive and easy to install, but its performance depends on installation quality: fill the cavity fully, do not compress or fold the batt, and cut it to fit around wiring and boxes. It does not stop air movement, so it needs a separate air control layer. Insulation relies on trapped still air, so crushing it reduces its R-value.

#### 18. Why does mineral wool board suit exterior insulation where drying to the outside is desirable?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It repels liquid water but lets vapor pass, so a wall can dry outward, and it is noncombustible
2. It acts as a Class I vapor retarder, so no vapor can reach the sheathing from inside the building
3. It has the highest R per inch of any insulation, so much thinner layers are needed for the same R
4. It is lightweight and inexpensive, so it is the cheapest choice for continuous insulation
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Mineral wool is made from spun basalt rock or industrial slag. It is noncombustible, commonly used in fire-rated assemblies, and its density makes it a good sound absorber. Its vapor permeability gives it an advantage over foam plastics in exterior applications. It is heavier and more expensive than fiberglass, with R per inch similar to or slightly higher.

#### 19. Why is cellulose often dense-packed in closed wall cavities?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Dense-packing converts cellulose into an air barrier, so no other air control layer is needed anywhere
2. Dense-packing makes cellulose waterproof, so it can be installed on the cold side of the sheathing
3. Dense-packing fills voids around wiring and framing better than batts and slows air movement in the cavity
4. Dense-packing eliminates settling, so the labeled R-value is guaranteed without any added depth in the attic
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Cellulose is made mostly from recycled newsprint treated with borate compounds that resist fire, insects, and mold. Blown at a higher density it fills irregular spaces that batts cannot, though it is not a substitute for an air barrier. It absorbs and releases moisture, which buffers short wetting events, but it must be kept dry, and loose-fill depth settles over time.

#### 20. Why might closed-cell spray foam be chosen for a rim joist in a Minnesota house?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is vapor-permeable and soft, so it lets the rim joist dry in both directions
2. It seals air leaks and insulates in one step, with the highest R per inch of the foams
3. It is the lowest cost per R of any insulation, so large areas can be covered economically
4. It is noncombustible and so needs no thermal barrier where it is exposed
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Both types of spray foam seal air leaks as they cure, so one application can supply the thermal and air control layers. Closed-cell foam is denser, has about R-6 to R-7 per inch, stiffens the surface, and in a few inches acts as a Class II vapor retarder. Open-cell is soft, about R-3.6 per inch, and vapor-permeable. Closed-cell costs much more per R and must be installed by trained crews.

    **Concept Tested:** Spray Foam Insulation

    **See:** [Spray Foam Insulation](index.md#spray-foam-insulation)
