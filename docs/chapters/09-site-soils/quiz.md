# Quiz: Site Work, Soils, and Groundwater

Test your understanding of the concepts in this chapter with these review questions, one for each of the 11 concepts covered.

---

#### 1. A lot is 200 ft wide and 150 ft deep. Zoning requires setbacks of 10 ft on each side, 25 ft at the front, and 15 ft at the rear. What is the area of the buildable envelope?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 30,000 ft²
2. 19,800 ft²
3. 27,000 ft²
4. 22,500 ft²
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The envelope's width is 200 − 10 − 10 = 180 ft, and its depth is 150 − 25 − 15 = 110 ft, so the area is 180 × 110 = 19,800 ft². The lot area (30,000) ignores all setbacks, 27,000 ignores the front and rear setbacks, and 22,500 ignores the rear setback. Easements, wetlands, or steep slopes would shrink the usable area further.

    **Concept Tested:** Site Analysis

    **See:** [Site Analysis](index.md#site-analysis)

#### 2. A sieve analysis finds 5 percent gravel, 25 percent sand, and 70 percent fines of low plasticity. Which classification and behavior fit best?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A coarse-grained silty sand with good drainage, high strength, and low frost susceptibility
2. A well-graded gravel with excellent bearing capacity and excellent drainage
3. A fine-grained, silt-like soil with poor drainage and very high frost susceptibility
4. An organic soil that is very strong when saturated and easy to compact in lifts
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Fines are particles smaller than 0.075 mm that pass the No. 200 sieve. When more than half of a sample passes it, the soil is fine-grained. With low-plasticity fines, the soil behaves like a silt: poor drainage, low to moderate strength, strength lost when saturated, and very high frost susceptibility. The coarse-grained options contradict the 70 percent fines, and organic soil is weak and compressible.

    **Concept Tested:** Soil Types

    **See:** [Soil Types](index.md#soil-types)

#### 3. A column carries 60 kips and the soil's allowable bearing capacity is 2,000 psf. What footing area is required?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 30 ft²
2. 3 ft²
3. 60 ft²
4. 120 ft²
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Required area equals load divided by allowable bearing capacity: 60,000 lb / 2,000 psf = 30 ft², about 5.5 ft square. The value 3 ft² comes from using kips against psf with the wrong decimal, and 60 and 120 ft² use capacities of 1,000 and 500 psf. Weaker soil demands a larger footing, and a real design also adds the footing's own weight.

    **Concept Tested:** Soil Bearing Capacity

    **See:** [Soil Bearing Capacity](index.md#soil-bearing-capacity)

#### 4. A crew strips topsoil from the whole building pad before installing any silt fence. What problem does this sequence invite?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Compaction of the topsoil by the equipment, which makes the stockpile unusable for later landscaping
2. Loss of the utility markings, which are removed along with the topsoil layer during stripping
3. Settlement of the future footings, which would rest on the thin layer of topsoil left behind
4. Bare soil with no sediment control, which can wash off the site in the first rain and draw a stop-work order
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Preparation is as much about protecting the surroundings as changing the land. The recommended order is to locate utilities, survey, install erosion and sediment controls, protect what stays, then clear, grub, and strip. Stripping first exposes bare soil with nothing to stop runoff, which is the most common sequencing mistake. Topsoil is stripped because it is unsuitable for supporting a building.

    **Concept Tested:** Site Preparation

    **See:** [Site Preparation](index.md#site-preparation)

#### 5. An excavation removes 300 yd³ of soil measured in place (bank volume). With a swell factor of 25 percent, what loose volume must the trucks haul?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 240 yd³
2. 325 yd³
3. 375 yd³
4. 300 yd³
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Once dug, soil is loosened and occupies more space. The loose volume is the bank volume times (1 + swell factor): 300 × 1.25 = 375 yd³. Dividing by 1.25 gives 240 yd³, adding 25 yd³ gives 325, and 300 ignores swell entirely. Quantities should be tracked separately as bank, loose, and compacted volumes, because compacted fill shrinks again.

    **Concept Tested:** Excavation

    **See:** [Excavation](index.md#excavation)

#### 6. Why should below-grade space be designed for the seasonal high water level and not the water level observed on the day of the borings?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The water table rises with spring snowmelt and rain, and a dry-season boring can read too low
2. The water table stays constant all year, so any boring date gives a reliable value for design
3. Groundwater only exerts pressure in spring, so the other seasons are not important for design
4. Saturated soil is stronger than dry soil, so the high level is the safest assumption for bearing
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Groundwater fills the spaces between soil particles below the water table, and hydrostatic pressure increases by about 62.4 psf per foot of depth. A slab 6 ft below the water table sees about 374 psf of uplift, which can crack and lift it. Saturated soil is usually weaker, not stronger, and the water table varies with season.

    **Concept Tested:** Groundwater

    **See:** [Groundwater](index.md#groundwater)

#### 7. An entry sits at elevation 100.00 ft and the parking lot edge, 50 ft away, is at 98.50 ft. What is the walk's slope, and how does it compare with the 5 percent threshold between a walk and a ramp?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 3 percent, which is below 5 percent, so it must be treated as a ramp
2. 3 percent, which is below 5 percent, so it remains a regular walking route
3. 33 percent, which is above 5 percent, so it must be treated as a ramp
4. 0.03 percent, which is below 5 percent, so it needs no slope at all
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The drop is 1.5 ft over 50 ft, or 0.03, which is 3 percent. Accessibility standards generally treat a walking surface steeper than 1:20 (5 percent) as a ramp with additional requirements, so this walk is a regular route. The value 33 percent inverts the ratio. Codes also commonly require ground to fall at least 6 in within the first 10 ft away from the foundation.

    **Concept Tested:** Grading

    **See:** [Grading](index.md#grading)

#### 8. What is the practical purpose of the code's frost depth requirement for exterior footings?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It places the bottom of footings at the depth where the water table is found in spring
2. It places the bottom of footings at the depth where the soil changes from silt to granular
3. It places the bottom of footings above frozen soil so they can be inspected in winter
4. It places the bottom of footings below the depth to which ground freezes in a severe winter
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Frost depth is the depth to which the ground freezes, and the code sets a minimum depth for the bottoms of footings so they sit below frozen soil. In the Twin Cities area it is commonly 42 inches and greater farther north, but the local building official establishes the project value. It records the deepest freezing expected in a severe winter, not the frost line seen on a single cold morning.

    **Concept Tested:** Frost Depth

    **See:** [Frost Depth](index.md#frost-depth)

#### 9. What does a French drain do?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It uses perforated pipe in gravel to intercept groundwater
2. It uses a shallow vegetated channel to carry surface water away from pavement
3. It uses an inlet in the pavement to collect surface runoff
4. It uses a basin to hold stormwater and release it slowly
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Subsurface drainage uses perforated pipe in gravel to intercept groundwater. Surface drainage uses grading, swales (shallow vegetated channels), catch basins (pavement inlets), and storm sewers. Municipalities and watershed districts commonly require basins that hold back stormwater and release it slowly so downstream flooding does not increase.

    **Concept Tested:** Site Drainage

#### 10. Why does the geotechnical investigation order borings early, during schematic design, rather than after the foundation is drawn?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Borings are cheaper in schematic design because the soil is easier to sample before drawings begin
2. The code requires foundation plans to be drawn before the soil's allowable bearing capacity is known
3. The standard penetration test cannot be performed once the building footprint is staked
4. A report that arrives first can guide where the building goes, while a late report can force a redesign
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Soil testing combines field exploration and laboratory analysis, directed by a geotechnical engineer, and replaces assumptions with data on grain size, Atterberg limits, moisture, and compaction. The report recommends allowable bearing capacity and records frost and groundwater observations. Borings are a small fraction of the project budget and protect against the largest foundation unknowns.

    **Concept Tested:** Soil Testing

    **See:** [Soil Testing](index.md#soil-testing)

#### 11. A slab edge rises each winter over wet silt. Which measure removes one of the three conditions needed for frost heave?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Add a second layer of concrete to increase the slab's weight
2. Replace the silt beneath the slab edge with clean gravel
3. Seal the slab surface so snow cannot melt onto it
4. Increase the slab thickness so the freezing front cannot reach it
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Frost heave requires frost-susceptible soil, a supply of water, and freezing temperatures together. The main cause is the growth of ice lenses as freezing soil draws water upward by capillary action. Silt is the worst offender. Removing any one condition stops heave, so options include footings below frost depth, replacing silty soil with clean gravel, or draining the water away.

    **Concept Tested:** Frost Heave

    **See:** [Frost Heave](index.md#frost-heave)
